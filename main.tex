\begin{abstract}
This thesis investigates how a real-time regime signal interacts with cross-sectional momentum and finds that momentum is not a single stable anomaly but a regime-dependent family of horizon-specific effects: in calm markets, intermediate-horizon continuation dominates the cross-section; in the most acute panic months, beaten-down stocks across all horizons become more likely to outperform, while in milder panic months the cross-section retains continuation structure. A Bayesian Hidden Markov Model produces a filtered probability of market panic, which is combined with momentum at 12 lookback horizons in machine learning models to rank stocks. The regime signal functions as a context variable -- it carries no direct linear return-predictive content but conditions how the model jointly uses the full momentum term structure in a given market state. Out-of-sample on 2011--2025 U.S. equities, only a nonlinear model (XGBoost) exploits this structure (Sharpe 1.11, six-factor alpha 24.1\%, $t = 4.81$); both a classification-based and a regression-based linear model collapse, establishing that the interaction is inherently nonlinear. Cross-sectional z-score analysis confirms this regime-dependent structure in the raw data. The regime signal is essential (removing it reduces the Sharpe by more than 60\%) and most valuable during stress, with the long-short spread widening from 1.3\% to 3.1\% per month. These findings are consistent with the interpretation that momentum's well-documented fragility arises partly from applying unconditional models to what is fundamentally a regime-dependent phenomenon.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Management Summary}

Cross-sectional momentum, the tendency for recent stock-market winners to keep winning and losers to keep losing, is one of the most profitable anomalies in finance and one of the most fragile. Momentum portfolios suffered losses exceeding 50\% during the 2009 market rebound and crash repeatedly around regime transitions. This thesis tests whether momentum's predictive structure is regime-dependent rather than unconditional, and whether exploiting that dependence requires nonlinear modelling.

\paragraph{Introduction (Section~\ref{sec:introduction}).} The thesis introduces a real-time regime signal derived from a Bayesian Hidden Markov Model applied to four macrofinancial stress indicators. The signal enters a cross-sectional stock-selection model as a context variable that conditions how the model jointly uses the full momentum term structure, not as a direct return predictor. Three research questions guide the analysis. (i) Does the regime-momentum interaction require nonlinear modelling, and if so, why does a linear model fail on the same features? (ii) How does the cross-sectional structure of momentum change between calm and panic regimes? (iii) How robust is the interaction to alternative signal specifications, risk preferences, and prolonged stress?

\paragraph{Literature (Section~\ref{sec:literature}).} The thesis sits at the intersection of three literatures. Standard cross-sectional momentum at intermediate horizons \citep{JegadeeshTitman1993, NovyMarx2012}, with behavioural drivers in gradual information diffusion \citep{HongStein1999}; momentum crashes and exposure-based risk management, where \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} and \citet{BarrosoSantaClara2015} reduce gross exposure during stress; and regime-conditional asset pricing, where \citet{Cooper2004} and \citet{GHM2023} use binary or weight-blending regime treatments. The thesis differs from each strand by selecting stocks by absolute momentum level rather than rank, by re-ranking instead of re-scaling exposure when crash conditions arise, and by using a probabilistic regime signal whose marginal value scales with cross-sectional ambiguity.

\paragraph{Methodology (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).} A two-stage framework. Stage one estimates a Bayesian two-state Hidden Markov Model via Gibbs sampling on four monthly stress indicators (drawdown, return dispersion, relative market participation, and the BAA-AAA credit spread), producing a filtered probability of market panic. Stage two combines this regime signal with twelve momentum horizons in models of increasing complexity (a deterministic formula, logistic regression, Ridge regression, and XGBoost), evaluated in long-short construction with NYSE breakpoints and value weighting.

\paragraph{Data (Section~\ref{sec:data}).} The sample is U.S. equities (CRSP, 1990--2025), split into a 1990--2010 training period and a 2011--2025 out-of-sample test period (167 months). Delisting returns follow \citet{Shumway2001}. The HMM feature set is chosen via a three-pass selection pipeline (quality screen, portfolio ranking, and seed-stability validation). International replication uses Compustat Global for the United Kingdom and Japan, with the HMM feature set re-selected per region.

\paragraph{Results (Section~\ref{sec:results}).} XGBoost achieves an out-of-sample Sharpe of $1.11$ and a six-factor alpha of $24.1\%$ ($t = 4.81$); the regime-conditional advantage is concentrated in panic, where XGB earns $1.53$ Sharpe against $0.84$ in calm while every benchmark either collapses or stays near zero. Removing the regime signal cuts XGB's Sharpe by more than $60\%$, establishing that the signal is essential. Both linear baselines on identical features collapse, establishing that the regime-momentum interaction is inherently nonlinear. Cluster analysis of the long leg's z-curve profiles recovers four recurring selection modes that trace a single market cycle (classical winner selection in calm, hedged selection in transition, dispersed-recovery loser selection in post-panic months, and term-structure-wide reversal in active panic). Cluster-level CAPM betas confirm that XGB avoids the leg-beta inversion that drives traditional momentum's crashes. International replication produces Sharpes of $0.84$ in the United Kingdom and $0.50$ in Japan with locally fitted HMMs.

\paragraph{Conclusion (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).} The thesis makes two contributions to the momentum literature. First, the regime signal acts as a routing variable that flips the direction of momentum selection in the panic regime, producing two reversal modes with no direct analogue in the classical reversal literature, namely a dispersed-recovery state where the long leg sits below the cross-section average while the cross-section itself is up, and a term-structure-wide reversal at the panic peak. Second, the model selects by momentum level rather than rank, which reframes momentum's crash management as a re-ranking decision rather than an exposure-scaling one. Limitations include the 167-month test sample, specification search on the HMM feature set, and reversal-mode vulnerability to prolonged fundamental deterioration. Future work should extend the framework to longer historical windows that include depression-style downturns and to additional asset classes.
