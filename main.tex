\section*{Management Summary}

Cross-sectional momentum, the tendency for recent stock-market winners to keep winning and losers to keep losing, is one of the most profitable anomalies in finance and one of the most fragile. Momentum portfolios suffered losses exceeding 50\% during the 2009 market rebound and crash repeatedly around regime transitions. This thesis examines what form cross-sectional momentum's regime-dependence takes, and how a nonlinear model captures and exploits that form. The aim is to understand the mechanism, not to propose a strategy that outperforms the market on aggregate.

\paragraph{Introduction (Section~\ref{sec:introduction})} The introduction sets out the empirical setting (U.S. equities 1990--2025, with UK and Japan replication) and defines cross-sectional momentum, then motivates the study through the anomaly's fragility around regime transitions and identifies the gap in prior crash-management work, which adjusts only portfolio exposure rather than the underlying selection rule. It poses the research question, how the cross-section of momentum reorganises across regimes and what mechanism drives that change, and outlines an approach that combines a Bayesian HMM regime signal with the momentum term structure inside a nonlinear cross-sectional model, whose selections are then opened up to characterise the mechanism. The section closes with the headline findings, the two contributions (the methodological combination and the mechanism it reveals), and an outline of the remaining sections.

\paragraph{Literature (Section~\ref{sec:literature})} The thesis sits at the intersection of four literatures. Standard cross-sectional momentum at intermediate horizons \citep{JegadeeshTitman1993, NovyMarx2012}, with behavioural drivers in gradual information diffusion \citep{HongStein1999}; momentum crashes and exposure-based risk management, where \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} and \citet{BarrosoSantaClara2015} reduce gross exposure during stress; regime-conditional asset pricing, where \citet{Cooper2004} and \citet{GHM2023} use binary or weight-blending regime treatments; and machine learning in cross-sectional return prediction \citep{Gu2020}. The thesis investigates how these strands interact, characterising the mechanism by which the cross-sectional structure of momentum reorganises across regimes when a continuous probabilistic regime signal and the full momentum term structure are jointly fed to a nonlinear cross-sectional model.

\paragraph{Methodology (Section~\ref{sec:methodology})} A two-stage framework. Stage one estimates a Bayesian two-state Hidden Markov Model via Gibbs sampling on four monthly stress indicators, producing a filtered probability of market panic. Stage two combines this regime signal with twelve momentum horizons in models of increasing complexity (a deterministic formula, a logistic regression, and an XGBoost ensemble), evaluated in long-short construction with NYSE breakpoints and value weighting.

\paragraph{Data (Section~\ref{sec:data})} The sample is U.S. equities (CRSP, 1990--2025), split into a 1990--2010 training period and a 2011--2025 out-of-sample test period (167 months), with delisting returns incorporated to prevent survivorship bias. The HMM feature set is chosen via a four-pass selection pipeline (quality screen, portfolio ranking, definitive validation at higher seed counts, and seed-stability check). International replication uses Compustat Global for the United Kingdom and Japan, with the HMM feature set re-selected per region.

\paragraph{Results (Section~\ref{sec:results})} The headline finding is that the cross-section of momentum reorganises systematically across regimes, and that capturing this reorganisation requires both a probabilistic regime signal and a nonlinear model. XGBoost on the regime signal and the full momentum term structure delivers a six-factor alpha of $24.1\%$ ($t = 4.81$) and an out-of-sample Sharpe of $1.11$, with the advantage concentrated in panic ($1.53$ versus $0.84$ in calm), while the linear baseline on identical features collapses and removing the regime signal alone cuts the Sharpe by more than $60\%$. The novel contribution lies in opening up the model's selections: cluster analysis of the long leg's cross-sectional z-curves recovers four recurring selection modes that trace a single market cycle (classical winner selection in calm, a hedged transition, dispersed-recovery loser selection post-panic, and term-structure-wide reversal in active panic), and cluster-level CAPM betas show that the leg-beta inversion that drives traditional momentum's crashes is absent in every regime, distinguishing this mechanism from the exposure-scaling responses in the prior literature. International replication produces Sharpes of $0.84$ in the United Kingdom and $0.50$ in Japan with locally fitted HMMs.

\paragraph{Conclusion (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion})} The central conclusion is that cross-sectional momentum's regime-dependent structure is real and characterisable, and that the regime signal acts as a \emph{context variable}: it carries near-zero direct return-predictive content, yet it conditions how a nonlinear cross-sectional model uses the full momentum term structure in a given market state. The thesis makes two contributions. First, the methodological combination is itself novel: prior regime-conditional momentum work pairs simple regime treatments (binary indicators, volatility thresholds, fast-slow blends) with linear or rank-based rules, whereas this thesis pairs a continuous probabilistic HMM regime filter built from multiple stress indicators with a nonlinear cross-sectional model (XGBoost), and the combination is what enables the empirical mechanism. Second, opening up that mechanism reveals two interlocking facets: structurally, XGB ranks stocks on predicted forward return rather than per-horizon momentum rank, allowing the long leg to hold stocks not top-ranked at any individual horizon, a composition that no rank-based momentum strategy can produce by construction; behaviourally, this compositional flexibility enables the inversion of selection direction in panic while keeping the long-short portfolio fully invested, in contrast to the exposure-scaling responses of \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} and \citet{BarrosoSantaClara2015}, with cluster-level CAPM betas confirming that the leg-beta inversion that drives traditional momentum's crashes is absent in every regime. The primary limitation is reversal-failure vulnerability in prolonged fundamental deterioration, where the beaten-down stocks selected during panic do not recover; the most direct future extension is a hybrid that layers a D\&M-style exposure-scaling overlay on top of XGB's stock-selection, addressing this vulnerability without sacrificing XGB's selection advantage in shorter panics.
