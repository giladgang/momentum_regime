\section{Literature Review}\label{sec:literature}

This review covers four strands of literature that frame the question pursued in this thesis: the cross-sectional momentum anomaly and its horizon structure, momentum crashes and exposure-based risk management, regime models and their use in regime-conditional asset pricing, and the application of machine learning to cross-sectional return prediction. Each strand has identified part of the picture; none has, on its own, posed the question of how the full term structure of cross-sectional momentum reorganises across regimes.

\subsection{The Momentum Anomaly and Its Horizon Structure}\label{sec:lit_momentum}

\citet{JegadeeshTitman1993} established cross-sectional momentum as a pervasive feature of equity markets: portfolios that buy past winners and sell past losers earn significant returns over 3-to-12-month holding periods. The original strategy ranks stocks by cumulative returns over the past twelve months (skipping the most recent month to avoid microstructure effects) and has been replicated across international markets, asset classes, and sample periods \citep{JegadeeshTitman2001, Asness2013}.

The economic mechanism behind momentum remains debated. Behavioural explanations emphasise investor underreaction to information \citep{Barberis1998, HongStein1999}, while risk-based explanations argue that momentum profits compensate for time-varying systematic risk \citep{FamaFrench2015}. This thesis does not take a position on the underlying mechanism but exploits the empirical regularity that momentum's cross-sectional structure varies with market conditions.

A defining feature of momentum is its sensitivity to the lookback horizon. Short lookbacks of one to three months capture short-term reversal effects \citep{Jegadeesh1990, JegadeeshTitman1993}, intermediate horizons of six to twelve months capture continuation \citep{NovyMarx2012}, and lookbacks beyond twelve months reflect long-term mean reversion \citep{DeBondtThaler1985}. \citet{LeeSwaminathan2000} document a momentum lifecycle in which the same signal evolves from continuation to reversal as information ages. Together, these findings suggest that momentum is not a single signal but a family of horizon-specific patterns whose relative strength varies with market conditions, a hypothesis this thesis tests directly by exposing all twelve monthly horizons jointly to the cross-sectional model rather than committing to a single lookback ex ante.

\subsection{Momentum Crashes and Exposure-Based Solutions}\label{sec:lit_crashes}

Despite its long-run profitability, momentum is prone to severe crashes. \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} document that momentum portfolios suffered catastrophic losses during the 1932 and 2009 market rebounds, and show that these crashes are predictable: when the market has recently declined, past losers tend to be high-beta stocks that rally disproportionately during recoveries, while past winners are low-beta defensive stocks that lag. \citet{BarrosoSantaClara2015} propose a simple risk-management response: scaling momentum exposure inversely with the strategy's recent realised volatility, a constant-volatility overlay that substantially raises the Sharpe ratio. \citet{BianchiDePolisPetrella2022} extend this approach by constructing a crash indicator from the joint time variation of conditional volatility and skewness, again operating on the strategy's exposure rather than its composition.

The liquidity literature provides a theoretical basis for why momentum crashes reverse. Margin spirals \citep{BrunnermeierPedersen2009} and institutional flow pressure \citep{VayanosWoolley2013} create temporary price dislocations during stress that revert when selling pressure subsides, a mechanism central to this thesis's interpretation of the regime-momentum interaction.

What unites this strand is the choice of remedy: each approach scales \emph{how much} momentum to hold based on the strategy's own past returns or volatility. This thesis instead adapts \emph{which stocks} are held using a forward-looking regime signal derived from contemporaneous stress indicators, opening a stock-selection channel complementary to the exposure-scaling literature.

\subsection{Regime Models and Regime-Conditional Asset Pricing}\label{sec:lit_hmm}

Hidden Markov Models have a long history in financial econometrics, originating with \citet{Hamilton1989} for business-cycle analysis and extended to Bayesian estimation via Gibbs sampling by \citet{AlbertChib1993}, an approach this thesis adapts to a multivariate setting. A common finding in the regime-switching literature for equity markets is that returns are well described by a two-state model with a high-mean, low-volatility state and a low-mean, high-volatility state, where the bear state is less persistent but more volatile \citep{Ang2002, Guidolin2007}. Hamilton's original application fitted the regime model to GNP growth rather than returns themselves, a design choice this thesis follows by using macrofinancial stress indicators as observable inputs.

A separate strand asks whether asset-pricing anomalies are themselves regime-conditional. \citet{Cooper2004} establish that momentum profits depend on the prior market state, with strong continuation following up-markets and weaker or reversed performance following down-markets. More recent work integrates regime estimation directly into portfolio construction. \citet{ShuMulvey2024} use a sparse jump model to identify factor-specific bull and bear regimes and feed the resulting classifications into a Black-Litterman framework for dynamic factor allocation. The unifying feature of this strand is that the regime variable enters portfolio decisions through a hand-coded rule, threshold, or parametric weight: regimes drive switches between models, scalings of exposure, or blendings of signals, but are not themselves treated as features that a learner can interpret.

The most directly related work is \citet{GHM2023}, who classify each month into one of four market cycles (Bull, Correction, Bear, Rebound) from the trailing one-month and rolling twelve-month market returns. In each cycle a different blending parameter $a$ determines the weight on one-month versus twelve-month momentum: the composite signal is $a \cdot mom_1 + (1-a) \cdot mom_{12}$, where $a=0$ corresponds to pure twelve-month momentum and $a=1$ to pure one-month reversal. Their dynamic strategy selects the optimal blending parameters for each non-Bull state via grid search on the training sample. The framework here generalises that approach along three axes: from a deterministic four-state classification based on trailing returns to a continuous panic probability from a Bayesian HMM on real-time stress indicators; from two horizons to the full twelve-horizon term structure; and from a parametric linear blend to a learned nonlinear combination. Each of these moves is necessary, individually, to pose a question GHM cannot: whether the cross-sectional structure of momentum reorganises across regimes. Since GHM design their strategies for long-only construction, a direct long-short performance comparison is not appropriate; Section~\ref{sec:results} instead uses the GHM market-cycle variable as an alternative regime input within the XGBoost framework, providing a fair test of whether trailing-return-based regime definitions capture the same information as the HMM's stress indicators.

Across this strand, the regime variable is consistently used as a switch, threshold, or parametric weight rather than as a continuous learned feature. This thesis treats $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ instead as an input passed to a learner that decides how to use it.

\subsection{Machine Learning in Cross-Sectional Asset Pricing}\label{sec:lit_ml}

The use of machine learning methods for cross-sectional stock selection has grown rapidly. \citet{Gu2020} compare a wide range of methods on a kitchen-sink characteristic set including multiple momentum horizons, finding that tree-based models and neural networks substantially outperform linear models by capturing nonlinear interactions among firm characteristics. Gradient-boosted trees \citep{Chen2016} have proven particularly effective for tabular financial data because they handle missing values natively, capture nonlinear relationships and interactions without explicit specification, and are robust to irrelevant features. A central challenge in applying these methods to asset pricing is interpretability, addressed in this thesis through SHAP values \citep{Lundberg2017, Lundberg2020}, which decompose each prediction into feature-level contributions and admit an exact polynomial-time algorithm for tree ensembles.

The closest methodological precedent is \citet{BeckmeyerWiedemann2025}, who apply machine learning to learn data-driven weights across past daily returns for a single momentum signal, constructing a dynamically reweighted strategy that outperforms the conventional twelve-minus-one specification. Their finding establishes that data-driven weighting of past-return information carries content beyond fixed schemes. The framework here differs in two respects. First, the weighting is learned not over the daily history of one signal but over a vector of twelve monthly horizons. Second, and more importantly, the weighting is learned \emph{conditional on a regime context variable}, isolating the regime-conditioning role as the primary contribution. The unconditional weighting in \citet{BeckmeyerWiedemann2025} averages across calm and panic regimes; this thesis tests whether disaggregating that average reveals a structural reorganisation of the term structure between states.

Despite these advances, no published study examines how the full term structure of cross-sectional momentum predictability reorganises across regimes, or whether exploiting this reorganisation requires nonlinear modelling.

\subsection*{Position of This Thesis}

The four strands above each identify part of the picture. The momentum literature documents horizon structure but treats it unconditionally. The crash literature documents fragility but adapts only the exposure dimension. The regime-conditional asset-pricing literature conditions on market state but uses regimes as switches or parametric weights, not as learned features. The machine-learning literature offers flexible function classes but has applied them either to kitchen-sink predictor zoos or to unconditional momentum weighting, not to the specific question of regime-conditional term-structure shape. This thesis combines a probabilistic regime estimate with a multi-horizon momentum vector inside a tree ensemble and uses SHAP and z-score evidence to ask how the cross-section reorganises between calm and panic. The regime signal that emerges is best read as a non-priced state variable, conditioning the joint use of momentum horizons rather than carrying its own risk premium, an interpretation supported by the factor-alpha tests reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}.
