\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This thesis developed a two-stage framework for regime-conditioned cross-sectional momentum investing. A Bayesian two-state Hidden Markov Model, estimated via Gibbs sampling on four macrofinancial stress indicators, produces a real-time filtered probability of market panic. Three portfolio construction methods of increasing complexity combine this regime signal with momentum at multiple horizons and fundamental characteristics to rank stocks each month.

As stated at the outset, the primary goal of this thesis is not to maximise portfolio returns but to uncover how the regime signal interacts with cross-sectional momentum. Out-of-sample evaluation over 167 months (2011--2025) provides evidence for four main findings. First, both machine learning methods deliver meaningfully higher risk-adjusted returns than unconditional momentum strategies and the market-state approach of \citet{GHM2023}. Second, fundamental quality screening appears to be the primary driver of risk-adjusted improvement, while the regime signal contributes by enabling higher absolute returns through regime-adaptive stock selection rather than by improving the Sharpe ratio. Third, the evidence supports the thesis's central claim: the regime signal is not a direct return predictor; it is a context variable that governs which momentum horizons are informative. This interaction is captured by XGBoost through tree splits but is invisible to the linear logistic regression. Fourth, a risk-aversion analysis suggests that the regime signal is most valuable when the investor is willing to bear risk: its relative importance drops from nearly 30\% of total feature importance under pure return maximisation to approximately 6\% under high risk aversion, consistent with the signal's value lying in enabling aggressive, regime-conditional stock selection. Together, these findings provide evidence that cross-sectional momentum is a family of horizon-specific effects whose composition rotates with market conditions, and that the regime signal is the variable governing this rotation.

These results offer a different perspective from the view, articulated by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} and \citet{BarrosoSantaClara2015}, that the primary benefit of regime awareness for momentum strategies is crash avoidance. \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} demonstrate that momentum crashes are predictable from past market declines and propose scaling exposure accordingly, while \citet{BarrosoSantaClara2015} use the strategy's own realised volatility to the same end. Both approaches target the short, violent drawdowns in long-short momentum portfolios. In the long-only, cross-sectional framework of this thesis, however, panic-period Sharpe ratios are already high for all strategies (1.15--1.45), because many months the HMM labels as ``panic'' are in fact recovery months with strong positive returns while stress indicators have not yet normalised. The economically meaningful gap lies in the calm periods that constitute two-thirds of the test period, where the regime-conditioned methods improve Sharpe ratios from 0.38--0.42 to 0.77--0.80. The regime signal's primary contribution is not protecting the portfolio during crashes but improving stock selection during normal times by identifying which momentum horizons are currently informative.

The ablation analysis sheds further light on the HMM's contribution. Market drawdown (DD), the feature most closely related to the mechanism identified by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}, is the single most informative HMM input. A single-feature HMM using DD alone produces an XGBoost Sharpe of 0.997, nearly matching the full four-feature model (1.007). Return dispersion (DISP) is the second most valuable input (0.955), capturing cross-sectional disagreement that signals changes in the correlation structure affecting momentum. Realised volatility (VOL, 0.855) and relative market participation (REL\_N, 0.810) contribute primarily by smoothing the regime signal and reducing false classifications, rather than by providing independent regime information. Together, these four equity-based stress indicators outperform both credit-based alternatives and the backward-looking market return classification of \citet{GHM2023}, because they capture dimensions of market stress that are not spanned by the momentum features themselves.

\subsection{Scope and Generalisability}\label{sec:generalisability}

Several limitations bound what can be concluded from these results. The test period (2011--2025) covers 167 months -- respectable for monthly portfolio analysis, but still a single historical path through a period dominated by low interest rates (2011--2021) followed by a rapid tightening cycle. The regime-conditioned strategies perform well across all three five-year sub-periods (Appendix~\ref{app:subperiod}), but whether the specific regime-momentum interactions identified here would persist in structurally different environments -- sustained high inflation, a prolonged bear market, or a market dominated by different sector composition -- remains an open question. The fixed training split (1990--2010) ensures strict out-of-sample discipline but also means the HMM parameters reflect a particular era; if the statistical properties of calm and panic regimes shift over time, the model may lose calibration. The sample is also limited to US equities; whether similar regime-dependent momentum structures exist in international markets or other asset classes is untested. Finally, the causal interpretation that the regime signal \emph{conditions} momentum's term structure, while supported by multiple lines of evidence (SHAP values, partial dependence plots, ablation studies, and the LR-XGB divergence), is ultimately inferred from observational data and a single modelling framework. Readers should interpret the findings as evidence consistent with this mechanism rather than as definitive proof.

\subsection{Future Work}\label{sec:future_work}

Several directions could extend this framework.

\paragraph{Adaptive regime estimation}
The current HMM is trained on 1990--2010 data and its parameters are held fixed during the test period. This assumes that the statistical properties of calm and panic regimes remain stationary over time. If the market undergoes structural change, for instance a permanent increase in baseline volatility due to algorithmic trading, higher-frequency information flow, or shifting monetary policy regimes, the trained thresholds may become miscalibrated. Concretely, features that were one standard deviation above the training mean may become the new normal, causing the model to classify structurally calm periods as panic. This concern is not hypothetical: the sustained elevation of $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ in the most recent years of the sample may partly reflect such drift rather than genuine prolonged stress. Rolling or expanding-window re-estimation of the HMM, or an online Bayesian updating scheme that allows the emission parameters to evolve over time, would address this limitation and likely improve the reliability of the regime signal in extended out-of-sample periods.

\paragraph{More than two regimes}
The binary calm/panic classification may be too coarse. A three-state model distinguishing, for example, low-volatility trending markets, choppy range-bound markets, and crisis episodes could enable more granular portfolio adaptation. Bayesian model selection criteria (e.g., marginal likelihood comparison) could guide the choice of the number of states.

\paragraph{Alternative feature sets}
The current HMM uses four stress indicators chosen for economic interpretability. High-frequency data (intraday volatility, order flow imbalances) or textual sentiment indicators could provide earlier regime detection. Similarly, the cross-sectional scoring models could be enriched with additional firm-level characteristics or alternative data sources.

\paragraph{Transaction costs and implementation}
The analysis applies a uniform 10 basis point one-way transaction cost,\footnote{The 10 basis point estimate reflects the transaction cost environment for large-cap, NYSE-listed securities. For a value-weighted portfolio using NYSE breakpoints, the effective cost may be even lower, as the portfolio is naturally tilted toward the most liquid names.} but does not model the higher costs that may affect smaller or less liquid stocks. Incorporating more realistic, stock-specific transaction cost estimates, particularly for the more volatile XGBoost strategy that may have higher turnover, would provide a more accurate comparison of net-of-cost performance across methods.

\paragraph{Investor-specific portfolio selection}
The risk-aversion analysis in Section~\ref{sec:risk_aversion} shows that incorporating a variance penalty into XGBoost's training objective reduces both volatility and the model's reliance on the regime signal, but at the cost of lower Sharpe ratios. An alternative approach would be to blend M1 and M2 portfolios directly, or to use a portfolio optimisation layer that jointly considers expected returns and covariances at the portfolio level rather than penalising individual stock variance. Such an end-to-end framework, for instance using differentiable portfolio optimisation or reinforcement learning, could potentially find risk-return combinations that neither model achieves alone.
