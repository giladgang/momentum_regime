\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This thesis developed a two-stage framework for regime-conditioned cross-sectional momentum investing. A Bayesian two-state Hidden Markov Model, estimated via Gibbs sampling on four macrofinancial stress indicators, produces a real-time filtered probability of market panic. Three portfolio construction methods of increasing complexity combine this regime signal with momentum at multiple horizons and fundamental characteristics to rank stocks each month.

As stated at the outset, the primary goal of this thesis is not to maximise portfolio returns but to uncover how the regime signal interacts with cross-sectional momentum. Out-of-sample evaluation over 167 months (2011--2025) using long-short portfolio construction provides evidence for four main findings. First, in long-short construction, only the nonlinear model (XGBoost, M2) produces economically meaningful risk-adjusted returns (Sharpe 1.08, annualised CAPM alpha of 23.8\%, $t = 4.08$). All other strategies collapse: logistic regression achieves a Sharpe of only $-$0.03, and fixed momentum is effectively dead. This demonstrates that the regime-momentum interaction is inherently nonlinear and cannot be captured by a single linear coefficient on the regime signal. Second, the regime signal is essential for long-short momentum stock selection. Without the HMM regime signal, XGBoost's performance drops substantially. The regime signal enables M2 to adapt \emph{which stocks} it selects across regimes -- exploiting momentum continuation \citep{JegadeeshTitman1993} in calm markets and short-term reversal \citep{BrunnermeierPedersen2009} during panic. Third, model-free information coefficient analysis confirms this mechanism: momentum is not a single stable anomaly but a family of horizon-specific effects whose composition rotates with market conditions. In calm markets, intermediate-to-long horizons predict returns; in panic, the pattern inverts to short-horizon reversal. Fourth, a risk-aversion analysis shows that the regime signal is most valuable when the investor is willing to bear risk: its relative importance declines with risk aversion, consistent with its role in enabling aggressive, regime-conditional stock selection.

These results complement the approach of \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} and \citet{BarrosoSantaClara2015}, who use regime information for \emph{exposure scaling} -- adjusting how much of a fixed momentum portfolio to hold. This thesis demonstrates an alternative channel: \emph{stock selection} -- adjusting which stocks to hold based on the regime. M2's Sharpe ratio of 0.97 exceeds D\&M's managed momentum (0.49) on the same data with the same transaction costs, because stock selection can exploit the regime-conditional rotation of momentum's term structure, whereas exposure scaling applies a uniform adjustment to a fixed portfolio. However, the two approaches address different vulnerabilities. M2's reversal bets during panic are short-lived (profitable at 1 month, negative at 3 and 6 months), making the strategy vulnerable to prolonged bear markets without recovery. D\&M's exposure scaling is specifically designed for sustained downturns. A hybrid approach that combines M2's stock selection with D\&M-style exposure reduction during prolonged stress could address both vulnerabilities simultaneously, a direction explored in Section~\ref{sec:future_work}.

The ablation analysis sheds further light on the HMM's contribution. Market drawdown (DD), the feature most closely related to the mechanism identified by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}, is the single most informative HMM input. DD alone achieves a Sharpe of 0.66, nearly matching the full four-feature model. Removing DD or REL\_N causes the largest performance drops, while DISP and CS each contribute less marginally but improve the stability of the regime signal across seed combinations. Together, these four indicators -- spanning equity market declines (DD), cross-sectional disagreement (DISP), structural market attrition (REL\_N), and corporate credit stress (CS) -- outperform the backward-looking market return classification of \citet{GHM2023}, because they capture dimensions of market stress from multiple market segments that are not spanned by the momentum features themselves.

\subsection{Scope and Generalisability}\label{sec:generalisability}

The strategy's primary vulnerability is a prolonged fundamental deterioration where beaten-down stocks do not recover. The holding period analysis (Section~\ref{sec:holding}) confirms this: reversal bets are profitable at 1 month but turn sharply negative at 3 and 6 months. During extended bear markets without recovery, the strategy would accumulate losses. A formal stress test (Appendix~\ref{app:stress_test}) shows positive skewness (+0.81) and a 95\% Value-at-Risk of $-$5.0\% per month, suggesting the strategy is more resilient than static momentum but not immune to sustained downturns.

Several additional limitations bound what can be concluded from these results. The test period (2011--2025) covers 167 months -- respectable for monthly portfolio analysis, but still a single historical path through a period dominated by low interest rates (2011--2021) followed by a rapid tightening cycle. Critically, this period does not contain a prolonged recession, so the strategy's vulnerability to sustained bear markets (identified in the holding period analysis) cannot be tested empirically. M2 performs well across all three five-year sub-periods (Appendix~\ref{app:subperiod}), but whether the specific regime-momentum interactions identified here would persist in structurally different environments -- sustained high inflation, a prolonged fundamental deterioration, or a market dominated by different sector composition -- remains an open question.

The Newey--West $t$-statistic for M2's mean excess return over the market is 0.90 (insignificant), which is expected for a market-neutral long-short strategy: the portfolio has near-zero market beta, so it should not be benchmarked against the market. The economically meaningful test is the factor model alpha, which is highly significant across all specifications ($t > 4$).

The fixed training split (1990--2010) ensures strict out-of-sample discipline but also means the HMM parameters reflect a particular era; if the statistical properties of calm and panic regimes shift over time, the model may lose calibration. The sample is limited to US equities; whether similar regime-dependent momentum structures exist in international markets or other asset classes is untested. Finally, the causal interpretation that the regime signal \emph{conditions} momentum's term structure, while supported by multiple lines of evidence (SHAP values, partial dependence plots, IC rotation, ablation studies, and the LR-XGB divergence), is ultimately inferred from observational data and a single modelling framework. Readers should interpret the findings as evidence consistent with this mechanism rather than as definitive proof.

\subsection{Future Work}\label{sec:future_work}

Several directions could extend this framework.

\paragraph{Adaptive regime estimation}
The current HMM is trained on 1990--2010 data and its parameters are held fixed during the test period. This assumes that the statistical properties of calm and panic regimes remain stationary over time. If the market undergoes structural change, for instance a permanent increase in baseline volatility due to algorithmic trading, higher-frequency information flow, or shifting monetary policy regimes, the trained thresholds may become miscalibrated. Concretely, features that were one standard deviation above the training mean may become the new normal, causing the model to classify structurally calm periods as panic. This concern is not hypothetical: the sustained elevation of $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ in the most recent years of the sample may partly reflect such drift rather than genuine prolonged stress. Rolling or expanding-window re-estimation of the HMM, or an online Bayesian updating scheme that allows the emission parameters to evolve over time, would address this limitation and likely improve the reliability of the regime signal in extended out-of-sample periods.

\paragraph{More than two regimes}
The binary calm/panic classification may be too coarse. A three-state model distinguishing, for example, low-volatility trending markets, choppy range-bound markets, and crisis episodes could enable more granular portfolio adaptation. Bayesian model selection criteria (e.g., marginal likelihood comparison) could guide the choice of the number of states.

\paragraph{Alternative feature sets}
The current HMM uses four stress indicators chosen for economic interpretability. High-frequency data (intraday volatility, order flow imbalances) or textual sentiment indicators could provide earlier regime detection. Similarly, the cross-sectional scoring models could be enriched with additional firm-level characteristics or alternative data sources.

\paragraph{Exposure-scaling circuit breaker}
The strategy's primary vulnerability is a prolonged fundamental deterioration where beaten-down stocks do not recover. The holding period analysis confirms this: reversal bets fail beyond one month. A natural extension is to combine M2's stock selection with a D\&M-style exposure-scaling overlay: when a sustained bear market indicator (e.g., a credit-spread threshold or consecutive months of elevated $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$) is triggered, overall portfolio exposure is reduced. This hybrid approach would preserve M2's superior stock selection during normal stress episodes while adding protection during prolonged downturns that the test period does not contain.

\paragraph{Transaction costs and implementation}
The analysis applies a uniform 10 basis point one-way transaction cost, but does not model the higher costs that may affect smaller or less liquid stocks. In long-short construction, M2's turnover is higher than in long-only due to regime-conditional switching between continuation and reversal bets. Incorporating more realistic, stock-specific transaction cost estimates would provide a more accurate picture of implementability.

\paragraph{Investor-specific portfolio selection}
The risk-aversion analysis in Section~\ref{sec:risk_aversion} shows that incorporating a variance penalty into XGBoost's training objective reduces both volatility and the model's reliance on the regime signal, but at the cost of lower Sharpe ratios. An alternative approach would be to use a portfolio optimisation layer that jointly considers expected returns and covariances at the portfolio level rather than penalising individual stock variance. Such an end-to-end framework, for instance using differentiable portfolio optimisation or reinforcement learning, could potentially find risk-return combinations that the current approach does not achieve.
