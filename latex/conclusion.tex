\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The central finding of this thesis is that cross-sectional momentum is not a single stable anomaly but a family of horizon-specific effects whose composition rotates with market conditions. In calm markets, intermediate-horizon continuation dominates; in panic, short-horizon reversal takes over. This rotation, confirmed by model-free information coefficient analysis, explains why only a nonlinear model (XGBoost) succeeds in long-short construction while a linear model with identical features collapses: the regime signal functions as a context variable that governs which momentum horizons are informative, an interaction that requires tree-based conditioning to exploit. Removing the regime signal halves the model's ability to distinguish future winners from losers, and the signal captures stress information not spanned by the momentum features themselves.

These findings complement the exposure-scaling approach of \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} and \citet{BarrosoSantaClara2015} with a stock-selection channel: rather than adjusting how much to hold, the regime signal adjusts which stocks to hold. Stress tests identify the strategy's vulnerability to prolonged fundamental deterioration, where the reversal mechanism breaks down, suggesting a hybrid approach as a direction for future work (Section~\ref{sec:future_work}).

\subsection{Scope and Generalisability}\label{sec:generalisability}

The strategy's primary vulnerability is a prolonged fundamental deterioration where beaten-down stocks do not recover. The stress test in Section~\ref{sec:stress_test} quantifies this: a 12-month recession reduces the worst-case Sharpe to 0.75 with a drawdown of $-$51\%, while a 24-month depression pushes the worst-case Sharpe to 0.44 with a $-$70\% drawdown. The strategy survives because calm-period continuation provides a floor, but a sustained downturn where reversal consistently fails would be painful. The 2011--2025 test period does not contain such a scenario.

Several additional limitations bound what can be concluded from these results. The test period (2011--2025) covers 167 months -- respectable for monthly portfolio analysis, but still a single historical path through a period dominated by low interest rates (2011--2021) followed by a rapid tightening cycle. Critically, this period does not contain a prolonged recession, though a true out-of-sample test on the 2008--2009 crisis (Appendix~\ref{app:gfc_oos}) confirms that M2 would have survived the momentum crash, earning +21\% to +43\% during the 2009 rebound while traditional momentum lost $-$60\%. M2 performs well across all three five-year sub-periods (Appendix~\ref{app:subperiod}), but whether the specific regime-momentum interactions identified here would persist in structurally different environments -- sustained high inflation, a prolonged fundamental deterioration, or a market dominated by different sector composition -- remains an open question.

The Newey--West $t$-statistic for M2's mean excess return over the market is 0.90 (insignificant), which is expected for a market-neutral long-short strategy: the portfolio has near-zero market beta, so it should not be benchmarked against the market. The economically meaningful test is the factor model alpha, which is highly significant across all specifications ($t > 4$).

The fixed training split (1990--2010) ensures strict out-of-sample discipline but also means the HMM parameters reflect a particular era. However, an expanding-window test (Appendix~\ref{app:expanding}) shows that re-estimating the HMM with more recent data does not improve performance, suggesting the regime structure is stable over the sample period. The sample is limited to US equities; whether similar regime-dependent momentum structures exist in international markets or other asset classes is untested. Finally, the causal interpretation that the regime signal \emph{conditions} momentum's term structure, while supported by multiple lines of evidence (SHAP values, partial dependence plots, IC rotation, ablation studies, and the LR-XGB divergence), is ultimately inferred from observational data and a single modelling framework. Readers should interpret the findings as evidence consistent with this mechanism rather than as definitive proof.

\subsection{Future Work}\label{sec:future_work}

Several directions could extend this framework.

\paragraph{Adaptive regime estimation}
The current HMM is trained on 1990--2010 data and its parameters are held fixed during the test period. This assumes that the statistical properties of calm and panic regimes remain stationary over time. If the market undergoes structural change, for instance a permanent increase in baseline volatility due to algorithmic trading, higher-frequency information flow, or shifting monetary policy regimes, the trained thresholds may become miscalibrated. Concretely, features that were one standard deviation above the training mean may become the new normal, causing the model to classify structurally calm periods as panic. This concern is not hypothetical: the sustained elevation of $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ in the most recent years of the sample may partly reflect such drift rather than genuine prolonged stress. Rolling or expanding-window re-estimation of the HMM, or an online Bayesian updating scheme that allows the emission parameters to evolve over time, would address this limitation and likely improve the reliability of the regime signal in extended out-of-sample periods.

\paragraph{More than two regimes}
The binary calm/panic classification may be too coarse. A three-state model distinguishing, for example, low-volatility trending markets, choppy range-bound markets, and crisis episodes could enable more granular portfolio adaptation. Bayesian model selection criteria (e.g., marginal likelihood comparison) could guide the choice of the number of states.

\paragraph{International replication}
The current analysis is limited to US equities. Testing whether the continuation-to-reversal rotation and the nonlinearity finding replicate in international markets (e.g., Europe, Japan, emerging markets) would strengthen the generalisability of the mechanism.

\paragraph{Alternative feature sets}
The current HMM uses four stress indicators chosen for economic interpretability. High-frequency data (intraday volatility, order flow imbalances) or textual sentiment indicators could provide earlier regime detection.

\paragraph{Exposure-scaling circuit breaker}
The strategy's primary vulnerability is a prolonged fundamental deterioration where beaten-down stocks do not recover. The holding period analysis confirms this: reversal bets fail beyond one month. A natural extension is to combine M2's stock selection with a D\&M-style exposure-scaling overlay: when a sustained bear market indicator (e.g., consecutive months of elevated $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$) is triggered, overall portfolio exposure is reduced. Preliminary tests on the current sample -- scaling exposure to 50\% after 2--4 consecutive months with $\pi_t^{\text{filter}} > 0.7$--$0.9$ -- reduce the Sharpe from 1.08 to approximately 1.00--1.03 without meaningfully improving maximum drawdown. This is expected: the 2011--2025 test period contains no prolonged recession, so the circuit breaker only cuts profitable panic months. The value of this mechanism would emerge in a sustained downturn outside the current sample, making it a natural candidate for out-of-sample validation on extended data.

\paragraph{Regime-conditional feature sets}
The ablation analysis (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results}) shows that adding fundamental characteristics reduces calm-period performance while leaving panic-period performance unchanged. This suggests that the optimal feature set may itself be regime-dependent: momentum and the regime signal suffice during panic (where reversal dominates), but fundamentals might add value during calm if used differently -- for example, as a quality filter applied after the momentum ranking rather than as an input to the model. Exploring regime-conditional feature sets, where the model uses different inputs depending on the market state, could improve calm-period stock selection without diluting the reversal mechanism.

\paragraph{Transaction costs and implementation}
The analysis applies a uniform 10 basis point one-way transaction cost, but does not model the higher costs that may affect smaller or less liquid stocks. In long-short construction, M2's turnover is higher than in long-only due to regime-conditional switching between continuation and reversal bets. Incorporating more realistic, stock-specific transaction cost estimates would provide a more accurate picture of implementability.

\paragraph{Investor-specific portfolio selection}
The risk-aversion analysis in Section~\ref{sec:risk_aversion} shows that incorporating a variance penalty into XGBoost's training objective reduces both volatility and the model's reliance on the regime signal, but at the cost of lower Sharpe ratios. An alternative approach would be to use a portfolio optimisation layer that jointly considers expected returns and covariances at the portfolio level rather than penalising individual stock variance. Such an end-to-end framework, for instance using differentiable portfolio optimisation or reinforcement learning, could potentially find risk-return combinations that the current approach does not achieve.
