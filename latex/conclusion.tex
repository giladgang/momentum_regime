\section{Summary, Conclusions and Recommendations}\label{sec:conclusion}

\paragraph{Summary}
This thesis asked whether cross-sectional momentum's predictability is regime-dependent and whether exploiting that dependence requires nonlinear modelling. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} developed a two-stage framework that combines a Bayesian Hidden Markov regime model with a cross-sectional stock-selection model. Section~\ref{sec:data} described the U.S. equity sample and the UK and Japan replication samples. Section~\ref{sec:results} reported the out-of-sample portfolio performance, the cluster analysis that characterises the regime-momentum mechanism, robustness across risk aversion and historical stress, and the international replication. The remainder of this chapter draws conclusions from these results, discusses their limitations, and recommends directions for future research.

\paragraph{Conclusions and Contributions}
The central finding is that cross-sectional momentum's predictability shifts with the market regime, in both strength and direction, and that exploiting this shift requires nonlinear conditioning on a regime signal. The Bayesian HMM filtered probability $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, combined with the twelve momentum horizons in an XGBoost ensemble, generates an out-of-sample Sharpe of $1.11$ and a six-factor alpha of $24.1\%$ ($t = 4.81$) over 2011--2025; linear baselines on identical features collapse, and removing the regime signal cuts XGB's Sharpe by more than $60\%$. The mechanism replicates internationally on UK and Japanese equities with locally-fitted HMMs, and the 25-year expanding-window backtest spanning 2000--2024 confirms it survives the historical stress events outside the main test sample.

The thesis makes two contributions to the momentum literature. First, $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ acts as a routing variable that flips the direction of momentum selection in panic, producing two reversal modes with no direct analogue in the classical reversal literature: a post-panic recovery state where the long leg holds stocks below the cross-section average while the market itself rallies, and a uniform term-structure-wide reversal at the panic peak (clusters 3 and 4; Section~\ref{sec:novelty}). Second, the model selects by momentum level rather than rank, reframing momentum's crash management as a re-ranking decision rather than the exposure-scaling response of \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} and \citet{BarrosoSantaClara2015}; the leg-beta inversion that drives traditional momentum's crashes is absent in every cluster (Appendix~\ref{app:leg_betas}). The two approaches are complementary, and a hybrid combining XGB's stock-selection with a D\&M-style exposure-scaling overlay would address the reversal-failure vulnerability without sacrificing XGB's stock-selection advantage in shorter panics.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Reversal-failure vulnerability}
The strategy's primary vulnerability is a prolonged economic deterioration where beaten-down stocks do not recover because their underlying businesses are failing rather than because of temporary mispricing. The dot-com bust is its historical realisation (Section~\ref{sec:historical_stress}). A sustained multi-year decline without any reversal (a depression-style outcome) lies outside the 25-year historical sample.

\paragraph{Test period scope}
The 2011--2025 test period does not contain a prolonged recession. Performance is strongest in 2021--2025, a period with frequent regime transitions; in calm-dominated years the model earns modest returns from standard continuation. A permanently calm market would reduce the strategy to ordinary momentum.

\paragraph{Frozen HMM parameters and regime drift}
The HMM is estimated on 1990--2010 and held fixed during the test period. This assumes that the statistical properties of calm and panic regimes remain stationary. If the market undergoes structural change (a permanent increase in baseline volatility due to algorithmic trading or shifting monetary policy regimes, for example), the trained thresholds may become miscalibrated, with structurally calm periods classified as panic. The sustained elevation of $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ in recent years may partly reflect such drift rather than genuine prolonged stress. The 25-year expanding-window backtest in Section~\ref{sec:historical_stress} (in which the HMM is re-estimated annually using only data available at the time) reproduces the production result on the 2011--2024 overlap, indicating that the regime structure is reasonably stable; online re-estimation would still provide a stronger guarantee.

\paragraph{Memoryless regime conditioning}
XGBoost observes only the current month's features and has no memory of the $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ trajectory, so a fresh spike and a sustained elevation are treated identically despite their different implications for reversal. Section~\ref{sec:future_work} sketches a sequential extension.

\paragraph{Implementation frictions}
The analysis assumes frictionless short selling and applies a uniform 10 basis point one-way transaction cost. Cost sensitivity (Appendix~\ref{app:transaction_costs}) confirms XGB remains profitable at 50 bps, but the uniform cost understates the borrow fees and hard-to-borrow constraints that would apply most strongly to the short leg for small or less liquid stocks. Value-weighting and NYSE decile breakpoints tilt the portfolio toward large, liquid names, partially mitigating this concern.

\paragraph{Specification search}
The HMM feature selection procedure uses out-of-sample portfolio Sharpe in its second pass, meaning the selected combination (DD+DISP+REL\_N+CS) is informed by test-period outcomes. Section~\ref{sec:feature_selection} discusses the mitigating factors in full (the quality screen in Pass~1, the cross-seed stability criterion in Pass~3, and the qualitatively similar performance of leave-one-out specifications, Table~\ref{tab:hmm_feature_ablation}); but the reported 1.11 Sharpe should be read with this caveat.

\subsection{Recommendations for Future Research}\label{sec:future_work}

Four directions extend this framework most directly.

\paragraph{Richer regime-signal representation}
The current cross-sectional model conditions only on the contemporaneous filtered probability $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, which discards information about regime dynamics. Two complementary extensions are natural. Looking forward, the HMM's transition probabilities support a $k$-step regime forecast $P(s_{t+k} = 1 \mid \mathbf{z}_{1:t})$, which would let the portfolio reposition before a regime shift rather than reacting to it; the expected gain is concentrated in transition months, where the current framework trades on a stale view of the regime in precisely the months when the portfolio is furthest from its regime-conditional optimum. Looking backward, the trajectory of $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ distinguishes a fresh spike (where mean reversion is most likely) from a sustained elevation (where the reversal mechanism may be breaking down), but the current scalar input collapses these into the same value. A sequential model, such as an LSTM, a transformer over a fixed window of regime features, or feature engineering with lagged $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ values, a short-window rate of change, and a duration-since-entry counter, would expose the trajectory directly. The two extensions are complementary; the forward direction provides anticipation, the backward direction provides memory.

\paragraph{Alternative nonlinear models}
XGBoost is one of several nonlinear model classes, and the central mechanism (that $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ gates the use of momentum horizons) should in principle be recoverable by any sufficiently expressive architecture. The Random Forest comparison in Appendix~\ref{app:xgb_sensitivity} partially tests this; bagging still selects $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ as a material feature, but both Sharpe and the regime signal's SHAP share fall substantially, showing that the boosted additive structure is what elevates the regime signal from a marginal predictor to a primary gating variable. The more informative test is whether the mechanism survives under genuinely different inductive biases. An attention-based model would learn horizon-by-regime weights directly and produce an interpretable per-horizon attention profile that could be compared against the SHAP decomposition in Table~\ref{tab:shap}; a regularised multilayer perceptron with explicit $\pi \times mom_k$ and $mom_k \times mom_j$ interaction inputs would isolate whether the dependence on boosting reflects sequential residual fitting or a shortage of data for richer architectures. Recovering the same mechanism across these alternatives would strengthen the claim that the regime-conditional shift is a property of the data rather than of XGBoost specifically.

\paragraph{Regime-conditional feature sets}
Adding ten firm-level fundamentals to XGB reduces Sharpe (Section~\ref{sec:fund_ablation}) because the new features absorb a substantial share of SHAP importance and dilute the regime-momentum signal. The fund-only variant matches the baseline's calm performance but produces a far weaker panic Sharpe, because firm-level characteristics describe stable attributes that do not flip direction when the regime shifts. The optimal feature set may itself be regime-dependent. During panic, when reversal dominates, momentum and the regime signal carry the mechanism. During calm, when the continuation premium is weaker and more diffuse, fundamentals may provide a useful second dimension. A layered construction in which XGB produces the initial long-short ranking and fundamentals enter as a post-ranking filter (excluding bottom-quintile-quality names from the long leg, high-leverage names from the short leg) is a natural starting point; a more ambitious variant trains separate cross-sectional models for the calm and panic branches, each with its own feature set. Both designs require careful out-of-sample validation because the regime-conditional split reduces effective sample size.

\paragraph{Exposure-scaling overlay (D\&M complement)}
An exposure-scaling overlay layered on top of XGB's stock-selection requires a triggering rule. Natural candidates are $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ above a threshold for a minimum number of consecutive months, or its rolling maximum over a fixed window exceeding a calibrated bound. Preliminary checks on the 2011--2025 sample show no improvement from the overlay because the sample contains no prolonged recession, so the circuit breaker only cuts profitable panic months; its value would emerge in a sustained downturn outside the current window. The dot-com sub-period of the 25-year expanding-window backtest (Section~\ref{sec:historical_stress}) is a natural setting to evaluate candidate overlay rules.
