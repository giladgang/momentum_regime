\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

All strategies are evaluated out-of-sample on 2011--2025 (167 months), using the long-short construction described in Section~\ref{sec:stage2}. The central question is whether momentum's cross-sectional structure is regime-dependent, and whether exploiting this dependence requires nonlinear modelling.


\subsection{Portfolio Performance}\label{sec:performance_results}

\subsubsection{Strategy Comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:performance} reports the out-of-sample performance of all strategies.\footnote{All Sharpe ratios are computed using raw returns rather than excess returns above the risk-free rate. Since the same convention is applied to every strategy, relative comparisons are unaffected. The risk-free rate averaged roughly 2\% annualised over the 2011--2025 window, so absolute Sharpe ratios are overstated by approximately 0.05--0.10. Sharpe is computed from the arithmetic monthly mean annualised by $\sqrt{12}$, while annualised returns reported elsewhere are compound (geometric); under high volatility the two definitions of average return differ, so Sharpe should not be inferred from a table by dividing annualised return by annualised volatility (e.g.\ at depth~1, the reported Sharpe is $0.53$ but $8.7\% / 19.4\% = 0.45$).}

\input{tables/table_performance}

Momentum's cross-sectional predictability is regime-dependent, and exploiting this dependence requires both a probabilistic regime signal and a nonlinear model. XGBoost combined with the HMM filtered probability (M2, mom+$\pi$) achieves an annualised Sharpe of 1.11 with a six-factor alpha of 24.1\% ($t = 4.81$; Appendix~\ref{app:factor_alphas}). Block bootstrap paired tests reject equality of Sharpe ratios between M2 and each benchmark in Table~\ref{tab:performance} at $p < 0.025$; M2's own 95\% Sharpe confidence interval is $[0.67, 1.53]$, comfortably above zero, while every benchmark's interval straddles zero (Appendix~\ref{app:bootstrap}). An expanding-window backtest extending the OOS sample to 30 years (1995--2024) reproduces this Sharpe within sampling noise on the 167-month overlap with the main test period (0.88; see Section~\ref{sec:historical_stress}, which also covers the dot-com bust and GFC out-of-sample). Every other specification fails to produce economically meaningful long-short performance, and the pattern of failures is informative.

Unconditional 12-month momentum achieves a Sharpe of 0.06 with a maximum drawdown of $-$72.2\%, exposing the crash vulnerability documented by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} and \citet{BarrosoSantaClara2015}. A deterministic regime switch (M0) improves this marginally to 0.11. The logistic regression (M1), given identical features to M2, collapses to $-$0.03; a Ridge regression with the same 13 features but a continuous-return target (matching XGBoost's) fails more decisively, with Sharpe $-$0.57 and a coefficient on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ of $+0.003$ (Appendix~\ref{app:ridge}). The failure is therefore linearity itself, not the classification target. Hand-engineering the missing nonlinearity into M1 through $\pi^2$ and $\pi \times mom_k$ terms lifts it to 0.32, establishing that the regime-momentum interaction is real while also showing that pre-specified interactions recover less than a third of what XGBoost finds automatically. A fund-augmented variant (M2: mom+$\pi$+fund) adds ten firm-level fundamentals to M2's feature set; it reduces the Sharpe to 0.89 while lowering beta from 0.45 to 0.07 and tightening the maximum drawdown from $-$22.8\% to $-$18.3\%. Section~\ref{sec:fund_ablation} analyses this variant alongside several feature-set ablations, which together provide additional evidence that $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$'s role is specifically to condition the momentum term structure.

\subsubsection{Regime Signal Ablation}

The regime signal is essential. Removing $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ and retraining on the 12 momentum features alone drops the Sharpe to 0.43 (Table~\ref{tab:regime_signal_ablation}). Replacing $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ with the four raw stress indicators (DD, DISP, REL\_N, CS) performs even worse (Sharpe 0.26): with four additional dimensions the model must reconstruct the regime structure at each split, while the HMM distils those indicators into a single dimension that one tree split can condition on. The HMM's role is not to provide a convenient feature but to solve regime identification \emph{before} stock selection, freeing the cross-sectional model to use the regime signal rather than construct it. If the model cannot extract a meaningful regime signal even when given the four raw indicators directly, it certainly cannot do so from momentum features alone, which are one further step removed from the underlying regime dynamics.

\subsubsection{Feature-Set Ablation}\label{sec:fund_ablation}

The regime signal ablation shows that removing $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ reduces Sharpe by more than 60\%. A complementary question is whether adding widely used firm-level fundamentals strengthens the strategy, or whether the regime-momentum interaction is load-bearing on its own. This subsection tests both directions by adding, substituting, and removing feature blocks from the production configuration.

Ten firm-level fundamentals are tested: seven standard value-quality-size characteristics (book-to-market, return on equity, leverage, asset growth, gross profitability, earnings growth, and log market equity) and three cash-flow-based earnings-quality measures (operating cash flow over assets, free cash flow over assets, and the accrual component of earnings scaled by assets). All are computed from Compustat quarterly data with a four-month reporting lag to ensure real-time availability; definitions are given in Appendix~\ref{app:fundamental_features}. Each variant is trained at production settings (50 XGBoost seeds, depth 4, learning rate 0.05) and evaluated identically to M2.

\input{tables/table_fundamentals_ablation}

Adding the ten fundamentals to the baseline reduces Sharpe from 1.11 to 0.89 (Table~\ref{tab:fund_ablation}). The new features absorb 44\% of total SHAP attention, redirecting it away from the momentum term structure (54\% to 27\%) and the regime signal (46\% to 29\%) without net improvement in portfolio performance. Beta falls from 0.45 to 0.07 and maximum drawdown tightens from $-$22.8\% to $-$18.3\%; the strategy's excess-return Newey-West $t$-statistic falls from 4.37\textsuperscript{***} to 3.56\textsuperscript{***}, still highly significant but materially weaker. The fund-augmented variant therefore behaves more like a market-neutral low-beta strategy than a high-alpha regime-conditioned one.

The fund-only variant (ten fundamentals, no momentum, no regime signal) identifies the source of the dilution directly. Its calm Sharpe of 0.86 is essentially equal to the baseline's 0.84, but its panic Sharpe of 0.38 is a fraction of the baseline's 1.53. Firm-level characteristics describe stable attributes (leverage, profitability, valuation) that do not flip direction when the market enters panic. Adding the regime signal to fundamentals (fund + $\pi$, panic Sharpe 0.72) roughly doubles the fund-only panic Sharpe but remains well short of the full model, because without the momentum term structure there is no directional relationship for the regime signal to reorganise. Removing $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ from the momentum + fundamentals variant drops Sharpe to 0.63 (panic 0.57), close to fund-only's 0.59. Across both ablations, removing either the momentum term structure or the regime signal causes the same collapse: fundamentals cannot substitute for the regime-momentum interaction, which is the load-bearing mechanism.

These ablations support the interpretation that $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ is a context variable for the momentum term structure specifically, rather than a general-purpose conditioning input. Characteristics that describe stable firm quality are useful in calm markets, where the fund-only calm Sharpe matches the baseline's. But those same characteristics carry no information about regime shifts, and adding them to the joint model dilutes the signal that does. A regime-conditional feature-set design, in which the model uses different inputs in calm and panic, is discussed as future work in Section~\ref{sec:future_work}.


\subsection{The Regime-Momentum Interaction}\label{sec:regime_results}

M2 uses momentum differently across regimes (Table~\ref{tab:regime_sharpe}): Sharpe 1.53 in panic against 0.84 in calm. The regime signal adds the most value precisely when the model switches from continuation to reversal. Where exposure-scaling methods reduce positions during stress to limit losses, M2 leans into panic by reconstructing the portfolio to exploit the reversal (Section~\ref{sec:dm_comparison}). This composition-side mechanism leaves a measurable signature on the legs' market betas: M2's long-leg $\beta$ rises from 1.40 in calm to 1.65 in panic while the short-leg $\beta$ falls from 1.22 to 0.98 (Appendix~\ref{app:leg_betas}). The cross-leg spread $\beta_L - \beta_S$ widens from 0.18 to 0.67 but never inverts -- both legs remain positive-$\beta$ in panic, in contrast to the leg-beta inversion that drives traditional momentum's crashes \citep{DanielMoskowitz2016}.

\input{tables/table_regime_sharpe}

The regime signal is structurally embedded in the ensemble rather than applied selectively. Across the 25{,}000 trees of the 50-seed production ensemble, 18{,}463 (73.9\%) contain at least one split on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, and a typical long-leg stock's path through the ensemble passes through a $\pi$-split 55\% of the time: regime conditioning is built into roughly three-quarters of the model's decision rules and consulted in the majority of individual stock evaluations. The economically informative statistic is how this reliance is state-dependent. In panic months, 65\% of the long leg's total predicted return flows from $\pi$-splitting trees; in calm months, only 17\% does. The model leans on the regime signal predominantly during stress, and its calm-period predictions come mostly from trees that operate on momentum alone. This pattern is consistent with the Sharpe decomposition in Table~\ref{tab:regime_sharpe}: the regime signal adds the most value in exactly the months where it carries the most weight.

M2's predictions are decomposed using two complementary tools. SHAP values \citep{Lundberg2017} measure how much each horizon contributes to the model's prediction (definitions in Appendix~\ref{app:shap}). Cross-sectional z-scores measure where in the momentum distribution the model's selected stocks fall: for each stock in the long or short leg, the z-score at horizon $k$ is $(mom_k^s - \bar{mom}_k) / \sigma_k$, computed from the cross-sectional mean and standard deviation of all stocks that month. Figure~\ref{fig:term_structure} reports these measures averaged across all selected stocks and all months in each regime.

Aggregate feature importance is split almost evenly: $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ accounts for 46\% of total SHAP, the 12 momentum horizons together for 54\% (Table~\ref{tab:shap}). Between regimes, momentum's share rises from 50\% in calm to 58\% in panic while $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$'s share falls from 50\% to 42\%. The model leans on momentum more heavily during stress, but uses it in a fundamentally different way.

\subsubsection{Calm: Momentum Continuation}\label{sec:calm}

\paragraph{Z-scores: characteristics of selected stocks}
The z-score curve for the calm long leg is humped: near zero at month~1, peaking at $+0.39$ at month~8, declining to $+0.17$ at month~12. The short leg mirrors this with negative z-scores, deepest at months 8--10 ($-0.27$ to $-0.30$). The long-short spread is 1.33\% per month. Notably, the peak z-score of $+0.39$ is well above average but far from the distribution's upper tail -- the model does not select the highest-momentum stocks (Section~\ref{sec:novelty} discusses this).

\paragraph{SHAP: where the model discriminates}
SHAP is concentrated at the long end: months 11 and 12 are the most influential individual horizons (18.5\% and 18.0\% of long-leg SHAP), with months 8--12 together accounting for 67\%. Months 1--7 each contribute between 3.7\% and 6.2\%. The model receives 12 undifferentiated momentum horizons and independently recovers the intermediate-to-long-horizon dominance documented by \citet{NovyMarx2012}. The z-score peak at month~8 and the SHAP peak at months 11--12 capture different things: z-scores show where the momentum signal is \emph{clearest} (the widest long-short separation), while SHAP shows where the signal is \emph{most influential} for the model's prediction.

\subsubsection{Panic: Reversal}\label{sec:panic}

\paragraph{Z-scores: characteristics of selected stocks}
The humped curve flattens in panic. The long leg's z-score drops to $-0.09$ to $-0.19$ across all horizons ($-19.5\%$ trailing 12-month momentum, $-5.2\%$ at one month). The uniform depression is consistent with non-discretionary selling pressure \citep{BrunnermeierPedersen2009, VayanosWoolley2013} that depresses returns at every lookback simultaneously, with constrained arbitrage capital \citep{ShleiferVishny1997} allowing the dislocation to persist.

The short leg is asymmetric at month~1: its z-score spikes to $+0.30$ against the long leg's $-0.19$. The model shorts stocks with recent short-term recoveries ($+3.7\%$ at one month) but negative longer-horizon momentum, consistent with unsustained stress rallies \citep{DeBondtThaler1985}. The long-short spread in panic is 3.09\% per month, more than double the calm-period spread.

\paragraph{Crash versus recovery within panic}
The z-score profiles reported above average across all 59 panic months. Decomposing panic by concurrent market direction reveals that this average masks two distinct selection patterns (Figure~\ref{fig:panic_subtypes}). In the 18 months where the market declined during panic (crash months), the long leg is below the short leg at every horizon (spread of $-0.15$ to $-0.41$), and the strategy loses $-0.83\%$ per month (Sharpe $-$0.48). In the 41 months where the market rose during panic (recovery months), the pattern changes: the long leg remains below the short leg at months 1--5, but flips above it at months 7--12 (spread of $+0.08$ to $+0.15$), and the strategy earns $+5.39\%$ per month (Sharpe 2.40).

The overall panic Sharpe of 1.53 is driven entirely by recovery months. The model applies the same learned function to both sub-types, but during recoveries some stocks have already begun to rebound at longer horizons, providing separation that the model exploits. During crashes, stocks continue to fall and the reversal bet fails. This provides direct evidence for the reversal failure vulnerability discussed in Section~\ref{sec:stress_test}.

\paragraph{SHAP: where the model discriminates}
The SHAP distribution shifts modestly toward shorter horizons compared to calm, but months 7--12 still dominate (accounting for roughly 63\% of the long leg's momentum SHAP). Month~1 SHAP rises notably on the short side (10.1\%, compared to 5.0\% in calm), indicating that the model's predictions become more sensitive to recent returns when identifying stocks to short during stress. This is consistent with \citet{Nagel2012}, who shows that returns to short-term reversal spike during crises when liquidity provision is most valuable.

\subsubsection{Heterogeneity within Panic}\label{sec:heterogeneity_panic}

The panic profile reported in Section~\ref{sec:panic} averages 59 monthly cross-sectional snapshots into a single curve, and the crash/recovery decomposition in Section~\ref{sec:panic} splits that average along the axis of concurrent market direction. This subsection introduces a second, orthogonal decomposition along the axis of cross-sectional momentum profile. Applying $K$-means clustering ($k=3$) to each panic month's long-leg 12-horizon z-profile recovers three sub-regimes with stable sizes $(17, 18, 24)$ across six independent random seeds, silhouette $0.46$, and Adjusted Rand Index $0.95$ against an independent retraining of the XGBoost ensemble with a disjoint set of 50 seeds. The same structure is recovered when clustering on the long-short spread vector or on the level-removed shape vector, indicating the decomposition reflects a feature of the cross-section rather than an artefact of the input choice.

Figure~\ref{fig:zscore_subregimes} and Table~\ref{tab:zscore_subregime} report the three sub-regimes.

\paragraph{Mild panic ($n=24$, $\bar\pi = 0.96$)}
A humped long-leg profile peaking at $z = +0.48$ at month~8, qualitatively indistinguishable from the calm profile in Section~\ref{sec:calm}. The HMM is highly confident these months are panic, yet the cross-sectional momentum landscape exhibits the canonical continuation shape. Episodes include 2012-05 to 2012-08, 2019-02 to 2019-03, 2020-02 / 05 / 07, and most of the months in the 2023-09 to 2024-11 panic stretch (in which deep crisis is absent altogether). Conditional Sharpe $1.38$, well above the calm $0.84$. The 2023-09 to 2024-11 stretch is the empirical realisation of the sustained-$\pi$-elevation concern in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}: the cross-section in those months is calm-like, and the model's selection adapts accordingly.

\paragraph{Transition ($n=18$, $\bar\pi = 0.95$)}
A weak U-dip with the deepest values at months 4--5 ($z \approx -0.24$). Episodically these months wrap deep-crisis episodes (e.g.\ 2018-12 $\rightarrow$ 2019-01 $\rightarrow$ 2019-02). Conditional Sharpe $1.51$.

\paragraph{Deep crisis ($n=17$, $\bar\pi = 1.00$)}
A long-leg profile uniformly negative across all 12 horizons (mean $z = -0.87$), deepest at the long end ($z = -0.94$ at month~12). This is the sub-regime in which the term-structure-wide loser portfolio described in Section~\ref{sec:panic} appears in pure form. It includes 2011-09 / 10 (Eurozone), 2015-10 and 2016-01 to 2016-03 (China devaluation), 2019-01, 2020-04 (COVID), and the 2022-05 to 2023-01 stretch -- nine consecutive months of the rate-hike bear market. Conditional Sharpe $1.84$, the highest of any sub-regime.

The HMM filtered probability cannot distinguish the three sub-regimes (mean $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ is $0.96$ in mild panic, $0.95$ in transition, and $1.00$ in deep crisis); what separates them is the realised cross-sectional momentum landscape. The conditional Sharpes reported above are post-hoc diagnostics rather than investable strategy returns, because the cluster labels are determined after the fact from each month's realised long-leg z-profile rather than from real-time information; the pooled panic Sharpe of $1.53$ in Section~\ref{sec:regime_results} is the corresponding investable quantity.\footnote{The conditional Sharpes $1.84$, $1.51$, $1.38$ on counts $17$, $18$, $24$ give a count-weighted average of $1.55$, close to but not equal to the pooled $1.53$ because the Sharpe ratio does not aggregate linearly across sub-samples.}

\paragraph{State-dependent role of $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$}
The aggregate panic $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ SHAP share of $42\%$ reported in Section~\ref{sec:regime_results} masks substantial within-panic variation. Computing the SHAP share within each sub-regime separately yields $52.0\%$ in mild panic, $46.6\%$ in transition, and $13.3\%$ in deep crisis (Table~\ref{tab:zscore_subregime}, all-stock-month rows). The leg-resolved decomposition shows the same pattern with a sharper contrast: for the long leg, $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$'s SHAP share is $34.5\%$ in mild panic, $26.3\%$ in transition, and $7.5\%$ in deep crisis -- mild panic's share is the highest across all sub-regimes including calm at $27.9\%$. For the short leg, $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ contributes $53.2\%$ in mild panic, $48.2\%$ in transition, and $22.4\%$ in deep crisis. The regime signal carries the most weight precisely when the cross-section is least informative about the regime: in mild-panic months a momentum-only model would replicate the calm selection rule, and $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ is the feature that overrides this; in deep-crisis months the cross-section is so distinctive that momentum splits alone suffice. This sharpens the routing interpretation in Section~\ref{sec:tree_paths}: $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ functions as a router whose marginal contribution scales with cross-sectional ambiguity, not as a switch with constant influence. The month-1 short-side SHAP spike noted in Section~\ref{sec:panic} ($10.1\%$ of momentum-only SHAP in panic) decomposes accordingly: $9.7\%$ in mild panic, $8.1\%$ in transition, $16.6\%$ in deep crisis; the spike is concentrated in the deep-crisis sub-regime, where \citet{Nagel2012}'s liquidity-evaporation mechanism is most active.

\paragraph{Triangulation with leg-betas}
The same heterogeneity is visible in the leg-beta picture introduced in Section~\ref{sec:regime_results}. The published panic $\beta_L$ of $1.65$ -- attributed there to M2's tilt amplification -- decomposes to $1.27$ in mild panic, $2.24$ in transition, and $1.50$ in deep crisis. The amplification is concentrated in transition months; in mild panic the long-leg $\beta$ falls below the calm value of $1.40$. The cross-leg spread $\beta_L - \beta_S$ remains strictly positive in all three sub-regimes ($+0.26$ in mild panic, $+1.23$ in transition, $+0.55$ in deep crisis), so the no-leg-beta-inversion claim of Section~\ref{sec:regime_results} holds within every panic sub-regime, not just on average. The cross-sectional z-profile and the CAPM leg-beta thus converge: mild panic is closer to calm than to deep crisis on both measures, deep crisis is the most extreme sub-regime, and aggregating panic months obscures which mechanism operates in which months.

\subsubsection{Discussion}\label{sec:novelty}

Prior work has established that momentum profits depend on market state \citep{Cooper2004}, that momentum crashes are tied to rebounds after stress \citep{DanielMoskowitz2016}, and that blending fast and slow momentum signals across market cycles can improve performance \citep{GHM2023}. The analysis above contributes three findings that go beyond these results.

\paragraph{The deep-crisis sub-regime selects a term-structure-wide loser portfolio}
The decomposition in Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity_panic} shows that the flat, negative z-score profile appears in pure form in the 17 deep-crisis months, where the long leg is uniformly negative across all 12 horizons (mean $z = -0.87$). This shape is qualitatively different from short-term reversal \citep{Jegadeesh1990, Nagel2012}, which targets month~1 only, and from long-term contrarian strategies \citep{DeBondtThaler1985}, which operate at multi-year horizons. In calm and in mild-panic months, the model recovers the intermediate-horizon continuation shape of \citet{NovyMarx2012}. The contrast in selection shape across regimes -- humped continuation in calm and mild panic, flat-negative in deep crisis -- has not, to the best of our knowledge, been documented in this form.

\paragraph{The regime signal acts as a router with state-dependent influence}
The regime does not flip one horizon from continuation to reversal or shift weight between a fast and a slow signal. It alters how the model uses the entire 12-horizon term structure: buying from the upper tail at intermediate horizons in calm and from the lower tail at all horizons in deep crisis. The SHAP distribution remains stable across regimes (Table~\ref{tab:zscore_shap_detail}): the model attends to the same horizons in both states. What changes is the direction of selection at those horizons, and the strength with which $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ does the routing. As Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity_panic} shows, $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$'s long-leg SHAP share is $34.5\%$ in mild-panic months, where the cross-section appears calm-like and the model relies on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ to override the surface appearance, and $7.5\%$ in deep-crisis months where the momentum cross-section is so extreme that momentum splits alone suffice. The regime signal therefore functions as a router whose marginal contribution scales with cross-sectional ambiguity, not as a behaviour switch with constant influence.

\paragraph{Selection by momentum level, not momentum rank}
Traditional momentum strategies rank stocks by trailing return at a fixed lookback \citep{JegadeeshTitman1993}. Here, the portfolio ranks on \emph{predicted forward return} -- a learned function of all 12 horizons and the regime signal. The model learns which momentum \emph{level} is associated with future outperformance. In calm, this distinction is modest: the long leg's z-scores peak at $+0.39$, above average but far from the upper tail. In deep crisis (Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity_panic}), it becomes decisive: the long leg selects stocks \emph{below} the cross-sectional average at every horizon, a selection no fixed-rank strategy would produce.

\paragraph{The role of nonlinearity}
That nonlinear models outperform linear ones in cross-sectional asset pricing is well established \citep{Gu2020}. What is specific here is \emph{where} the nonlinearity matters: in transforming a macrofinancial regime probability into a conditioning variable for multi-horizon momentum selection. Section~\ref{sec:nonlinearity} provides structural evidence that the nonlinearity is not generic feature interaction but specifically the ability of $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ to act as a context switch.

\subsection{Nonlinearity and Feature Interactions}\label{sec:nonlinearity}

\subsubsection{Tree Path Analysis}\label{sec:tree_paths}

The per-horizon decomposition in Section~\ref{sec:regime_results} treats each horizon independently, leaving open whether the model relies on specific \emph{interactions} between horizons. To test this, 5{,}000 sampled stocks from each regime$\times$leg group are traced through 50{,}000 individual trees, recording which momentum horizon groups each decision path splits on (Appendix~\ref{app:combo_analysis}). Horizons are grouped into four bands: short (S, months 1--3), medium (M, months 4--6), intermediate (I, months 7--9), and long (L, months 10--12). Table~\ref{tab:combo_freq} reports the frequency of each of the 15 possible combinations; each column sums to 100\%.

\input{tables/table_combo_freq}

The combination frequencies are stable across all four groups: I{+}L accounts for 13--15\% of paths, M{+}I{+}L for 10--11\%, and S{+}M for 7--8\%. No unexpected interaction emerges. The distribution matches what the per-horizon SHAP decomposition would predict: horizons with higher SHAP weight appear more frequently in tree paths and combine in proportion to their individual importance.

This locates the specific source of nonlinearity that makes XGBoost essential. The model's value does not come from complex momentum-momentum interactions that differ across regimes -- the horizon combinations are regime-invariant. It comes from conditioning the \emph{direction} of selection on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$: the same tree structure that buys intermediate-horizon winners in calm buys intermediate-horizon losers in panic, depending on which branch the regime signal routes each stock through. A linear model assigns a single fixed coefficient to each horizon; XGBoost effectively assigns coefficients whose sign flips with the regime. Table~\ref{tab:combo_long_cp} in Appendix~\ref{app:combo_analysis} quantifies this: the calm-minus-panic z-score difference is positive for all 39 entries in the long leg, with the largest shifts at the intermediate horizon ($+$0.36 to $+$0.55).

\subsubsection{Tree Depth and Feature Interactions}\label{sec:depth_analysis}

Tree depth provides direct evidence for why multi-dimensional conditioning is required (Figure~\ref{fig:depth_sharpe}). Annualised return rises monotonically from depth~1 (8.7\%, no feature interactions) through depth~4 (21.7\%), with the largest gains at depths~2 and~3 where the model first gains multi-way conditioning. Volatility is nearly constant across depths ($\approx$19.5\%): deeper trees improve signal quality rather than amplifying risk-taking. A depth-2 model, which conditions on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ and one momentum horizon per tree, achieves 15.7\% -- 28\% below the full model. Reading the term-structure shape requires comparing multiple horizons simultaneously, which only becomes possible at depth~$\geq 3$. Performance peaks at depth~4 and declines beyond depth~5.

The sub-regime decomposition in Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity_panic} provides a more specific account of the depth requirement. The three panic sub-regimes correspond to three distinct multi-horizon shapes: humped at intermediate horizons (mild panic), weak U-dip in middle horizons (transition), and uniform negativity (deep crisis). Distinguishing these shapes within a $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$-conditional tree branch requires reading at least three momentum horizons. Depth~4 provides the depth budget required to encode three-shape discrimination after a $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$-routing split: depth~1 for the regime gate plus depth~3 for within-regime shape reading.

\subsection{Risk Aversion and Stress Testing}\label{sec:risk_aversion}

\subsubsection{Risk-Aversion Control}

An investor may prefer lower portfolio risk at the cost of some expected return. We incorporate a risk-aversion parameter $\gamma$ into the strategy and trace the out-of-sample trade-off across $\gamma \in [0, 1]$.

The natural starting point is the mean-variance utility $r - \frac{\gamma}{2}\sigma^2$ \citep{Markowitz1952}, but it is the wrong lens for this problem for two reasons. First, mechanically, applying the penalty as a scoring function in a long-short construction produces a betting-against-beta portfolio: the penalty is subtracted symmetrically from every stock's score, which pushes the long book toward low-volatility names and the short book toward high-volatility names. Realised portfolio volatility then rises rather than falls as $\gamma$ grows. Appendix~\ref{app:alt_targets} confirms this pattern across several mean-variance variants. Second, economically, mean-variance penalises variance in \emph{absolute} terms: a stock at $\sigma = 30\%$ is penalised nine times more than one at $\sigma = 10\%$, and the penalty's importance scales with portfolio wealth. Real investors behave as if their risk aversion is \emph{relative} rather than absolute, with a stable tolerance for proportional risk that does not change with wealth level. Constant relative risk aversion captures this; mean-variance does not.

We therefore adopt a CRRA utility that penalises $\log\sigma$ instead of $\sigma^2$, and evaluate each stock's utility separately under a long direction and a short direction. The volatility penalty enters identically in both: a volatile stock is equally unattractive as a holding or as a short position, so the investor's aversion to risk does not flip sign with trading direction. Longs are the top decile of long-utility across the full cross-section; shorts are the top decile of short-utility. The full specification is given in Appendix~\ref{app:crra_construction}.

Figure~\ref{fig:risk_aversion} shows the resulting Pareto curve. At $\gamma = 0.1$, Sharpe stays at 1.01 while maximum drawdown tightens from $-25\%$ to $-18\%$, a near-free reduction in tail risk. At $\gamma = 0.5$, annualised volatility halves from 19.7\% to 11.2\%, drawdown halves to $-12\%$, and Sharpe settles at 0.78. At $\gamma = 1.0$, volatility falls to 5.2\% (roughly a quarter of the market's) and Sharpe remains positive at 0.46. An investor can choose any point on this curve based on their volatility budget or drawdown tolerance. Beyond $\gamma \approx 1.5$, the risk penalty dominates the return signal and the long-short spread collapses into dollar-neutral noise. The framework does not blow up; it simply stops producing a directional bet.

\subsubsection{Stress Test: Prolonged Bear Market}\label{sec:stress_test}

The reversal mechanism documented in Section~\ref{sec:panic} rests on a specific premise: that price declines during panic are temporary (driven by liquidity pressure) rather than permanent (driven by economic deterioration). The historical stress periods in the test sample support this: M2 earned $+29.9\%$ during the COVID crash (February--April 2020) and $+21.5\%$ during the 2022 bear market (January--October 2022). In both cases, the decline was temporary and reversed within months. The panic sub-type decomposition in Section~\ref{sec:panic} corroborates this: the 18 panic months with negative concurrent market returns produced an average loss of $-0.49\%$ per month, while the 41 recovery months generated $+4.42\%$.

But the premise can fail. In a sustained economic deterioration -- a depression or structural decline -- beaten-down stocks fall because their businesses are failing, not because of temporary mispricing. We call this \emph{reversal failure}: the regime classification is correct, but the momentum structure within that regime has changed. To quantify this vulnerability, we simulate recessions of increasing duration, replacing consecutive months with M2's average losing panic-month return ($-$3.44\% per month) at every possible insertion point.

The strategy can absorb substantial downturns. A 12-month recession produces a worst-case drawdown of $-$49\%, after which calm-period continuation resumes. Only a depression-length downturn of 24 months produces a drawdown of $-$69\%. This mirrors the vulnerability identified by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} for traditional momentum, but on the opposite side: standard momentum crashes when winners stop winning; the regime-conditioned strategy would struggle when losers stop reverting. The hybrid approach combining stock selection with exposure scaling (Section~\ref{sec:future_work}) is designed for this contingency. Section~\ref{sec:historical_stress} provides direct historical evidence on how the strategy actually behaved during the dot-com bust and the global financial crisis, complementing this synthetic stress test.

\subsubsection{Historical Stress: Out-of-Sample Evidence}\label{sec:historical_stress}

The simulation in Section~\ref{sec:stress_test} estimates worst-case behaviour under synthetic recessions. To complement it with direct historical evidence, the full pipeline (HMM + XGBoost) is re-estimated annually from 1995 onward, with each year's prediction made by a model trained only on data available through the prior year-end. This produces a 30-year out-of-sample monthly return series spanning 1995--2024 that includes the dot-com bust, the global financial crisis, and the 2020 COVID episode. Methodology details are in Appendix~\ref{app:expanding_window}; the rest of this subsection focuses on how the strategy actually behaved when stress arrived.

\input{tables/table_expanding_subperiods}

\paragraph{Worst observed stress: the dot-com bust}
The 2000--2002 dot-com bust produced the strategy's largest historical drawdown -- $-64.8\%$ peak-to-trough. Restricted to the bear-market proper (March 2000--October 2002), the strategy lost $-43.8\%$ cumulative over 32 months (Sharpe $-0.39$). This is the historical realisation of the reversal-failure vulnerability described in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}: a prolonged sector-rotating bear in which beaten-down stocks did not promptly recover, so the panic-regime reversal selection bled. The strategy survived the episode and resumed positive returns thereafter (the 2003--2006 sub-sample produces Sharpe 0.70), but the realised drawdown is severe.

\paragraph{Stress at the targeted failure mode: the 2009 momentum crash}
The single most informative episode is March--December 2009, the period \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} document as a severe momentum crash. Under the same long-short construction used throughout this thesis (NYSE breakpoints, value-weighted, 10\,bps cost), unconditional 12-month momentum lost $-62.6\%$ cumulative during those ten months. Under expanding-window out-of-sample, the regime-conditional strategy gained $+20.5\%$ cumulative (Sharpe 0.73) during the same window. Across the full GFC episode (October 2007 -- June 2009) the strategy returned $+22.8\%$ cumulative (Sharpe 0.48). This is direct historical evidence that the regime mechanism survives the specific stress event that defeats unconditional momentum.

\paragraph{Synthetic and historical envelopes agree}
The synthetic stress test in Section~\ref{sec:stress_test} estimates a worst-case 24-month synthetic recession at $-70\%$ maximum drawdown. The historical dot-com realisation ($-64.8\%$ drawdown over 32 months) sits inside this envelope: the simulation did not understate the risk. Together the two tests cover both scenarios within the 1995--2024 sample (the real bears observed in US history) and beyond it (synthetic prolonged stress), bounding the strategy's stress profile from both sides.

\paragraph{Long-horizon survival}
Aggregating across all 30 years and all stress events, the strategy produces $+1{,}405\%$ cumulative return with a Sharpe of 0.50. Each historical drawdown is followed by recovery; no stress episode in the sample is unrecoverable. Sub-period samples are small enough that point estimates carry wide confidence intervals, so the qualitative pattern across periods is more informative than the levels.

\subsubsection{Comparison with \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}}\label{sec:dm_comparison}

On the same data and with the same transaction costs, M2 (Sharpe 1.11, CAPM alpha 23.4\%, $t = 4.08$) outperforms the D\&M managed momentum strategy (Sharpe 0.49, CAPM alpha 10.3\%, $t = 1.86$).\footnote{The CAPM alpha is used here rather than the six-factor alpha (24.1\%, $t = 4.81$) reported elsewhere so that M2 and D\&M are compared under the same one-factor specification used throughout the \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} evaluation. The two alphas differ by 0.9 percentage points for M2 because controlling for the momentum factor (UMD) and the FF5 factors leaves the cross-sectional-regime alpha essentially unchanged.} The two approaches use regime information differently: D\&M scale portfolio \emph{exposure} based on predicted crash risk (how much to hold), while M2 changes \emph{which stocks} are held by selecting from the opposite tail of the cross-sectional distribution (what to hold).

A natural extension is to combine the two: use M2's stock selection for portfolio construction and D\&M-style exposure scaling as a circuit breaker. Adding D\&M-style scaling on top of M2 does not improve performance in the current test period, because the condition it guards against -- a sustained reversal failure where beaten-down stocks never recover -- did not occur. Although the test period contains panic streaks of up to 15 consecutive months, M2 never loses more than 3 consecutive panic months. The stress test above (Table~\ref{tab:stress_scenarios}) shows that only a depression-length downturn of 24 months or more would produce drawdowns severe enough to justify exposure scaling. The hybrid approach remains a valuable contingency for deployment in environments where such prolonged deterioration is possible (Section~\ref{sec:future_work}). The sub-regime decomposition in Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity_panic} sharpens the complementarity: D\&M-style scaling would help in deep-crisis episodes, where the long leg sits term-structure-wide negative and the reversal bet is most exposed to failure; in mild-panic months it would unnecessarily reduce positions because the cross-section there resembles calm and the strategy continues to earn a calm-like Sharpe.
