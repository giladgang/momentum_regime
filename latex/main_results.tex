\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

The primary objective of this thesis is not to maximise out-of-sample returns but to understand how regime information interacts with cross-sectional momentum. Specifically, it asks whether regime information can be used for \emph{stock selection} (changing which stocks are held) rather than \emph{exposure scaling} (changing how much of a fixed portfolio to hold), and whether this alternative mechanism requires nonlinear modelling. Portfolio performance is the vehicle for this analysis, not an end in itself.

All strategies are evaluated out-of-sample on the test period (January 2011 to November 2025, 167 months). Each method produces a long-short portfolio: the top decile of stocks (the \emph{long side}) is bought, and the bottom decile (the \emph{short side}) is sold. Both sides are value-weighted using NYSE breakpoints, rebalanced monthly with one-way transaction costs of 10 basis points. Long-short construction is the standard in the momentum literature \citep{JegadeeshTitman1993, DanielMoskowitz2016, BarrosoSantaClara2015} because it isolates the momentum anomaly from market beta, providing a cleaner test of stock selection skill.

The analysis proceeds in five stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Section~\ref{sec:performance_results} compares the overall performance of all strategies, establishing that only the nonlinear model (M2) survives long-short construction.
    \item Section~\ref{sec:regime_results} decomposes performance by market regime, explains the economic mechanisms at work, examines portfolio characteristics, stress-tests the strategy's vulnerability, and provides model-free confirmation via information coefficient analysis.
    \item Section~\ref{sec:shap_results} analyses SHAP feature importance and the partial dependence structure of the regime signal.
    \item Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results} presents ablation studies, including the role of fundamentals and the comparison with \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}.
    \item Section~\ref{sec:risk_aversion} examines how risk aversion affects the regime signal's importance.
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{Portfolio Performance}\label{sec:performance_results}

Table~\ref{tab:performance} reports the out-of-sample performance of all strategies.

\input{tables/table_performance}

The central finding is stark: in long-short construction, only M2 (XGBoost) produces economically meaningful risk-adjusted returns. M2 achieves an annualised Sharpe ratio of 1.08 with returns of 20.6\%, volatility of 19.1\%, and a maximum drawdown of $-$22.5\%. All other strategies collapse. M1 (logistic regression) achieves a Sharpe of only $-$0.03 with the same features, despite having identical input information. Fixed 12-month momentum is effectively dead (Sharpe 0.06, maximum drawdown $-$72.3\%), confirming the momentum crash phenomenon documented by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}.

The collapse of M1 is the first piece of evidence for the thesis's central claim: the regime-momentum interaction is inherently nonlinear. A linear model assigns $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ a single coefficient that cannot capture the conditional logic required to adapt both the long and short sides across regimes. XGBoost's tree splits learn regime-conditional rules that are invisible to a linear specification (even with polynomial and interaction augmentations; see Appendix~\ref{app:m1_poly}).

Factor model regressions confirm that M2's returns are not explained by known risk factors. The CAPM alpha is 23.8\% annualised ($t = 4.08$), and the alpha remains significant across all standard specifications: Fama--French three-factor (23.7\%, $t = 4.46$), Carhart four-factor (24.9\%, $t = 4.74$), Fama--French five-factor (23.4\%, $t = 4.56$), and the six-factor model including momentum (24.7\%, $t = 4.78$). The alpha \emph{increases} as more factors are added, because M2 has near-zero loadings on all factors: the strategy is approximately market-neutral and its returns are not repackaged exposure to value, size, profitability, investment, or momentum factors.

Removing the regime signal drops M2's Sharpe from 1.08 to 0.48 (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results}), confirming that the HMM captures decision-relevant information not spanned by the momentum features themselves.

A spanning test formalises the comparison between M1 and M2. Regressing M2's monthly returns on M1's returns yields an alpha of 21.8\% ($t = 4.27$, $p < 0.001$), confirming that M2 captures information that M1 cannot. The reverse regression yields an insignificant alpha ($t = -0.32$), indicating that M1 adds nothing beyond what M2 already provides.

\subsection{Regime-Conditional Performance and Economic Mechanism}\label{sec:regime_results}

Decomposing performance by market regime reveals why M2 succeeds where other strategies fail (Table~\ref{tab:regime_sharpe}).

\input{tables/table_regime_sharpe}

M2 achieves a Sharpe ratio of 1.50 during panic months compared to 0.80 during calm months. This panic-period dominance contrasts sharply with the long-only literature, where calm-period improvement typically drives overall performance. The difference arises because long-short construction exposes a dimension of skill that long-only construction masks: the ability to correctly position the short side.

\subsubsection{Calm Months: Momentum Continuation}\label{sec:calm}

In calm markets, the standard momentum continuation effect documented by \citet{JegadeeshTitman1993} dominates: past winners continue to outperform past losers. The economic mechanism is well understood: firm-specific good news -- strong earnings, positive analyst revisions, improving fundamentals -- diffuses gradually through the market as investors update their beliefs with a delay \citep{Hong1999, Barberis1998}. A stock that has risen over the past 6--12 months is likely still in the process of being re-priced upward, and the reverse holds for declining stocks. M2 exploits this by buying stocks riding established upward trends and shorting stocks in sustained decline. The resulting long-short spread of 1.33\% per month is modest but consistent. Because the portfolio is approximately market-neutral ($\beta \approx 0$), this spread is not driven by broad market movements -- it reflects M2's ability to rank stocks correctly within each cross-section.

\subsubsection{Panic Months: Short-Term Reversal}\label{sec:panic}

In panic months, M2 achieves a Sharpe of 1.50 because it correctly adapts to a fundamentally different market environment.

The economic intuition for the reversal rests on three reinforcing mechanisms. First, \emph{liquidity spirals} \citep{BrunnermeierPedersen2009}: during market stress, leveraged investors (hedge funds, proprietary desks) face margin calls and are forced to liquidate positions regardless of fundamental value. This forced selling concentrates in the most beaten-down names, pushing their prices temporarily below intrinsic value. When the selling pressure subsides, these stocks snap back. Second, \emph{institutional flow pressure} \citep{VayanosWoolley2013}: mutual funds experiencing redemptions during downturns sell their holdings, creating predictable downward price pressure that reverses once outflows stabilise. Third, \emph{investor overreaction} \citep{DeBondtThaler1985}: panic selling reflects fear-driven extrapolation of recent losses, causing prices to overshoot on the downside.

On the other side of the trade, stocks that held up during the decline -- typically large, low-beta, ``defensive'' names -- become temporarily overvalued as investors crowd into perceived safe havens. When the panic subsides and risk appetite returns, these defensive stocks underperform as capital rotates back into beaten-down, higher-beta names.

M2 learns this regime-specific pattern through its tree splits: it buys beaten-down stocks that are likely to snap back and shorts the defensive names that are likely to lag during recovery. The stocks M2 buys earn 4.46\% per month on average, while those it shorts earn only 1.38\%, producing a long-short spread of 3.09\%. Crucially, this spread does not come from market exposure (the portfolio has near-zero beta) but from M2 correctly distinguishing which stocks will recover and which will lag.

\subsubsection{Portfolio Characteristics by Regime}\label{sec:port_chars}

The stock-level characteristics of M2's portfolio confirm the continuation-vs-reversal mechanism. In calm months, M2's long side holds stocks with positive trailing momentum (+31.3\% over 12 months), moderate size (median market cap \$291M), and relatively low book-to-market ratios (0.70). These are momentum continuation candidates riding established trends. The short side holds similar stocks with slightly higher momentum (+32.6\%), reflecting the compressed cross-sectional spread in calm markets.

In panic months, the profile inverts. The long side holds beaten-down stocks with negative 12-month momentum ($-$5.3\%) and negative 1-month momentum ($-$1.4\%), smaller size (median \$218M), and higher book-to-market (1.00). The short side holds larger stocks (median \$629M) that have held up during the decline, with positive 1-month momentum (+5.1\%). These are the ``defensive'' names that the strategy bets against during recoveries.

\subsubsection{Holding Period Analysis}\label{sec:holding}

The reversal mechanism has a clear time structure. At the 1-month horizon, panic reversal bets are profitable: the long-short spread is +0.60\%. However, at 3 months the spread turns sharply negative ($-$4.85\%), and at 6 months it is $-$5.70\%. The 1-month time scale is consistent with the institutional mechanics of forced selling: margin calls are typically settled within days to weeks, mutual fund redemptions are processed within 30 days, and portfolio rebalancing by risk-managed funds occurs at month-end. Once this selling pressure subsides, prices revert. If the reversal instead reflected fundamental mispricing, it would persist over longer horizons. Monthly rebalancing is therefore essential: holding the panic portfolio for longer than one month would transform a profitable strategy into a losing one.

\subsubsection{Stress Test: What If Reversal Stops Working?}\label{sec:stress_test}

During panic months, M2 earns a long-short spread of 3.09\% per month by buying stocks that recently fell and shorting stocks that recently held up. This spread -- the \emph{reversal premium} -- arises because temporary selling pressure (margin calls, fund redemptions) pushes prices below fundamental value, and they snap back once the pressure subsides.

But the reversal premium depends on a critical assumption: that the price declines are temporary, not permanent. \emph{Reversal failure} occurs when beaten-down stocks do not recover -- because the underlying companies are genuinely deteriorating, not merely suffering from liquidity-driven mispricing. In a prolonged fundamental deterioration -- a depression, a banking crisis where firms actually fail, or a structural decline like Japan's lost decades -- buying beaten-down stocks is catching a falling knife. The stocks M2 buys keep falling, the ``defensive'' stocks M2 shorts hold their value, and the long-short spread turns negative.

This is the mirror-image vulnerability of the thesis's central finding: standard momentum fails when continuation stops working \citep{DanielMoskowitz2016}; the regime-conditioned strategy fails when reversal stops working. To quantify this risk, four scenarios are simulated by progressively degrading the reversal premium during panic months.

The degradation is gradual, not catastrophic. If the reversal premium simply disappears (row 2), the strategy still earns a Sharpe of 0.65 from calm-period momentum continuation alone. Even a 50\% reversal failure (row 3) leaves the strategy marginally profitable. Only a \emph{full inversion} -- where every reversal bet loses -- breaks the strategy entirely. This is the mirror image of the traditional momentum crash: standard momentum fails when continuation stops working \citep{DanielMoskowitz2016}; the regime-conditioned strategy fails when reversal stops working.

A bootstrap simulation of a 12-month bear window in which all reversal bets fail produces a median Sharpe of 0.85 (5th percentile: 0.59) with a median maximum drawdown of $-$47.9\% (worst 5th percentile: $-$64.1\%). The strategy survives even severe stress, but the drawdown would be painful for most investors.

Importantly, the historical stress periods in the test sample \emph{confirm} the reversal mechanism rather than challenging it: M2 earned +30.3\% during the COVID crash (February--April 2020) and +14.9\% during the 2022 bear market (January--October 2022). The scenario that would break the strategy -- a sustained, fundamental-driven decline where beaten-down stocks never recover -- has not occurred in the 2011--2025 test period. This is both a strength (the strategy has not been tested in its worst-case scenario) and a limitation (its resilience to such scenarios remains unverified). The hybrid approach with D\&M-style exposure scaling (Section~\ref{sec:future_work}) is designed precisely for this contingency.

\subsubsection{Model-Free Confirmation: Information Coefficient Rotation}\label{sec:ic_analysis}

A natural concern is whether the regime-dependent behaviour described above reflects genuine economic structure or model-specific overfitting. To rule this out, Table~\ref{tab:ic_rotation} examines the raw predictive power of each momentum horizon in calm versus panic months, without involving any model. The information coefficient (IC) -- the Spearman rank correlation between a momentum signal and subsequent stock returns, computed cross-sectionally each month -- directly measures whether ranking stocks by a given horizon correctly identifies future outperformers.

\input{tables/table_ic_rotation}

The pattern is striking. In calm months, all 12 horizons have positive, significant ICs ($t > 2.5$), with the strongest power at intermediate horizons ($mom_8$ through $mom_{10}$, IC $\approx 0.03$): past winners keep winning. In panic months, the pattern inverts -- short-horizon signals ($mom_1$ through $mom_3$) develop \emph{negative} ICs ($-$0.012 to $-$0.010), meaning that stocks which dropped most recently are more likely to bounce back than to keep falling. Longer horizons retain weakly positive ICs but none is individually significant ($t < 1.6$), reflecting the limited sample of 59 panic months. The key finding is the systematic \emph{contrast}: all 12 horizons are significant in calm, none in panic, and the short horizons flip sign. This confirms that the continuation-to-reversal rotation M2 exploits is a real feature of the data, not an artefact of the model.

\paragraph{Why other strategies fail in panic}
Other strategies cannot adapt to this regime shift. Fixed momentum holds past winners long and past losers short -- which is catastrophic when momentum reverses during market rebounds, precisely the ``momentum crash'' documented by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}. When the market recovers from a panic, past losers (high-beta beaten-down stocks) rally sharply while past winners (defensive low-beta stocks) lag. A fixed momentum strategy is short the stocks that rally most and long the stocks that lag, producing severe losses. M1 (logistic regression) assigns fixed linear weights to each momentum horizon, so it cannot switch from continuation to reversal signals as the regime changes. This failure confirms that the regime-momentum interaction is inherently nonlinear: exploiting it requires a model that can learn conditional rules such as ``in panic, buy recent losers and short recent winners; in calm, do the opposite.''

\subsection{Ablation Studies}\label{sec:ablation_results}

\paragraph{Regime signal alternatives}
Table~\ref{tab:regime_signal_ablation} compares XGBoost performance under three regime signal specifications: the baseline HMM $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, the GHM market-cycle variable \citep{GHM2023}, and no regime signal. The HMM signal produces the highest Sharpe ratio, substantially exceeding both the GHM cycle and the no-signal baseline. The GHM cycle, constructed from the same underlying market returns as the momentum features, provides no incremental information for a nonlinear model.

\input{tables/table_regime_signal_ablation}

\paragraph{HMM feature ablation}
The include-one and leave-one-out experiments (Table~\ref{tab:hmm_feature_ablation}) isolate each HMM feature's contribution. Market drawdown (DD) alone achieves the highest standalone Sharpe, confirming its role as the dominant stress indicator. In the leave-one-out analysis, removing DD or REL\_N causes the largest performance drops, while DISP and CS each contribute marginally. Despite their small marginal contributions, DISP and CS reduce the standard deviation of the Sharpe ratio across independent seed partitions from 0.12 (DD alone) to 0.08 (full four-feature model), because they provide independent information from different market segments -- cross-sectional disagreement (DISP) and corporate credit conditions (CS) -- that smooths the regime classification and reduces sensitivity to MCMC initialization.

\input{tables/table_hmm_feature_ablation}

\paragraph{The role of fundamental features}
Adding seven fundamental characteristics\footnote{Book-to-market, return on equity, gross profitability, leverage, asset growth, earnings growth, and log market equity. Full definitions are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:fundamental_features}.} to the mom+$\pi$ specification reduces the Sharpe from 1.08 to 0.98. Returns fall from 20.6\% to 18.6\%, and volatility compresses slightly from 19.1\% to 19.3\%. The CAPM alpha drops from 23.8\% to 20.1\% ($t = 3.38$), but remains highly significant. This pattern indicates that fundamentals act as a quality screen -- they steer M2 toward more stable stocks -- rather than providing an independent source of regime-conditional alpha. The regime-momentum interaction remains the primary driver of long-short performance regardless of whether fundamentals are included.

\paragraph{Comparison with \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}}
The D\&M managed momentum strategy, which scales exposure to a fixed WML portfolio based on a binary bear indicator, achieves a Sharpe ratio of 0.49 with a CAPM alpha of 10.3\% ($t = 1.86$) on the same data and with the same transaction costs. M2's alpha of 23.8\% ($t = 4.08$) is both larger and more statistically significant. The two approaches exploit the same type of regime information through fundamentally different channels: D\&M adjust \emph{how much} of a fixed portfolio to hold (exposure scaling), while M2 adjusts \emph{which stocks} to hold (stock selection). Within the test period, adding D\&M-style exposure scaling on top of M2's stock selection does not improve performance, suggesting that M2 already internalises the regime information that D\&M uses for scaling. However, the two mechanisms address different vulnerabilities: M2's stock selection exploits short-lived reversal premia that dissipate beyond one month (Section~\ref{sec:holding}), making it vulnerable to prolonged bear markets without recovery. D\&M's exposure scaling, by contrast, is specifically designed to reduce position size during sustained downturns. A hybrid approach that combines M2's stock selection with an exposure-scaling circuit breaker for prolonged stress is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:future_work}.


\subsection{Risk Aversion and the Value of the Regime Signal}\label{sec:risk_aversion}

The preceding analysis established that the regime signal is essential for long-short momentum. A natural question is whether this finding depends on the investor's risk preferences. XGBoost is retrained with a mean-variance utility target that penalises stock-level variance:
\begin{equation}
    y_t^s = r_{t+1}^s - \frac{\gamma}{2}\,\sigma_s^2,
\end{equation}
where $\gamma \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots, 10\}$ is the risk-aversion parameter. At $\gamma = 0$ this recovers the baseline model; as $\gamma$ increases, the model favours lower-volatility stocks.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{risk_aversion_analysis.png}
    \caption{Effect of risk aversion on portfolio performance and feature importance. As $\gamma$ increases, annualised returns and volatility decline, and the regime signal's relative SHAP importance decreases. The Sharpe ratio falls from 1.14 at $\gamma = 0$ to 0.95 at $\gamma = 10$.}
    \label{fig:risk_aversion}
\end{figure}

The results show that the regime signal's relative SHAP importance declines with risk aversion. At $\gamma = 0$, $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ is among the most important features. At high $\gamma$, the model defaults to a momentum-dominated strategy that relies less on regime-conditional reweighting. The Sharpe ratio declines from 1.14 at $\gamma = 0$ to 0.95 at $\gamma = 10$, confirming that the regime signal is most valuable when the investor is willing to bear risk, because it enables the aggressive, regime-conditional switching between momentum horizons that drives M2's performance.
