\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

All strategies are evaluated out-of-sample on 2011--2025 (167 months), using the long-short construction described in Section~\ref{sec:stage2}. The central question is whether momentum's cross-sectional structure is regime-dependent, and whether exploiting this dependence requires nonlinear modelling. The analysis proceeds from aggregate performance (Section~\ref{sec:performance_results}), to the regime-momentum interaction (Section~\ref{sec:regime_results}), nonlinearity and feature interactions (Section~\ref{sec:nonlinearity}), and finally risk aversion and stress testing (Section~\ref{sec:risk_aversion}).


\subsection{Portfolio Performance}\label{sec:performance_results}

\subsubsection{Strategy Comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:performance} reports the out-of-sample performance of all strategies.\footnote{All Sharpe ratios are computed using raw returns rather than excess returns above the risk-free rate. Since the same convention is applied to every strategy, relative comparisons are unaffected. The risk-free rate averaged roughly 2\% annualised over the 2011--2025 window, so absolute Sharpe ratios are overstated by approximately 0.05--0.10.}

\input{tables/table_performance}

The central result is that momentum's cross-sectional predictability is regime-dependent, and that exploiting this dependence requires both a probabilistic regime signal and a nonlinear model. XGBoost combined with the HMM filtered probability (M2, mom+$\pi$) achieves an annualised Sharpe of 1.11 with a six-factor alpha of 24.7\% ($t = 4.78$; see Appendix~\ref{app:factor_alphas} for the full factor model regressions). Every other specification in the table fails to produce economically meaningful long-short performance, and the pattern of failures is informative.

Unconditional momentum fails: fixed 12-month momentum achieves a Sharpe of 0.06 with a maximum drawdown of $-$72.3\%, consistent with the vulnerability of unconditional momentum to crash risk \citep{DanielMoskowitz2016} and time-varying volatility \citep{BarrosoSantaClara2015}. Conditioning on the regime helps directionally -- M0, which switches the lookback horizon based on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, improves to 0.11 -- but crude switching is not enough. The logistic regression (M1), which receives identical features to M2, collapses to a Sharpe of $-$0.03. Augmenting M1 with hand-engineered nonlinear terms ($\pi^2$ and $\pi \times mom_k$ for each horizon $k$) improves it to 0.32 -- confirming that the regime-momentum interaction exists -- but this still captures less than a third of XGBoost's performance, which discovers richer multi-way interactions automatically. Section~\ref{sec:regime_results} examines why.

\subsubsection{Regime Signal Ablation}

The regime signal is essential: removing $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ entirely and retraining on the 12 momentum features alone drops the Sharpe to 0.41 (Table~\ref{tab:regime_signal_ablation}). Without the regime signal, the model cannot distinguish calm from panic and applies the same momentum selection logic regardless of market conditions. Replacing $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ with the four raw stress indicators (DD, DISP, REL\_N, CS) performs even worse (Sharpe 0.30): with four additional dimensions the model must discover the regime structure from scratch at each split, while the HMM distils the indicators into a single dimension that XGBoost can condition on with a single tree split. This result validates the two-stage architecture: the HMM's role is not merely to provide a convenient feature, but to solve the regime identification problem \emph{before} stock selection, so that the cross-sectional model can focus its capacity entirely on using the regime signal rather than constructing it.

\subsection{The Regime-Momentum Interaction}\label{sec:regime_results}

This section examines how M2 adapts its use of momentum across regimes. We decompose performance by regime, then use SHAP values and cross-sectional z-scores to analyse the model's selection at each momentum horizon. The model recovers known patterns from the momentum literature in calm and discovers a term-structure-wide reversal mechanism in panic. Section~\ref{sec:nonlinearity} then verifies these findings at the tree-structure level.

Decomposing performance by market regime reveals how M2 uses momentum differently in calm and panic (Table~\ref{tab:regime_sharpe}). M2 achieves a Sharpe ratio of 1.56 during panic months compared to 0.82 during calm months. The panic-period Sharpe is nearly double the calm-period Sharpe, indicating that the regime signal adds the most value during stress -- precisely when the model switches from continuation to reversal. This is the opposite of how prior approaches handle market instability: where exposure-scaling methods reduce positions during stress to limit losses, M2 leans into panic by reconstructing its portfolio to exploit the reversal (Section~\ref{sec:dm_comparison} develops this contrast).

\input{tables/table_regime_sharpe}

To open the black box of M2's regime-conditional decisions, we decompose its predictions using two complementary tools: SHAP values \citep{Lundberg2017}, which measure how much each horizon contributes to the prediction (the formal definition and methodology are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:shap}), and cross-sectional z-scores, which measure where in the distribution the model selects at each horizon.

Table~\ref{tab:shap} reports aggregate feature importance: the regime signal $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ accounts for 45\% of total importance, comparable to the combined contribution of all 12 momentum horizons. The split between calm and panic is informative: momentum's share rises from 51\% in calm to 60\% in panic, while $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$'s share falls from 49\% to 40\%. The model relies more heavily on momentum signals during stress -- but as the analysis below reveals, it uses them in a fundamentally different way.

\input{tables/table_shap}

Figure~\ref{fig:term_structure} decomposes the aggregate SHAP importance into individual momentum horizons, split by regime and portfolio leg. The top panels plot the average cross-sectional z-score of stocks in the long and short legs at each lookback horizon from 1 to 12 months: a positive z-score means the selected stocks have higher momentum than the cross-sectional average at that horizon, and vice versa. Shaded bands show $\pm 1$ standard error. The bottom panels show each horizon's share of the model's total momentum SHAP contribution. Calm (left) and panic (right) are shown side by side.

An important distinction is needed for interpreting the figure. Z-scores describe the \emph{characteristics} of selected stocks -- where they fall in the cross-sectional momentum distribution at each horizon. SHAP measures something different: how much each horizon \emph{contributes to the model's prediction}. The two need not peak at the same horizon, and they do not: in calm, z-scores peak at month~8, while SHAP peaks at months 11--12. The subsections below analyse each measure separately before drawing joint conclusions.

\subsubsection{Calm: Momentum Continuation}\label{sec:calm}

\paragraph{Z-scores: characteristics of selected stocks}
The z-score curve for the calm long leg is humped: near zero at month~1, peaking at $+0.39$ at month~8, declining to $+0.17$ at month~12. The short leg mirrors this with negative z-scores, deepest at months 8--10 ($-0.27$ to $-0.30$). The long-short spread is 1.33\% per month.

However, $+0.39$ is well above average but far from the upper tail of the cross-sectional distribution. The model does not select the stocks with the highest momentum, but rather stocks at a moderate level -- a departure from traditional momentum strategies that always assign the highest-momentum stocks to the long leg (Section~\ref{sec:novelty}).

One possible interpretation is that the z-score peak at month~8 reflects where the momentum signal is clearest: the cross-sectional separation between the long and short legs is widest there (spread of 0.68, compared to 0.55 at month~11 and near zero at month~1), meaning the stocks the model is most confident about are most clearly differentiated by their intermediate-horizon momentum. However, this remains an assumption. A detailed analysis of the z-score behaviour across both regimes -- why it peaks at month~8 in calm, why it flattens uniformly in panic, and what these shapes reveal about the underlying return dynamics of selected stocks -- would require further investigation beyond the scope of this thesis.

\paragraph{SHAP: where the model discriminates}
Month~1 carries negligible SHAP (around 5\%), indicating the model largely ignores the most recent month's return when forming predictions, consistent with the short-term reversal documented by \citet{Jegadeesh1990} and the standard practice of skipping the most recent month in momentum construction \citep{JegadeeshTitman1993}.

Months 11 and 12 are the most influential individual horizons (18.5\% and 18.0\% of long-leg SHAP), with months 8--12 together accounting for roughly 67\%, consistent with \citet{NovyMarx2012}'s finding that intermediate-to-long-horizon returns are the primary driver of momentum profits. Where the z-score peak at month~8 indicates where the momentum signal is clearest (largest separation between long and short), the SHAP peak at months 11--12 indicates where the signal is strongest (most influential for the model's predictions). Section~\ref{sec:nonlinearity} examines how the model uses these horizons jointly through tree path analysis.

\paragraph{Model validation}
None of the patterns described above were imposed. The model receives 12 undifferentiated momentum horizons and independently recovers the skip-month convention \citep{Jegadeesh1990, JegadeeshTitman1993} and the concentration of predictive power at intermediate-to-long horizons \citep{NovyMarx2012} from the data alone. That the model arrives at these known results without guidance provides confidence that it captures genuine economic patterns rather than overfitting to noise.

\subsubsection{Panic: Reversal}\label{sec:panic}

\paragraph{Z-scores: characteristics of selected stocks}
In panic, the humped curve flattens. The long leg's z-score drops to $-0.09$ to $-0.19$ across all horizons, and the portfolio confirms this with $-19.5\%$ trailing 12-month momentum and $-5.2\%$ 1-month momentum. As in calm, the model does not select the most extreme stocks: the long leg holds \emph{moderately} below-average momentum stocks, not the deepest losers in the cross-section. The uniform depression across all horizons is consistent with the price impact of non-discretionary selling -- \emph{liquidity spirals} \citep{BrunnermeierPedersen2009} and \emph{institutional flow pressure} \citep{VayanosWoolley2013} force sales regardless of horizon-specific information, depressing returns at every lookback simultaneously. The mispricing persists because arbitrage capital is itself constrained during crises \citep{ShleiferVishny1997}, allowing the dislocation to remain exploitable at monthly frequency.

The short leg shows a distinct asymmetry at month~1: its z-score spikes to $+0.30$ while the long leg's is $-0.19$, and the short side holds stocks with positive 1-month momentum ($+3.7\%$). Beyond month~1, the short leg's z-scores turn negative from month~6 onward. The model shorts stocks that experienced a recent short-term recovery but have been underperforming over longer horizons. This pattern is consistent with temporary price recoveries during stress that are not supported by fundamentals: short-covering rallies and sentiment-driven buying can produce brief gains in individual stocks \citep{DeBondtThaler1985}, but these reversals tend to fade when the underlying selling pressure resumes, making them profitable short targets.

The resulting long-short spread in panic is 3.09\% per month -- more than double the calm-period spread of 1.33\% -- and comes entirely from cross-sectional stock selection (near-zero market beta). As in the calm regime, the precise z-score patterns -- why the long leg is uniformly negative rather than concentrated at specific horizons, and why the short leg exhibits a sharp asymmetry at month~1 -- warrant further investigation beyond the scope of this thesis.

\paragraph{SHAP: where the model discriminates}
The SHAP distribution shifts modestly toward shorter horizons compared to calm, but months 7--12 still dominate (accounting for roughly 63\% of the long leg's momentum SHAP). Month~1 SHAP rises notably on the short side (10.1\%, compared to 5.0\% in calm), indicating that the model's predictions become more sensitive to recent returns when identifying stocks to short during stress. This is consistent with \citet{Nagel2012}, who shows that returns to short-term reversal spike during crises when liquidity provision is most valuable.

\subsubsection{Discussion}\label{sec:novelty}

Prior work has established that momentum profits depend on market state \citep{Cooper2004}, that momentum crashes are tied to rebounds after stress \citep{DanielMoskowitz2016}, and that blending fast and slow momentum signals across market cycles can improve performance \citep{GHM2023}. The analysis above contributes three findings that go beyond these results, ordered from strongest to most incremental.

\paragraph{The panic long leg is a term-structure-wide loser portfolio}
The strongest empirical contribution is descriptive. In panic, the model's long leg is not merely tilted away from winners -- it is below the cross-sectional average at \emph{every} horizon from 1 to 12 months. This flat, negative z-score profile (Figure~\ref{fig:term_structure}) is qualitatively different from short-term reversal \citep{Jegadeesh1990, Nagel2012}, which targets month~1 only, from long-term contrarian strategies \citep{DeBondtThaler1985}, which operate at multi-year horizons, and from state-dependent strategies that switch between two momentum signals. A stock that is uniformly below average across the entire term structure is more likely a victim of indiscriminate forced selling than of genuine fundamental decline, which would typically appear first at short horizons and propagate outward. In calm, the model recovers the known intermediate-horizon continuation shape \citep{NovyMarx2012}. The contrast between these two profiles -- humped in calm, flat-negative in panic -- is a specific empirical fact that, to the best of our knowledge, has not been documented in this form.

\paragraph{The regime signal reorganises the full momentum term structure}
The second contribution is structural. The regime does not simply flip one horizon from continuation to reversal or shift weight between a fast and a slow signal. It alters how the model uses the \emph{entire} 12-horizon term structure: buying from the upper tail at intermediate horizons in calm and from the lower tail at all horizons in panic. The SHAP distribution remains broadly stable across regimes (Table~\ref{tab:zscore_shap_detail}), confirming that the model attends to the same horizons in both states. What changes is not which horizons matter, but the direction of selection at those horizons.

\paragraph{Selection by momentum level, not momentum rank}
A subtler departure from standard practice is that the model does not simply select stocks with the highest or lowest raw momentum. Traditional momentum strategies rank stocks directly by their trailing return at a fixed lookback and always assign the highest-momentum stocks to the long leg \citep{JegadeeshTitman1993}. Here, the portfolio is constructed by ranking on \emph{predicted forward return} -- a learned function of all 12 momentum horizons and the regime signal. The model therefore learns which momentum \emph{level} is associated with future outperformance, not just which stocks have the most extreme momentum. In calm, this distinction is modest: the long leg's z-scores peak at $+0.39$ (month~8), well above average but far from the distribution's upper tail. In panic, the distinction becomes decisive: the long leg selects stocks that are \emph{below} the cross-sectional average at every horizon, a selection that no fixed-rank momentum strategy would produce. Whether this flexibility is a genuine empirical feature of the data or partly an artefact of XGBoost's function approximation requires further investigation -- for example, by testing whether alternative nonlinear models recover the same momentum-level selection patterns, or by examining whether the selected momentum levels correspond to economically distinct groups of stocks (e.g., forced-selling victims vs.\ fundamental decliners).

\paragraph{The role of nonlinearity}
The third contribution concerns the modelling requirement. That nonlinear models outperform linear ones in cross-sectional asset pricing is well established \citep{Gu2020}. What is more specific here is \emph{where} the nonlinearity matters: in transforming a macrofinancial regime probability into a conditioning variable for multi-horizon momentum selection. Section~\ref{sec:nonlinearity} provides structural evidence that the nonlinearity is not generic feature interaction but specifically the ability of $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ to act as a context switch.

\subsection{Nonlinearity and Feature Interactions}\label{sec:nonlinearity}

\subsubsection{Tree Path Analysis}\label{sec:tree_paths}

The z-score and SHAP analysis in Section~\ref{sec:regime_results} treats each horizon independently. A natural question is whether the model relies on specific \emph{interactions} between horizons that the per-horizon decomposition would miss.

To test this, we trace 5{,}000 sampled stocks from each regime$\times$leg group through 50{,}000 individual trees, recording which momentum horizon groups each decision path splits on (see Appendix~\ref{app:combo_analysis} for details). Horizons are grouped into four bands: short (S, months 1--3), medium (M, months 4--6), intermediate (I, months 7--9), and long (L, months 10--12). Table~\ref{tab:combo_freq} reports the frequency of each of the 15 possible combinations, where each column sums to 100\%.

\input{tables/table_combo_freq}

The combination frequencies are remarkably stable across all four groups: I{+}L accounts for 13--15\% of paths everywhere, M{+}I{+}L for 10--11\%, and S{+}M for 7--8\%. No unexpected interaction emerges. The distribution is broadly consistent with what the per-horizon SHAP decomposition would predict: horizons with higher SHAP weight appear more frequently in tree paths, and they combine in proportion to their individual importance.

This clarifies the specific source of nonlinearity that makes XGBoost essential. The model's value does not come from discovering complex momentum-momentum interactions that differ across regimes -- the horizon combinations are regime-invariant. Rather, it comes from the ability to condition the \emph{direction} of selection on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$: the same tree structure that buys intermediate-horizon winners in calm buys intermediate-horizon losers in panic, depending on which branch the regime signal routes each stock through. A linear model cannot replicate this because it assigns a single fixed coefficient to each horizon; XGBoost effectively assigns coefficients whose sign flips with the regime. Table~\ref{tab:combo_long_cp} in Appendix~\ref{app:combo_analysis} confirms this quantitatively: the calm-minus-panic z-score difference is positive for all 39 entries in the long leg, with the largest shifts at the intermediate horizon ($+$0.36 to $+$0.55).

\subsubsection{Tree Depth and Feature Interactions}\label{sec:depth_analysis}

The tree depth analysis provides direct evidence for why multi-dimensional conditioning is required (Figure~\ref{fig:depth_sharpe}). Since XGBoost is trained to predict returns, we report annualised return by depth. Performance increases monotonically from depth~1 (7.3\%, no feature interactions) through depth~4 (21.9\%), with the largest gains at depths~2 and~3 where the model first gains access to multi-way conditioning. Annualised volatility is nearly constant across depths ($\approx$19.5\%), confirming that deeper trees improve signal quality rather than increasing risk-taking. A depth-2 model, which can only condition on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ and one momentum horizon per tree, achieves 15.0\% -- meaningful, but 31\% below the full model. Reading the term-structure shape requires comparing multiple horizons simultaneously, which is only possible at depth~$\geq 3$. Performance peaks at depth~4 and declines beyond depth~5.

\subsection{Risk Aversion and Stress Testing}\label{sec:risk_aversion}

\subsubsection{Alternative Training Targets}

The preceding analysis established that the regime signal is essential for long-short momentum. A natural question is whether this finding depends on how the model's training objective balances return against risk. To test this, XGBoost is retrained under three families of risk-adjusted targets, each with varying intensity:

\input{tables/table_risk_aversion}

Two findings emerge. First, under mild risk adjustment ($r/\sigma^{0.5}$ or small $\gamma$), the regime signal's share of feature importance actually \emph{increases} (from 45\% to 57\%), while the Sharpe ratio barely changes. Mild risk penalisation removes the noisiest bets but preserves the regime-conditional structure -- the model still adapts its joint use of momentum horizons across regimes, just with less exposure to extreme outcomes.

Second, aggressive risk adjustment breaks the strategy. At $\gamma = 0.5$ the Sharpe drops to 0.75 and the regime signal's share falls to 35\%; at $a \geq 1$ the strategy collapses (Sharpe 0.36). The variance penalty overwhelms the long-short spread, steering the model toward low-volatility stocks regardless of regime and effectively abandoning the mechanism that makes M2 successful.

The implication is that detecting the regime-dependent structure of momentum requires the model to be free to learn flexible, regime-conditional feature interactions. Conservative risk objectives constrain this flexibility, forcing the model toward static low-volatility selections that obscure the very regime-dependent structure the regime signal is designed to capture.

\subsubsection{Stress Test: Prolonged Bear Market}\label{sec:stress_test}

The reversal mechanism documented in Section~\ref{sec:panic} rests on a specific premise: that price declines during panic are temporary (driven by liquidity pressure) rather than permanent (driven by fundamental deterioration). The historical stress periods in the test sample support this: M2 earned $+29.9\%$ during the COVID crash (February--April 2020) and $+21.5\%$ during the 2022 bear market (January--October 2022). In both cases, the decline was temporary and reversed within months.

But the premise can fail. In a sustained economic deterioration -- a depression or structural decline -- beaten-down stocks fall because their businesses are failing, not because of temporary mispricing. We call this \emph{reversal failure}: the regime classification is correct, but the momentum structure within that regime has changed. To quantify this vulnerability, we simulate recessions of increasing duration, replacing consecutive months with M2's average losing panic-month return ($-$3.51\% per month) at every possible insertion point.

\input{tables/table_stress_scenarios}

The strategy can absorb substantial downturns. A 12-month recession produces a worst-case drawdown of $-$51\%, after which calm-period continuation resumes. Only a depression-length downturn of 24 months produces a drawdown of $-$70\%. This mirrors the vulnerability identified by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} for traditional momentum, but on the opposite side: standard momentum crashes when winners stop winning; the regime-conditioned strategy would struggle when losers stop reverting. The hybrid approach combining stock selection with exposure scaling (Section~\ref{sec:future_work}) is designed for this contingency.

\subsubsection{Comparison with \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}}\label{sec:dm_comparison}

On the same data and with the same transaction costs, M2 (Sharpe 1.11, CAPM alpha 23.8\%, $t = 4.08$) outperforms the D\&M managed momentum strategy (Sharpe 0.49, CAPM alpha 10.3\%, $t = 1.86$). The two approaches use regime information differently: D\&M scale portfolio \emph{exposure} based on predicted crash risk (how much to hold), while M2 changes \emph{which stocks} are held by selecting from the opposite tail of the cross-sectional distribution (what to hold).

A natural extension is to combine the two: use M2's stock selection for portfolio construction and D\&M-style exposure scaling as a circuit breaker. Adding D\&M-style scaling on top of M2 does not improve performance in the current test period, because the condition it guards against -- a sustained reversal failure where beaten-down stocks never recover -- did not occur. Although the test period contains panic streaks of up to 15 consecutive months, M2 never loses more than 3 consecutive panic months. The stress test above (Table~\ref{tab:stress_scenarios}) shows that only a depression-length downturn of 24 months or more would produce drawdowns severe enough to justify exposure scaling. The hybrid approach remains a valuable contingency for deployment in environments where such prolonged deterioration is possible (Section~\ref{sec:future_work}).

Taken together, the results provide converging evidence that, in the sample studied, momentum's cross-sectional predictability shifts between continuation and reversal across market regimes, and that exploiting this shift requires nonlinear conditioning on a regime signal. The signal's value lies not in predicting returns directly but in conditioning how the model jointly uses the full momentum term structure -- a multi-dimensional interaction that only tree-based models can learn. The next section summarises these findings and discusses their implications.
