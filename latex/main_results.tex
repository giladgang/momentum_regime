\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

All strategies are evaluated out-of-sample on 2011--2025 (167 months), using the long-short construction described in Section~\ref{sec:stage2}. The central question is whether momentum's cross-sectional structure is regime-dependent, and whether exploiting this dependence requires nonlinear modelling. The analysis proceeds from aggregate performance (Section~\ref{sec:performance_results}), to regime-conditional decomposition (Section~\ref{sec:regime_results}), to the regime-momentum interaction (Section~\ref{sec:shap_results}), ablation studies and benchmarking against \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results}), and finally the effect of risk aversion on the regime signal (Section~\ref{sec:risk_aversion}).


\subsection{Portfolio Performance}\label{sec:performance_results}

Table~\ref{tab:performance} reports the out-of-sample performance of all strategies.\footnote{All Sharpe ratios are computed using raw returns rather than excess returns above the risk-free rate. Since the same convention is applied to every strategy, relative comparisons are unaffected. The risk-free rate averaged roughly 2\% annualised over the 2011--2025 window, so absolute Sharpe ratios are overstated by approximately 0.05--0.10.}

\input{tables/table_performance}

The central result is that momentum's cross-sectional predictability is regime-dependent, and that exploiting this dependence requires both a probabilistic regime signal and a nonlinear model. XGBoost combined with the HMM filtered probability (M2, mom+$\pi$) achieves an annualised Sharpe of 1.11 with a six-factor alpha of 24.7\% ($t = 4.78$). Every other specification in the table fails to produce economically meaningful long-short performance, and the pattern of failures is informative.

Unconditional momentum fails: fixed 12-month momentum achieves a Sharpe of 0.06 with a maximum drawdown of $-$72.3\%, confirming the crash phenomenon documented by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}. Conditioning on the regime helps directionally -- M0, which switches the lookback horizon based on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, improves to 0.11 -- but crude switching is not enough. The logistic regression (M1), which receives identical features to M2, collapses to a Sharpe of $-$0.03, establishing that the regime-momentum interaction cannot be captured by a single set of linear weights. Sections~\ref{sec:shap_results} and~\ref{sec:ablation_results} examine why.

The HMM's compression of the four stress indicators into a single filtered probability is itself essential. Replacing $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ with the four raw indicators (DD, DISP, REL\_N, CS) fed directly to XGBoost reduces the Sharpe from 1.11 to 0.30, worse than removing regime information entirely (Sharpe 0.41 without any regime signal; see Table~\ref{tab:regime_signal_ablation}). The raw features add noise rather than signal: with four additional dimensions the model must discover the regime structure from scratch at each split, while the HMM distils the indicators into a single dimension -- the degree of market stress -- that XGBoost can condition on efficiently.

M2's factor alphas are significant across all standard specifications (Table~\ref{tab:factor_alphas}). The alpha is stable at 23--25\% across CAPM, Fama--French three- and five-factor, Carhart, and six-factor models, with $t$-statistics exceeding 4.0 in every case. Factor loadings are near zero throughout, confirming that M2's returns are not repackaged factor exposure. The Newey--West $t$-statistic for mean returns (1.83) is not individually significant, which is expected for a market-neutral long-short strategy with 19.7\% annualised volatility; the economically meaningful test is the factor alpha.

\subsection{Regime-Conditional Performance and Economic Mechanism}\label{sec:regime_results}

Decomposing performance by market regime reveals why M2 succeeds where other strategies fail (Table~\ref{tab:regime_sharpe}).

\input{tables/table_regime_sharpe}

M2 achieves a Sharpe ratio of 1.56 during panic months compared to 0.82 during calm months. This panic-period dominance contrasts sharply with the long-only literature, where calm-period improvement typically drives overall performance. The difference arises because long-short construction exposes a dimension of skill that long-only construction masks: the ability to correctly position the short side.

\subsubsection{Calm Months: Momentum Continuation}\label{sec:calm}

In calm markets, the standard momentum continuation effect documented by \citet{JegadeeshTitman1993} dominates: past winners continue to outperform past losers. The economic mechanism is well understood: firm-specific good news -- strong earnings, positive analyst revisions, improving fundamentals -- diffuses gradually through the market as investors update their beliefs with a delay \citep{HongStein1999, Barberis1998}. A stock that has risen over the past 6--12 months is likely still in the process of being re-priced upward, and the reverse holds for declining stocks.

M2 exploits this by going long stocks with the strongest upward trends and shorting stocks with weaker momentum. The portfolio characteristics confirm this: M2's long side holds stocks with strong positive trailing momentum (+23.7\% over 12 months), moderate size (median market cap \$327M), and relatively low book-to-market ratios (0.43). The short side holds larger stocks (median \$601M) with weaker positive momentum (+8.3\% over 12 months) -- these are lagging names that have underperformed the long side, consistent with M2 betting on continued relative outperformance of strong-momentum stocks. The resulting long-short spread of 1.33\% per month is modest but consistent. Because the portfolio is approximately market-neutral ($\beta \approx 0$), this spread is not driven by broad market movements -- it reflects M2's ability to rank stocks correctly within each cross-section.

\subsubsection{Panic Months: Reversal}\label{sec:panic}

In panic months, M2 achieves a Sharpe of 1.56 because it correctly identifies a fundamentally different cross-sectional structure. The regime signal enables the model to recognise that the predictive relationship between past returns and future returns has inverted: stocks that have fallen the most -- across both short and long lookback horizons -- are now more likely to outperform, not less.

The economic basis for this inversion rests on three reinforcing mechanisms, each of which is captured by the HMM's stress indicators. First, \emph{liquidity spirals} \citep{BrunnermeierPedersen2009}: during market stress, leveraged investors face margin calls and are forced to liquidate positions regardless of fundamental value, pushing prices temporarily below intrinsic value. When the selling pressure subsides, these stocks snap back. The HMM detects this through drawdown (DD), which measures the depth of the market decline, and the credit spread (CS), which widens as corporate distress and fire-sale risk increase. Second, \emph{institutional flow pressure} \citep{VayanosWoolley2013}: mutual funds experiencing redemptions sell their holdings, creating predictable downward price pressure that reverses once outflows stabilise. Return dispersion (DISP) captures this mechanism, as forced selling fractures the cross-sectional consensus and causes individual stocks to move in divergent directions. Third, \emph{investor overreaction} \citep{DeBondtThaler1985}: panic selling reflects fear-driven extrapolation of recent losses, causing prices to overshoot on the downside. Relative market participation (REL\_N) detects the structural market damage that accompanies severe overreaction episodes -- delistings, trading halts, and elevated acquisition rates.

On the other side, stocks that held up during the decline -- typically large, low-beta, ``defensive'' names -- become temporarily overvalued as investors crowd into perceived safe havens. When risk appetite returns, capital rotates back into beaten-down names.

The portfolio characteristics confirm this inversion. In panic months, M2's long side holds beaten-down stocks with negative 12-month momentum ($-$19.5\%) and negative 1-month momentum ($-$5.2\%), smaller size (median \$238M), and higher book-to-market (0.62). The short side holds larger stocks (median \$855M) that have held up during the decline, with positive 1-month momentum (+3.7\%) -- the ``defensive'' names that the strategy bets against during recoveries.

M2 learns this regime-specific cross-sectional structure through tree splits that condition jointly on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ and the full set of momentum horizons. The model does not simply reverse the slope of any single horizon; rather, it reweights the entire 12-dimensional momentum term structure as a function of the regime signal, an interaction that cannot be decomposed into per-horizon changes (Section~\ref{sec:tree_paths} provides structural evidence for this claim). Figure~\ref{fig:regime_decomp} shows the result: during panic, M2's long side earns 4.46\% per month while its short side earns only 1.38\%, producing a spread of 3.09\% -- more than double the calm-period spread of 1.33\%. This widening reflects the model's improved ability to distinguish future outperformers from underperformers when the regime signal indicates stress. The spread comes entirely from cross-sectional stock selection (the portfolio has near-zero market beta), confirming that the regime signal adds information about the \emph{relative} prospects of individual stocks, not about the market's direction.

\subsubsection{Stress Test: Prolonged Bear Market}\label{sec:stress_test}

The regime signal's value during panic rests on a specific economic premise: that price declines are temporary (driven by liquidity pressure) rather than permanent (driven by fundamental deterioration). When the premise holds, M2 correctly identifies which stocks are temporarily dislocated and which are not, producing a panic-month long-short spread of 3.09\% per month -- evidence that the regime signal captures meaningful cross-sectional information during stress.

But the premise can fail. In a genuine economic deterioration -- a depression, a banking crisis, or a structural decline -- beaten-down stocks fall because their businesses are failing, not because of temporary mispricing. In this scenario, the regime signal still identifies the market as stressed, but the cross-sectional pattern it conditions on (reversal) no longer holds. This is what we call \emph{reversal failure}: the regime classification is correct, but the momentum structure within that regime has changed.

To answer this, we simulate recessions of increasing duration. In each simulation, a block of consecutive months is replaced with the average return M2 earns during its actual losing panic months ($-$3.51\% per month), while all other months retain their observed returns. The simulation is repeated at every possible insertion point to capture the worst-case timing.

The strategy can absorb substantial downturns. Even a 12-month recession -- roughly the length of the 2008--2009 crisis -- produces a worst-case drawdown of $-$51\%, after which the strategy recovers as calm-period continuation resumes. Appending a simulated 6-month recession at the end of the test period (i.e., extending the sample with six months of losses at $-$3.51\% per month) still leaves the overall Sharpe ratio positive, because the preceding 167 months of returns provide a sufficient buffer. Only a depression-length downturn of 24 months produces a drawdown of $-$70\%, which would be painful for any momentum strategy.

The historical stress periods in the test sample confirm the reversal mechanism: M2 earned +29.9\% during the COVID crash (February--April 2020) and +21.5\% during the 2022 bear market (January--October 2022). In both cases, the decline was temporary and reversed within months.

The scenario that would truly break the strategy -- a sustained fundamental deterioration where beaten-down stocks never recover, as in the Great Depression or Japan's lost decades -- has not occurred in the 2011--2025 test period. This mirrors the vulnerability identified by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} for traditional momentum, but on the opposite side: standard momentum crashes when winners stop winning; the regime-conditioned strategy would struggle when losers stop reverting. The hybrid approach combining stock selection with exposure scaling (Section~\ref{sec:future_work}) is designed precisely for this contingency.


\subsection{The Regime-Momentum Interaction}\label{sec:shap_results}

Having established that M2 adapts stock selection across regimes, we now examine which momentum horizons it relies on and how their role changes between calm and panic.

\subsubsection{Feature Importance and the Momentum Term Structure}\label{sec:term_structure}

Two complementary tools are used: SHAP values and cross-sectional z-scores. SHAP \citep[SHapley Additive exPlanations;][]{Lundberg2017} decomposes each stock's predicted return into additive contributions from each feature, measuring how much each horizon \emph{contributes to the prediction} (see Appendix~\ref{app:shap} for the formal definition). Cross-sectional z-scores measure \emph{where in the distribution} the model selects: a z-score of $+0.39$ at month~8 means the selected stocks are 0.39 standard deviations above the cross-sectional average at that horizon. Together, SHAP reveals which horizons drive the model's decisions, while z-scores reveal the direction of selection at each horizon.

Table~\ref{tab:shap} reports aggregate feature importance. The regime signal $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ accounts for 45\% of total importance, comparable to the combined contribution of all 12 momentum horizons. Its importance increases from calm to panic months, confirming that XGBoost relies more heavily on the regime signal when markets are stressed.

Figure~\ref{fig:term_structure} decomposes this further by horizon. The top panels show the z-score of stocks selected for the long and short legs at each lookback; the bottom panels show each horizon's share of total momentum $|$SHAP$|$.

Three layers of interpretation emerge: the empirical patterns themselves, the established findings they recover, and the novel mechanism they reveal.

\paragraph{Calm long leg}
The z-score curve is humped: near zero at month~1, peaking at $+0.39$ at month~8, declining to $+0.17$ at month~12. The model buys stocks with strong intermediate-horizon momentum while ignoring their most recent return. SHAP peaks later (month~11), meaning the model pays most attention where the momentum signal begins to decay -- the horizons where distinguishing continuation from reversal is hardest.

\paragraph{Calm short leg}
The mirror image: z-scores are negative across all horizons, deepest at months 8--10 ($-0.27$ to $-0.30$). SHAP follows the same pattern as the long leg. The model shorts stocks that have underperformed at the same horizons where the long leg selects outperformers.

\paragraph{Panic long leg}
The humped curve flattens to $-0.09$ to $-0.19$ across all horizons. The model buys stocks that are uniformly below average at every lookback -- not targeting a specific horizon but identifying broad-based losers. SHAP shifts modestly toward shorter horizons but months 7--12 still dominate.

\paragraph{Panic short leg}
The z-score spikes to $+0.30$ at month~1 and declines toward zero at longer horizons. The model shorts stocks that rallied recently during the stress episode. SHAP also tilts toward month~1, making this the one panel where both selection and attention shift toward short horizons.

\paragraph{Literature support}
Several features of these patterns connect to established findings. The near-zero z-score and low SHAP weight at month~1 in calm reflect the short-term reversal effect documented by \citet{Jegadeesh1990}: at the 1-month horizon, last month's winners tend to underperform, so the model learns to largely ignore this signal. The peak at months 7--8 aligns with \citet{NovyMarx2012}'s finding that momentum profits are driven primarily by returns from 7 to 12 months prior to portfolio formation, consistent with the gradual diffusion of firm-specific information into prices \citep{HongStein1999}. The decline in z-score after month~8 is consistent with the momentum life cycle of \citet{LeeSwaminathan2000}, where initial underreaction gives way to overreaction, causing the continuation effect to fade at longer horizons. Notably, SHAP weight continues to rise after month~8 (peaking at month~11), suggesting the model pays increasing attention to precisely the horizons where the momentum signal begins to decay -- these are the horizons where distinguishing genuine continuation from incipient reversal is most valuable. The long--short symmetry in calm reflects the standard momentum framework of \citet{JegadeeshTitman1993}, where the same horizons identify both winners and losers, and the spread is widest where continuation is strongest. In panic, the short-horizon crossover at month~1 reflects the flight-to-safety effect: stocks that rallied recently while the broader market declined are temporarily overvalued, and the model shorts them accordingly. The model independently recovers all five patterns from the data.

\paragraph{Novelty}
The central finding is the panic long leg's uniformly negative z-scores. To our knowledge, no prior study has documented that a momentum-based model, when conditioned on regime, learns to \emph{buy losers in panic} across the entire term structure. This pattern differs qualitatively from conventional short-term reversal, which targets a specific horizon (typically month~1). Here, the model selects stocks that have underperformed at \emph{every} lookback from 1 to 12 months. A stock that is below average at every horizon is more likely a victim of indiscriminate selling than of a genuine fundamental decline, which would typically appear first at short horizons and propagate outward. This flat, negative profile is the signature of temporary liquidity-driven dislocation: during stress, forced selling by leveraged investors \citep{BrunnermeierPedersen2009} and fund redemptions \citep{VayanosWoolley2013} push prices below fundamental value across all horizons simultaneously. The model learns to exploit this reversal potential without being explicitly programmed to do so. The regime signal enables this behaviour: without it, a momentum model cannot distinguish between stocks that are falling due to fundamental deterioration and those falling due to transient liquidity shocks.

The relationship between the long and short legs deserves further attention. In both regimes, the short leg's z-score curve approximately mirrors the long leg's -- the model applies symmetric but opposite selection criteria to both sides of the portfolio. Whether this symmetry is an inherent property of cross-sectional momentum or an artefact of the decile-based construction is an open question for future research.

The fact that SHAP horizon weights remain broadly stable across regimes while the z-score direction flips confirms that the regime signal functions as a context switch rather than a feature reweighting mechanism. The one exception is month~1, where SHAP weight increases notably in panic (from 4.8\% to 7.0\% for the long leg). This is consistent with \citet{Nagel2012}, who shows that returns to short-term reversal spike during crises when liquidity provision is most valuable, and with \citet{GHM2023}, who emphasise short-term momentum in stressed market states. However, the model treats month~1 as a supporting signal rather than a primary one: months 8--12 still account for over 60\% of total momentum SHAP even in panic.

This mechanism contrasts with the managed momentum approach of \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}. D\&M showed that momentum crashes are predictable from market volatility and proposed scaling portfolio \emph{exposure} accordingly -- reducing the position size when crash risk is elevated. M2 takes the opposite approach: rather than retreating from the market during stress, it reconstructs the portfolio by selecting from the opposite tail of the cross-sectional distribution. The regime signal does not tell the model to do less; it tells the model to do something fundamentally different.

\paragraph{Summary}
The primary regime effect is not a reallocation of attention across horizons but a reversal of the selection direction. The model picks from the upper tail of momentum in calm and the lower tail in panic, using largely the same horizons and the same SHAP weights. The calm patterns recover five established findings from the momentum literature. The panic patterns reveal a novel mechanism: regime-conditioned buying of uniform losers, a form of term-structure-wide contrarian selection that has not been previously documented.

\subsubsection{Tree Path Analysis}\label{sec:tree_paths}

The SHAP and z-score analysis in Section~\ref{sec:term_structure} treats each horizon independently. A natural question is whether the model relies on specific \emph{interactions} between horizons -- for example, conditioning jointly on month~1 and month~11 -- that the per-horizon decomposition would miss. If such interactions exist, they could reveal structure in the momentum term structure that is not visible from marginal importance alone.

To test this, we trace 5{,}000 sampled stocks from each regime$\times$leg group through 50{,}000 individual trees, recording which momentum horizon groups each decision path splits on (see Appendix~\ref{app:combo_analysis} for details). Horizons are grouped into four bands: short (S, months 1--3), medium (M, months 4--6), intermediate (I, months 7--9), and long (L, months 10--12). With four groups, there are 15 possible non-empty combinations. Table~\ref{tab:combo_freq} reports the frequency of each combination across all four regime$\times$leg groups, where each column sums to 100\%.

\input{tables/table_combo_freq}

The combination frequencies are remarkably stable across all four groups: I{+}L accounts for 13--15\% of paths everywhere, M{+}I{+}L for 10--11\%, and S{+}M for 7--8\%. No single combination dominates, and no unexpected interaction emerges. The model does not, for instance, develop a calm-specific reliance on S{+}L or a panic-specific reliance on M{+}I. The distribution of horizon interactions is broadly consistent with what the per-horizon SHAP decomposition would predict: horizons with higher SHAP weight appear more frequently in tree paths, and they combine with each other roughly in proportion to their individual importance.

This confirms that the regime-momentum interaction operates primarily through the \emph{direction} of selection at each horizon (the z-score flip documented above), not through regime-specific horizon interactions. The model uses the same horizon combinations in both calm and panic; it is the selection direction, not the tree structure, that changes. Table~\ref{tab:combo_long_cp} in Appendix~\ref{app:combo_analysis} reports the calm-minus-panic z-score difference for each combination: all 39 entries for the long leg are positive, meaning the model selects stocks with higher relative momentum in calm and lower in panic at every horizon in every combination, with the largest shifts at the intermediate horizon ($+$0.36 to $+$0.55).

\subsubsection{Tree Depth and Feature Interactions}\label{sec:depth_analysis}

The tree depth analysis provides direct evidence for why multi-dimensional conditioning is required (Figure~\ref{fig:depth_sharpe}). Performance increases monotonically from depth~1 (Sharpe~0.46, no feature interactions) through depth~4 (Sharpe~1.11), with the largest gains at depths~2 and~3 where the model first gains access to multi-way conditioning. A depth-2 model, which can only condition on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ and one momentum horizon per tree, achieves a Sharpe of only~0.82 -- meaningful, but 26\% below the full model. Reading the term-structure shape -- for instance, distinguishing a sharp recent crash from a gradual decline -- requires comparing multiple horizons simultaneously, which is only possible at depth~$\geq 3$. Performance peaks at depth~4 and declines beyond, as overfitting to higher-order noise offsets any additional signal.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}\label{sec:ablation_results}

While SHAP measures each feature's contribution to individual predictions within the trained model, the ablation studies in this section retrain the model without specific features entirely -- testing whether the model's \emph{learning} depends on them, not just its predictions. A feature could have high SHAP importance but be redundant in an ablation if other features compensate when the model is retrained.

\paragraph{Regime signal removal}
Removing the regime signal and retraining XGBoost on the 12 momentum features alone drops the Sharpe from 1.11 to 0.41, confirming that the HMM captures decision-relevant information that the momentum features cannot compensate for even when the model is free to adapt.

\paragraph{Linear model confound test}\label{sec:target_confound}
Because M1 uses a classification target while M2 uses continuous returns, a natural concern is whether M1's failure reflects the choice of training target rather than linearity. A Ridge regression predicting continuous forward returns -- the same target as M2 -- achieves a Sharpe of $-$0.57, even worse than M1's $-$0.03 (Appendix~\ref{app:ridge}). Both linear models fail because they assign $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ a single coefficient that cannot capture the conditional logic required to adapt stock selection across regimes.

\paragraph{Regime signal alternatives}
Table~\ref{tab:regime_signal_ablation} compares XGBoost performance under three regime signal specifications: the baseline HMM $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, the GHM market-cycle variable \citep{GHM2023}, and no regime signal. The HMM signal produces the highest Sharpe ratio (1.11), exceeding the GHM cycle (0.95) and substantially exceeding the no-signal baseline (0.41). The GHM cycle does improve over no signal, but the HMM's real-time stress indicators capture regime information that trailing market returns alone cannot. All four GHM market-state strategies also collapse in long-short construction (Appendix~\ref{app:ghm}), because their deterministic blending of two horizons cannot adapt the short side to changing regimes.

\input{tables/table_regime_signal_ablation}

\paragraph{Comparison with \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}}
On the same data and with the same transaction costs, M2 (Sharpe 1.11, alpha 23.8\%, $t = 4.08$) outperforms the D\&M managed momentum strategy (Sharpe 0.49, alpha 10.3\%, $t = 1.86$).\footnote{Adding seven fundamental characteristics (book-to-market, ROE, gross profitability, leverage, asset growth, earnings growth, log market equity) to the mom+$\pi$ specification reduces the Sharpe from 1.11 to 0.98, with the loss concentrated in calm months. Fundamentals dilute the momentum signal without improving the reversal mechanism; the regime-momentum interaction, not stock quality, drives long-short performance.} The comparison illustrates two mechanisms for using regime information: D\&M scale exposure to a fixed portfolio (how much to hold), while M2 changes which stocks are held (what to hold). The tree path analysis in Section~\ref{sec:tree_paths} provides structural evidence for this distinction: the nearly identical horizon combination frequencies across regimes confirm that M2 maintains the same attention structure in both calm and panic, reversing the selection direction rather than scaling exposure. Adding D\&M-style scaling on top of M2 does not improve performance, suggesting that M2 already internalises the regime information D\&M uses.


\subsection{Risk Aversion and the Value of the Regime Signal}\label{sec:risk_aversion}

The preceding analysis established that the regime signal is essential for long-short momentum. A natural question is whether this finding depends on how the model's training objective balances return against risk. To test this, XGBoost is retrained under three families of risk-adjusted targets, each with varying intensity:

Two findings emerge. First, under mild risk adjustment ($r/\sigma^{0.5}$ or small $\gamma$), the regime signal's share of feature importance actually \emph{increases} (from 45\% to 57\%), while the Sharpe ratio barely changes. Mild risk penalisation removes the noisiest bets but preserves the regime-conditional structure -- the model still adapts its joint use of momentum horizons across regimes, just with less exposure to extreme outcomes.

Second, aggressive risk adjustment breaks the strategy. At $\gamma = 0.5$ the Sharpe drops to 0.75 and the regime signal's share falls to 35\%; at $a \geq 1$ the strategy collapses (Sharpe 0.36). The variance penalty overwhelms the long-short spread, steering the model toward low-volatility stocks regardless of regime and effectively abandoning the mechanism that makes M2 successful.

The implication is that detecting the regime-dependent structure of momentum requires the model to be free to learn flexible, regime-conditional feature interactions. Conservative risk objectives constrain this flexibility, forcing the model toward static low-volatility selections that obscure the very regime-dependent structure the regime signal is designed to capture.

Taken together, the results provide converging evidence that, in the sample studied, momentum's cross-sectional predictability shifts between continuation and reversal across market regimes, and that exploiting this shift requires nonlinear conditioning on a regime signal. The signal's value lies not in predicting returns directly but in conditioning how the model jointly uses the full momentum term structure -- a multi-dimensional interaction that only tree-based models can learn.
