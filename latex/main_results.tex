\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

All strategies are evaluated out-of-sample on 2011--2025 (167 months), using the long-short construction described in Section~\ref{sec:stage2}. The central question is whether momentum's cross-sectional structure is regime-dependent, and whether exploiting this dependence requires nonlinear modelling. Section~\ref{sec:performance_results} compares the four model variants (DET, LR, Ridge, XGB) against unconditional momentum benchmarks; XGB emerges as the method that captures the regime-momentum signal best, and the rest of the chapter takes XGB as the chosen model and analyses how it does so.


\subsection{Portfolio Performance}\label{sec:performance_results}

\subsubsection{Results Overview}

Table~\ref{tab:performance} reports the out-of-sample performance of all strategies.\footnote{Sharpe convention details (raw vs.\ excess returns; arithmetic-vs-geometric mean) are in Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation}.}

\input{tables/table_performance}

Momentum's cross-sectional predictability is regime-dependent, and exploiting this dependence requires both a probabilistic regime signal and a nonlinear model. XGB, an XGBoost regressor trained on the 12 momentum horizons together with the HMM filtered probability $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, achieves an annualised Sharpe of 1.11 with a six-factor alpha of 24.1\% ($t = 4.81$; Appendix~\ref{app:factor_alphas}). Block bootstrap paired tests reject equality of Sharpe ratios between XGB and each benchmark in Table~\ref{tab:performance} at $p < 0.025$; XGB's own 95\% Sharpe confidence interval is $[0.67, 1.53]$, comfortably above zero, while every benchmark's interval straddles zero (Appendix~\ref{app:bootstrap}). An expanding-window backtest extending the OOS sample to 30 years (1995--2024) reproduces this Sharpe within sampling noise on the 167-month overlap with the main test period (0.88; see Section~\ref{sec:historical_stress}, which also covers the dot-com bust and GFC out-of-sample). Every other specification fails to produce economically meaningful long-short performance, and the pattern of failures is informative.

Unconditional 12-month momentum achieves a Sharpe of 0.06 with a maximum drawdown of $-$72.2\%, exposing the crash vulnerability documented by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} and \citet{BarrosoSantaClara2015}. A deterministic regime switch (DET) improves this marginally to 0.11. The logistic regression (LR), given identical features to XGB, collapses to $-$0.01; a Ridge regression with the same 13 features but a continuous-return target (matching XGBoost's) fails more decisively, with Sharpe $-$0.57 and a coefficient on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ of $+0.003$ (Appendix~\ref{app:ridge}). The failure is therefore linearity itself, not the classification target. Hand-engineering the missing nonlinearity into LR through $\pi^2$ and $\pi \times mom_k$ terms lifts it to 0.32, establishing that the regime-momentum interaction is real while also showing that pre-specified interactions recover less than a third of what XGBoost finds automatically. Adding firm-level fundamentals to XGB's feature set degrades performance rather than improving it (Section~\ref{sec:fund_ablation}), evidence that $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$'s role is specifically to condition the momentum term structure.

\subsubsection{Regime Signal Ablation}

The regime signal is essential. Removing $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ and retraining on the 12 momentum features alone drops the Sharpe to 0.43 (Table~\ref{tab:regime_signal_ablation}). Replacing $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ with the four raw stress indicators (DD, DISP, REL\_N, CS) performs even worse (Sharpe 0.26). With four additional dimensions the model must reconstruct the regime structure at each split, while the HMM distils those indicators into a single dimension that one tree split can condition on. The HMM's role is not to provide a convenient feature but to solve regime identification \emph{before} stock selection, freeing the cross-sectional model to use the regime signal rather than construct it. If the model cannot extract a meaningful regime signal even when given the four raw indicators directly, it certainly cannot do so from momentum features alone, which are one further step removed from the underlying regime dynamics.

\subsubsection{Feature-Set Ablation}\label{sec:fund_ablation}

The regime signal ablation removes $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ from the production model. The complementary question is whether \emph{adding} widely used firm-level fundamentals strengthens the strategy. Ten characteristics are tested (seven value-quality-size measures and three cash-flow-based earnings-quality measures, real-time and four-month-lagged from Compustat; definitions in Appendix~\ref{app:fundamental_features}); each variant is trained at production settings.

\input{tables/table_fundamentals_ablation}

Adding the fundamentals to the baseline reduces Sharpe from 1.11 to 0.89 (Table~\ref{tab:fund_ablation}). The new features absorb 44\% of total SHAP, redirecting attention away from the momentum term structure (54\% to 27\%) and the regime signal (46\% to 29\%) without net improvement. Beta falls from 0.45 to 0.07 and maximum drawdown tightens from $-$22.8\% to $-$18.3\%, so the augmented variant behaves more like a low-beta market-neutral strategy than a high-alpha regime-conditioned one.

The fund-only variant identifies the source of the dilution. Its calm Sharpe of 0.86 essentially matches the baseline's 0.84, but its panic Sharpe of 0.38 is a fraction of the baseline's 1.53: stable firm attributes do not flip direction when the market enters panic. Adding the regime signal to fundamentals (fund + $\pi$) lifts panic Sharpe to 0.72 but remains well short of the full model, because without the momentum term structure there is no directional relationship for the regime signal to reorganise. The regime-momentum interaction is the load-bearing mechanism; fundamentals cannot substitute for it. A regime-conditional feature-set design is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:future_work}.


\subsection{How the Model Reorganises Momentum Across Regimes}\label{sec:regime_results}

Section~\ref{sec:performance_results} established that XGB is the only specification that produces meaningful out-of-sample long-short returns. This subsection asks what XGB learned. The answer is not that the model simply puts more weight on short-term momentum during stress or more weight on long-term momentum during calm periods. Instead, the model learns different cross-sectional momentum shapes and uses the regime signal $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ to route between them. At low $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, the long leg resembles a conventional momentum portfolio, selecting stocks with above-average intermediate-to-long horizon returns. At high $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, the long leg reverses, selecting stocks whose past returns are below the cross-sectional average across the entire 1--12 month term structure. The remainder of this subsection documents that reorganisation in three steps. SHAP establishes that both $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ and the momentum horizons are decision-relevant (Section~\ref{sec:horizon_evidence}). Cross-sectional z-scores then show \emph{what shape} the model actually buys at each $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ level (Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity_picks}). Clustering reduces the noisy monthly heatmap into four recurring selection modes, two continuation modes (low to ambiguous $\pi$) and two reversal modes (post-panic and active panic), with $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ acting as the routing variable between them.

\subsubsection{Per-Horizon Momentum SHAP}\label{sec:horizon_evidence}

XGB's predictions are decomposed using SHAP values \citep{Lundberg2017}, which measure how much each input contributes to the model's prediction (definitions in Appendix~\ref{app:shap}). Aggregate feature importance is split almost evenly. $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ accounts for 46\% of total SHAP, the 12 momentum horizons together for 54\% (Table~\ref{tab:shap}). The near-even split supports a context-variable role for $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$. It is neither a minor control nor a substitute for momentum, but a routing variable that determines how the model uses the momentum term structure. Between regimes, momentum's share rises from 50\% in calm to 58\% in panic while $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$'s share falls from 50\% to 42\%, with the model leaning on momentum more heavily during stress.

\input{tables/table_shap}

Beyond the aggregate split, the model's reliance on each horizon shifts between regimes. In the calm long leg, months 8--12 together account for $66.0\%$ of long-leg momentum SHAP, with months 11 and 12 the most influential individual horizons ($18.6\%$ and $16.8\%$). In the panic long leg, months 7--12 account for $67.2\%$; the same intermediate-to-long band carries roughly two-thirds of the prediction signal in both regimes, mildly shifted toward the shorter end during panic. The sharpest within-regime move is on the panic short side, where month-1 SHAP rises to $10.6\%$ versus $5.3\%$ in calm, so the model becomes more sensitive to recent returns when identifying stocks to short during stress. SHAP tells us \emph{which} horizons are decision-relevant; it does not tell us the sign of the relationship. The same SHAP weight can arise from three different selection rules. The model could buy stocks with high momentum at that horizon (the classical continuation signal of \citet{JegadeeshTitman1993}), buy stocks with low momentum instead (a reversal signal), or use the horizon only conditional on other features, rewarding the same input differently in different states of $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$. The third case is exactly what a tree-based model can encode and a linear model cannot. SHAP attribution alone cannot distinguish these three cases. To identify the economic content of the predictions, the next subsection turns from feature attribution to the cross-sectional characteristics of the stocks actually selected into the long leg.

\subsubsection{Clustering Selection Modes Across Market States}\label{sec:heterogeneity_picks}

The regime-conditional Sharpe is 1.53 in panic against 0.84 in calm (Table~\ref{tab:regime_sharpe}). Where exposure-scaling methods reduce positions during stress to limit losses, XGB instead reconstructs the portfolio (Section~\ref{sec:stress_test}); the rest of this subsection shows that the regime signal flips the way the model uses the momentum term structure, from selecting winners in calm to selecting losers in panic.

To trace where in the cross-section the model picks, we use cross-sectional z-scores: for each stock in the long or short leg, the z-score at horizon $k$ is $(mom_k^s - \bar{mom}_k) / \sigma_k$, computed from the cross-sectional mean and standard deviation of all stocks that month. Figure~\ref{fig:zscore_long_heatmap} plots the long-leg z-curve month by month.

Selection is not uniform across months, but the heatmap shows the model does not switch randomly either: it repeatedly returns to a small number of recognisable selection modes. To summarise these modes, we cluster the 167 monthly z-score profiles with $K$-means ($K=4$, treating each month as a 12-dimensional point). The partition is highly stable across random initialisations.\footnote{The same labels emerge across six independent random seeds for $163$ of $167$ months ($97.6\%$), with mean pairwise Adjusted Rand Index $0.96$. Pairwise centroid distances in 12-dimensional z-space range from $1.37$ (clusters 1 and 2, both above-average) to $4.90$ (clusters 1 and 4, the extremes), against a typical within-cluster scatter of $0.6$ to $0.9$.} The four groups nest inside a coarser two-fold partition: clusters 1 and 2 are the 115 months in which the long leg sits above the cross-section average (``above-average baskets'', $\bar\pi = 0.28$), and clusters 3 and 4 are the 52 months below (``below-average baskets'', $\bar\pi = 0.52$).

\paragraph{Cluster 1 (calm continuation): a quiet, broadly trending market.}
With current $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ at $0.21$ (the lowest of any cluster), the model has high confidence the regime is calm and commits to its standard winner-selection mode. The strength of this commitment shows in the long-leg z-curve, which has a z-mean of $+0.57$ — substantially above zero, and the second-largest absolute amplitude of any cluster after cluster 4. The curve is humped: picks sit only mildly above average at short horizons ($z \approx +0.26$ at month 1) but are clearly differentiated at intermediate horizons (peak $+0.80$ at month 8). In absolute terms, raw pick momentum reaches $+60\%$ at month 9, reproducing the buy-side of \citet{JegadeeshTitman1993}'s twelve-minus-one strategy. The intermediate-horizon peak matches \citet{NovyMarx2012}'s documented dominance, and the underlying mechanism is \citet{HongStein1999}'s gradual information diffusion, in which news enters prices slowly so winners keep winning until the information is fully absorbed.

These 53 months are a stable, mature bull market, the conditions under which classical momentum works best. The cross-section is positive at every horizon (average stock up $3\%$/$13\%$/$23\%$ over 1/6/12 months), so there is a coherent up-trend for the model to ride. The rise is broad rather than driven by a few outliers (cross-section skewness $4.6$/$6.2$/$6.7$, below the test-period average of $5.8$/$6.6$/$7.1$), which means winner-selection picks up genuine widespread participation rather than chasing a handful of unrepresentative names. Recent panic activity is at its lowest ($27\%$ of the prior 6 months in panic), so the cluster sits far from the post-bear-market rebounds where unconditional momentum is known to crash, and continuation can pay safely. The cluster is concentrated in 2013--2014 and runs in stretches of up to 21 consecutive months, the kind of extended bull market in which \citet{Cooper2004} document that momentum profits are most concentrated.

Cluster 1 earns a Sharpe of $0.92$, the lowest of the four. The Sharpe is moderate because the trend is mature; continuation still pays, but the strategy is riding an established rally rather than catching a turning point. Cluster 1 is a sanity check on the architecture, since at the lowest $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ values in the test sample the model recovers the classical cross-sectional momentum pattern \citep{JegadeeshTitman1993,Asness2013}. The regime-conditioning architecture adds new behaviour during stress without overriding the unconditional pattern that already works in calm.

\paragraph{Cluster 2 (mild continuation): a choppy market with no broad direction.}
With current $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ in an ambiguous middle band ($0.34$, neither clearly calm nor clearly panic), the model cannot commit to either winner-selection or loser-selection. The long-leg z-curve has the same humped shape as cluster 1 but at the smallest absolute amplitude of any cluster (z-mean $+0.19$ against the test-period long-leg average of $+0.09$, peak $+0.32$ at month 8); the muted commitment matches the most ambiguous regime signal of the four clusters. Performance comes from occasional outlier capture rather than systematic selection.

These 62 months are an ambiguous transition state. Recent stress is still elevated (6-month panic frequency $0.37$, above the test-period average of $0.34$), but the overall cross-section trend is mild ($mom_{\text{overall}} = 0.07$, below the test-period average of $0.08$), so no clear up-trend has yet formed. The cross-section is only mildly positive on average ($+2\%$/$+8\%$/$+11\%$ over 1/6/12 months), and cross-section skewness is the highest of any cluster ($7.2$/$7.6$/$8.1$), with a small number of extreme winners pulling the mean above zero while the median stock's return stays near flat, so even the modest positive level is not broad-based. Cluster 2 is the largest of the four ($n=62$) but appears only in short bursts (max 4 consecutive months), consistent with its role as a brief transition between regimes rather than a stable state. The muted shape is consistent with \citet{Cooper2004}'s finding that momentum profits depend on the prior market state, and with \citet{HongLimStein2000}'s slow-diffusion mechanism, in which momentum profits in non-trending markets concentrate in stocks completing their delayed information adjustment rather than spreading across the cross-section.

Cluster 2 earns a Sharpe of $0.93$, statistically indistinguishable from cluster 1, and exposes the limit of the regime-momentum mechanism. When $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ sits in the ambiguous middle band and the cross-section carries no coherent direction, the model cannot build a high-conviction long leg even with the regime signal available. The strategy earns roughly flat returns in these months and gets its small edge through occasional outliers rather than systematic selection.

\paragraph{Cluster 3 (post-panic recovery): a recovering market with high long-horizon dispersion.}
Moderate current $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ ($0.32$) combined with the highest 12-month prior frequency ($0.41$) is the feature combination the model has learned to read as a recently-resolved panic. XGB routes these months into a post-bear-rebound branch, building the long leg from cross-sectional losers rather than winners. The long-leg z-curve sits below the cross-section average at every horizon, with z-mean $-0.29$ (a moderate amplitude, smaller than the extreme commitments seen in clusters 1 and 4 because the post-panic signature is read jointly from $\pi$ and prior frequencies rather than from $\pi$ at an extreme value alone), and the deepest below-average at month 4 ($-0.36$) and month 12 ($-0.35$). The picks' absolute trailing returns range from $-6\%$ to $+1\%$ while the broader cross-section is up double digits. The picks rotate more in this cluster than in any other: only about $68\%$ of the long-leg names carry over from one cluster-3 month to the next consecutive cluster-3 month (the lowest carry-over fraction of any cluster), as some recovery candidates catch up and others remain behind.

These 31 months follow a panic-heavy year ($41\%$ of the prior 12 months were panic, the highest of any cluster), but current panic probability has dropped to a moderate $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}=0.32$, so the panic is resolving rather than ongoing. The cross-section is recovering accordingly, with the average stock up $+1\%$/$+11\%$/$+27\%$ over 1/6/12 months. Cross-section dispersion is the highest of any cluster at both the 5--8 and 9--12 month horizons ($0.56$ and $0.85$), reflecting an uneven recovery in which some stocks rebound while others still lag. Cluster 3 is concentrated in 2021 (9 of 31 months, the post-COVID rotation) and 2023. The increasing dispersion across horizons matches \citet{LeeSwaminathan2000}'s lifecycle interpretation, in which momentum signals evolve from continuation to reversal as information ages.

These months also exhibit the beta-reversion dynamic that \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} identify as crash-prone for unconditional momentum. The mechanism runs in two steps. First, during the preceding bear market, past losers tend to be high-$\beta$ stocks that fall disproportionately, and past winners tend to be low-$\beta$ defensives that hold up. When the recovery arrives, the same high-$\beta$ losers rally disproportionately while the low-$\beta$ defensives lag, so the cross-sectional ranking inverts. Cluster-level CAPM betas confirm the inversion empirically (Appendix~\ref{app:cluster_cross_validation}, Table~\ref{tab:cluster_k4_leg_betas}). Fixed 12-month momentum has the most negative long-minus-short $\beta$ spread in cluster 3 ($-0.75$, the deepest inversion of any cluster), so its long leg holds lower-beta stocks than its short leg and gets dragged down when the high-$\beta$ losers in its short leg rally. XGB has flipped the selection: its long-leg $\beta$ exceeds its short-leg $\beta$ ($+0.49$), riding the same beta-reversion that breaks fixed momentum.

Cluster 3 earns a Sharpe of $1.21$ on these loser picks. The novelty is the cluster's identification of a dispersed-recovery selection mode, triggered by moderate $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ paired with a panic-heavy 12-month history. While the cross-section is up double-digits and dispersion is at its highest, the long leg is built from stocks still below average at every horizon. This is qualitatively distinct from the short-horizon reversal of \citet{Jegadeesh1990} (one-month) and \citet{Lehmann1990} (weekly), which fire on a signal at a single short horizon, and from \citet{DeBondtThaler1985}'s long-horizon contrarian reversal at 3--5 year windows; cluster 3 selects names that sit below the cross-section average \emph{uniformly across the 1--12 month range}, a region of horizon space targeted by neither classical reversal. \citet{GHM2023} identify regime turning points like cluster 3 as critical inflection moments for momentum and propose blending fast and slow horizon weights to navigate them. XGB's response is different. It uses $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ as a regime signal to invert the entire selection direction rather than blending horizon weights.

\paragraph{Cluster 4 (deep crisis): an active panic with the whole market falling.}
The market has been falling for a sustained period. The probability of panic is at its highest ($\pi_t^{\text{filter}}=0.81$), $51\%$ of the prior 6 months were also panic (the highest of any cluster), and the cross-section is uniformly negative, with the average stock down $5\%$/$11\%$/$7\%$ over 1/6/12 months. With $\pi$ at this extreme, the model has unambiguous panic detection and commits fully to deep-crisis loser-selection. In this falling market the model picks the most beaten-down names. The long-leg z-curve sits uniformly between $-0.73$ and $-0.88$ across all 12 horizons (deepest around month 9), meaning the picks are roughly 0.7 to 0.9 cross-section standard deviations below the average stock at every horizon. Their absolute returns deepen from $-15\%$ over the prior month to $-48\%$ over the prior 12 months. The strategy bets on a rebound, since stocks that have fallen the furthest in the panic are typically those with the highest market beta, and these high-$\beta$ losers rally disproportionately once the stress resolves. This is the same high-$\beta$-rebound mechanism as cluster 3, but at the panic peak rather than during recovery.

Critically, only $30\%$ of the prior 12 months were panic, in line with the test-period average. Combined with the elevated current $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}=0.81$, this signature describes sharp, short panics concentrated in the recent few months rather than prolonged year-long deteriorations. The reversal bet rests on this premise; Section~\ref{sec:stress_test} examines what happens to the strategy when the panic is instead sustained. Cross-section dispersion is the lowest of any cluster at both the 5--8 and 9--12 month horizons ($0.35$ and $0.45$), and 1--4 month cross-section skewness is also the lowest ($3.67$), so stocks are moving down together rather than fanning out. Eleven of 21 months are in 2022 (Fed-hiking cycle), with corroborating instances in April 2020 (COVID trough), October 2015 and January 2016 (China devaluation, oil collapse), and January 2023.

Cluster 4 earns the highest Sharpe of any cluster ($1.82$, $95\%$ block-bootstrap CI $[0.98, 2.68]$), with a mean monthly return of $+3.13\%$. The rebound bet pays off in 13 of the 21 cluster-4 months ($62\%$), with wins averaging well above losses. The largest single-month gains are $+13.6\%$ in September 2011, $+10.6\%$ in December 2022, and $+10.4\%$ in January 2023, while the worst loss is $-8.0\%$ in October 2011. The combination of a high hit rate and asymmetric win/loss magnitudes drives the cluster's Sharpe. The novelty is twofold. First, this is the strongest amplitude of any cluster: the long-leg z-mean is $-0.82$, and \citet{Nagel2012}'s observation that short-horizon reversal spikes during crises shows the term-structure-wide pattern has no direct analogue in the single-horizon reversal literature already discussed for cluster 3. Cluster 4 differs from cluster 3 in being a more extreme version of the same uniform 1--12 month reversal, fired at the panic peak rather than during the recovery, with the most extreme $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ value driving the strongest selection commitment. Second, where \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} document the beta-reversion mechanism as a \emph{risk} to be scaled away from, XGB exploits it as a strategy. The regime signal makes the contrarian bet loadable, and it pays off precisely because these panics are sharp and short rather than prolonged. The SHAP shares confirm this routing structure. $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$'s combined long-and-short share is \emph{lowest} here ($0.24$) because once a tree has branched into the deep-crisis branch, momentum splits alone produce the loser portfolio. Cluster-level leg-beta and SHAP-share diagnostics for the full $K=4$ partition are in Appendix~\ref{app:cluster_cross_validation}.

\subsubsection{Discussion}\label{sec:novelty}

The $K=4$ partition isolates the central economic finding of this thesis. XGB does not simply shorten the momentum lookback during stress, nor does it use $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ as a constant return predictor. Instead, the model learns four recurring selection modes and lets the regime signal route between them. Clusters 1 and 2 are above-average baskets (continuation modes); clusters 3 and 4 are below-average baskets (reversal modes). The transition from continuation to reversal is not captured by any single fixed lookback or by any linear coefficient on a regime variable. It requires reading the same momentum horizons differently inside each branch of the regime-conditioned tree. The four clusters trace a clear $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$-to-mode mapping. At low $\pi$ (cluster 1, $0.21$) the model selects classical winners; at ambiguous middle-band $\pi$ (cluster 2, $0.34$) it loses conviction; at moderate $\pi$ paired with high recent-year panic frequency (cluster 3, $\pi=0.32$, 12-month prior $0.41$) it inverts to loser selection; at extreme $\pi$ (cluster 4, $0.81$) it builds a uniformly term-structure-wide reversal portfolio. This last mode is the first contribution to the existing momentum literature \citep{Cooper2004,DanielMoskowitz2016,GHM2023}: a term-structure-wide reversal portfolio in deep crisis with no direct analogue, since the classical reversal literature targets either single short horizons or long contrarian windows but not the uniform 1--12 month signature of cluster 4.

The cluster-level SHAP shares (Appendix~\ref{app:cluster_cross_validation}, Table~\ref{tab:cluster_k4_shap_shares}) mirror the same routing story from a different angle. $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$'s combined long-and-short SHAP share is highest in cluster 3 ($0.54$), where identifying the post-panic regime requires combining the current $\pi$ value with prior panic frequencies, so the regime signal is doing the most routing work. It is mid-range in clusters 1 and 2 ($0.50$ and $0.46$), where the regime signal is consulted but the momentum profile delivers the actual selection within the calm branch. It is lowest in cluster 4 ($0.24$), where once a tree has branched on extreme $\pi$, momentum splits alone produce the loser portfolio. The cluster-level SHAP shares therefore track how much routing work the regime signal has to do, complementing the z-curve evidence on what shape the model picks. This is the second contribution to the literature: a regime signal whose marginal value scales with cross-sectional ambiguity rather than acting as a constant feature, in contrast to single-coefficient regime treatments in linear specifications.

This is why the linear baselines on identical features collapse (Section~\ref{sec:performance_results}). A linear model assigns one slope per momentum horizon, so it can answer ``does high momentum predict high return?'' but not ``does high momentum predict high return in calm and low return in panic?''. XGB's tree splits encode that state-dependent reversal directly. Once a tree has branched on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, the same momentum input can be rewarded in one branch and penalised in the other. The regime signal is therefore not a return predictor in its own right but a routing variable that determines which momentum shape the model treats as attractive. This implies the third contribution: selection by momentum \emph{level} rather than momentum \emph{rank}. Where classical momentum buys top-decile and sells bottom-decile by cross-sectional ranking, XGB picks by absolute z-curve shape, and the attractive shape changes with the regime; cluster 3 buys names that sit moderately below the average at every horizon, and cluster 4 buys names that sit deeply below at every horizon. No fixed-rank strategy can replicate these portfolios because the rank position of an attractive stock changes with $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$.

Economically, this reframes momentum's crash-management problem as a stock-selection decision rather than an exposure-scaling one. \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} and \citet{BarrosoSantaClara2015} reduce gross exposure when crash conditions arise; XGB keeps full exposure but reorganises which stocks fill each leg. The cluster-level CAPM betas confirm this empirically (Appendix~\ref{app:cluster_cross_validation}, Table~\ref{tab:cluster_k4_leg_betas}). XGB maintains long-leg $\beta$ above short-leg $\beta$ in every cluster (spreads $+0.68, +0.40, +0.49, +0.43$ for clusters 1--4), while fixed 12-month momentum exhibits the D\&M leg-beta inversion in every cluster ($-0.24, -0.87, -0.75, -0.42$). Where exposure-scaling avoids the crash mechanism by shrinking, XGB avoids it by re-ranking.

The regime-aware adaptation also concentrates on the reversal side. Clusters 3 and 4 differ by $0.61$ in Sharpe while clusters 1 and 2 are indistinguishable ($0.01$); the model distinguishes reversal environments more sharply than trend-following ones. The cluster 4 reversal mechanism, which drives the largest single-cluster Sharpe ($1.82$), rests on a specific premise about the nature of panic-period declines, which Section~\ref{sec:stress_test} stress-tests.

\subsection{Strategy Pitfalls}\label{sec:risk_aversion}

The strategy's headline performance comes with three pitfalls. First, the baseline volatility is high (around $19.5\%$) and some investors will need to dial it down; Section~\ref{sec:risk_aversion_control} traces the risk-return trade-off offered by a CRRA risk-aversion control. Second, the panic-regime reversal rests on the premise that price declines during stress are temporary; a sustained economic deterioration would expose the strategy to reversal failure, which Section~\ref{sec:stress_test} quantifies with a synthetic-recession stress test. Third, the 2011--2025 test sample does not include the worst historical stresses; Section~\ref{sec:historical_stress} extends the evaluation to 30 years (1995--2024) via expanding-window backtests covering the dot-com bust, the GFC, and the 2009 momentum crash.

\subsubsection{Risk-Aversion Control}\label{sec:risk_aversion_control}

The first pitfall is volatility itself: an investor may prefer lower portfolio risk at the cost of some expected return. We incorporate a risk-aversion parameter $\gamma$ into the strategy and trace the out-of-sample trade-off across $\gamma \in [0, 1]$.

The natural starting point is the mean-variance utility $r - \frac{\gamma}{2}\sigma^2$ \citep{Markowitz1952}, but it is the wrong lens here. Mechanically, applied as a scoring function the penalty is subtracted symmetrically from every stock's score, pushing the long book toward low-volatility names and the short book toward high-volatility names: a betting-against-beta portfolio whose realised volatility rises rather than falls as $\gamma$ grows (Appendix~\ref{app:alt_targets} confirms this across several variants). Economically, mean-variance penalises variance in \emph{absolute} terms, but real investors behave as if risk aversion is \emph{relative}, with a stable tolerance for proportional risk that does not change with wealth. Constant relative risk aversion captures this; mean-variance does not.

We therefore adopt a CRRA utility that penalises $\log\sigma$ instead of $\sigma^2$, and evaluate each stock's utility separately under a long direction and a short direction. The volatility penalty enters identically in both: a volatile stock is equally unattractive as a holding or as a short position, so the investor's aversion to risk does not flip sign with trading direction. Longs are the top decile of long-utility across the full cross-section; shorts are the top decile of short-utility. The full specification is given in Appendix~\ref{app:crra_construction}.

Figure~\ref{fig:risk_aversion} shows the resulting Pareto curve.\footnote{The $\gamma=0$ baseline reflects the CRRA dual-utility implementation rather than the headline XGB scoring; small numerical differences from Section~\ref{sec:performance_results} (volatility $19.50\%$, maximum drawdown $-22.8\%$) are within seed noise.} At $\gamma = 0.1$, Sharpe stays at 1.01 while maximum drawdown tightens from $-25\%$ to $-18\%$, a near-free reduction in tail risk. At $\gamma = 0.5$, annualised volatility halves from 19.7\% to 11.2\%, drawdown halves to $-12\%$, and Sharpe settles at 0.78. At $\gamma = 1.0$, volatility falls to 5.2\% (roughly a quarter of the market's) and Sharpe remains positive at 0.46. An investor can choose any point on this curve based on their volatility budget or drawdown tolerance. Beyond $\gamma \approx 1.5$, the risk penalty dominates the return signal and the long-short spread collapses into dollar-neutral noise. The framework does not blow up; it simply stops producing a directional bet.

\subsubsection{Stress Test: Prolonged Bear Market}\label{sec:stress_test}

The reversal mechanism documented in Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity_picks} (cluster 4) rests on a specific premise: that price declines during panic are temporary (driven by liquidity pressure) rather than permanent (driven by economic deterioration). The historical stress periods in the test sample support this: XGB earned $+29.9\%$ during the COVID crash (February--April 2020) and $+21.5\%$ during the 2022 bear market (January--October 2022). In both cases, the decline was temporary and reversed within months.

But the premise can fail. In a sustained economic deterioration (a depression or structural decline), beaten-down stocks fall because their businesses are failing, not because of temporary mispricing. We call this \emph{reversal failure}: the regime classification is correct, but the momentum structure within that regime has changed. To quantify this vulnerability, we simulate recessions of increasing duration, replacing consecutive months with XGB's average losing panic-month return ($-$3.44\% per month) at every possible insertion point.

\input{tables/table_stress_scenarios}

The strategy can absorb substantial downturns. A 12-month recession produces a worst-case drawdown of $-$49\%, after which calm-period continuation resumes. Only a depression-length downturn of 24 months produces a drawdown of $-$69\%. This mirrors the vulnerability identified by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} for traditional momentum, but on the opposite side: standard momentum crashes when winners stop winning; the regime-conditioned strategy would struggle when losers stop reverting.

\citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}'s own response to momentum's crash risk is exposure scaling. The two approaches use regime information differently: D\&M scale \emph{exposure} (how much to hold), XGB changes \emph{which stocks} are held. On the same data and transaction costs, XGB (Sharpe $1.11$, CAPM alpha $23.4\%$) outperforms D\&M managed momentum (Sharpe $0.49$, CAPM alpha $10.3\%$). A hybrid that adds D\&M-style scaling on top of XGB as a circuit breaker (Section~\ref{sec:future_work}) does not improve performance in the current test period because no prolonged downturn occurred, but would help most in cluster 4, where the long leg sits term-structure-wide negative and the reversal bet is most exposed to failure. Section~\ref{sec:historical_stress} provides direct historical evidence on how the strategy actually behaved during the dot-com bust and the GFC, complementing this synthetic stress test.

\subsubsection{Historical Stress: Out-of-Sample Evidence}\label{sec:historical_stress}

The 14-year test sample used so far (2011--2025) misses the worst momentum-relevant stress events: the dot-com bust, the global financial crisis, and the 2009 momentum crash documented by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}. To extend the evaluation, the full pipeline (HMM + XGBoost) is re-estimated on an annual expanding-window schedule: for each year $T$ from 1995 onward, the model is trained only on data available through 31 December of year $T{-}1$ and then used to make predictions for year $T$. Stitching these year-by-year predictions together produces a 30-year out-of-sample monthly return series spanning 1995--2024. The series is genuinely out-of-sample at every point: no future information enters any year's prediction. Methodology details are in Appendix~\ref{app:expanding_window}.

\input{tables/table_expanding_subperiods}

\paragraph{Worst observed stress: the dot-com bust}
The 2000--2002 dot-com bust produced the strategy's largest historical drawdown of $-64.8\%$ peak-to-trough. Restricted to the bear-market proper (March 2000--October 2002), the strategy lost $-43.8\%$ cumulative over 32 months (Sharpe $-0.39$). This is the historical realisation of the reversal-failure vulnerability described in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}: a prolonged sector-rotating bear in which beaten-down stocks did not promptly recover, so the panic-regime reversal selection bled. The strategy survived the episode and resumed positive returns thereafter (the 2003--2006 sub-sample produces Sharpe 0.70), but the realised drawdown is severe.

\paragraph{Stress at the targeted failure mode: the 2009 momentum crash}
The single most informative episode is March--December 2009, the period \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} document as a severe momentum crash. Under the same long-short construction used throughout this thesis (NYSE breakpoints, value-weighted, 10\,bps cost), unconditional 12-month momentum lost $-62.6\%$ cumulative during those ten months. Under expanding-window out-of-sample, the regime-conditional strategy gained $+20.5\%$ cumulative (Sharpe 0.73) during the same window. Across the full GFC episode (October 2007--June 2009) the strategy returned $+22.8\%$ cumulative (Sharpe 0.48). This is direct historical evidence that the regime mechanism survives the specific stress event that defeats unconditional momentum.

\paragraph{Synthetic and historical envelopes agree}
The synthetic stress test's worst-case bound ($-69\%$ drawdown at a 24-month synthetic recession; Section~\ref{sec:stress_test}) contains the realised dot-com drawdown of $-64.8\%$, so the simulation does not understate the historical risk.

\paragraph{Long-horizon survival}
Aggregating across all 30 years and all stress events, the strategy produces $+1{,}405\%$ cumulative return with a Sharpe of 0.50. Each historical drawdown is followed by recovery; no stress episode in the sample is unrecoverable. Sub-period samples are small enough that point estimates carry wide confidence intervals, so the qualitative pattern across periods is more informative than the levels.

\subsection{International Robustness}\label{sec:international}

To test whether the cross-sectional regime-momentum mechanism extends beyond US data, we replicate the full pipeline on UK and Japanese equities (Compustat Global, 1990--2025).\footnote{Each region's HMM is fit on locally-selected stress features (DD+VOL+REL\_N for both, with BANK\_REL replacing the US-specific Moody's BAA-AAA spread in the candidate pool); details in Appendix~\ref{app:intl_feature_selection}. The cross-sectional model is unchanged from the US specification, except that long and short legs are formed from unconditional decile breakpoints across all listed stocks rather than NYSE-equivalent breakpoints (no analogous tier exists for either market).}

Both regions support the mechanism. UK XGB attains a Sharpe of 0.84 over 2011--2025, well above unconditional 12-month momentum's 0.45 and the local market's 0.62. JP XGB attains 0.50, substantially above unconditional momentum's 0.07 but below the JP market's 0.86, consistent with the documented weakness of Japanese momentum \citep{Asness2013}. Maximum drawdown is reduced from $-$62.6\% (fixed momentum) to $-$21.4\% in UK, and from $-$66.3\% to $-$19.8\% in JP.

Taken together, the results show that momentum's cross-sectional predictability shifts between continuation and reversal across market regimes, and that exploiting this shift requires nonlinear conditioning on a regime signal. The signal's value lies not in predicting returns directly but in conditioning how the model jointly uses the full momentum term structure, a multi-dimensional interaction only tree-based models can learn.
