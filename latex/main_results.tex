\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

All strategies are evaluated out-of-sample on 2011--2025 (167 months), using the long-short construction described in Section~\ref{sec:stage2}. The central question is whether momentum's cross-sectional structure is regime-dependent, and whether exploiting this dependence requires nonlinear modelling. The analysis proceeds from aggregate performance (Section~\ref{sec:performance_results}), to the regime-momentum interaction (Section~\ref{sec:regime_results}), ablation studies (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results}), and finally risk aversion and stress testing (Section~\ref{sec:risk_aversion}).


\subsection{Portfolio Performance}\label{sec:performance_results}

Table~\ref{tab:performance} reports the out-of-sample performance of all strategies.\footnote{All Sharpe ratios are computed using raw returns rather than excess returns above the risk-free rate. Since the same convention is applied to every strategy, relative comparisons are unaffected. The risk-free rate averaged roughly 2\% annualised over the 2011--2025 window, so absolute Sharpe ratios are overstated by approximately 0.05--0.10.}

\input{tables/table_performance}

The central result is that momentum's cross-sectional predictability is regime-dependent, and that exploiting this dependence requires both a probabilistic regime signal and a nonlinear model. XGBoost combined with the HMM filtered probability (M2, mom+$\pi$) achieves an annualised Sharpe of 1.11 with a six-factor alpha of 24.7\% ($t = 4.78$). Every other specification in the table fails to produce economically meaningful long-short performance, and the pattern of failures is informative.

Unconditional momentum fails: fixed 12-month momentum achieves a Sharpe of 0.06 with a maximum drawdown of $-$72.3\%, consistent with the deterioration of unconditional momentum strategies documented in the post-2000 literature \citep{DanielMoskowitz2016, BarrosoSantaClara2015}. Conditioning on the regime helps directionally -- M0, which switches the lookback horizon based on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, improves to 0.11 -- but crude switching is not enough. The logistic regression (M1), which receives identical features to M2, collapses to a Sharpe of $-$0.03, establishing that the regime-momentum interaction cannot be captured by a single set of linear weights. Sections~\ref{sec:regime_results} and~\ref{sec:ablation_results} examine why.

The HMM's compression of the four stress indicators into a single filtered probability is itself essential. Replacing $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ with the four raw indicators (DD, DISP, REL\_N, CS) fed directly to XGBoost reduces the Sharpe from 1.11 to 0.30, worse than removing regime information entirely (Sharpe 0.41 without any regime signal; see Table~\ref{tab:regime_signal_ablation}). The raw features add noise rather than signal: with four additional dimensions the model must discover the regime structure from scratch at each split, while the HMM distils the indicators into a single dimension -- the degree of market stress -- that XGBoost can condition on efficiently.

M2's factor alphas are significant across all standard specifications (Table~\ref{tab:factor_alphas}). The alpha is stable at 23--25\% across CAPM, Fama--French three- and five-factor, Carhart, and six-factor models, with $t$-statistics exceeding 4.0 in every case. Factor loadings are near zero throughout, confirming that M2's returns are not repackaged factor exposure. The Newey--West $t$-statistic for mean returns (1.83) is not individually significant, which is expected for a market-neutral long-short strategy with 19.7\% annualised volatility; the economically meaningful test is the factor alpha.

\subsection{The Regime-Momentum Interaction}\label{sec:regime_results}

This section examines how M2 adapts its use of momentum across regimes. We first decompose performance by regime, then use SHAP values and cross-sectional z-scores to analyse the model's selection at each momentum horizon. We show that the model recovers known patterns from the momentum literature in calm, and discovers a novel term-structure-wide reversal mechanism in panic. Tree path and depth analyses confirm that the nonlinearity comes from regime-conditional direction flipping, not from complex horizon interactions.

Decomposing performance by market regime reveals how M2 uses momentum differently in calm and panic (Table~\ref{tab:regime_sharpe}). M2 achieves a Sharpe ratio of 1.56 during panic months compared to 0.82 during calm months. This panic-period dominance contrasts sharply with the long-only literature, where calm-period improvement typically drives overall performance. In long-short construction, the ability to correctly position the short side becomes the primary source of value.

\input{tables/table_regime_sharpe}

To open the black box of M2's regime-conditional decisions, we decompose its predictions using two complementary tools: SHAP values \citep{Lundberg2017}, which measure how much each horizon contributes to the prediction (see Appendix~\ref{app:shap}), and cross-sectional z-scores, which measure where in the distribution the model selects at each horizon. Table~\ref{tab:shap} reports aggregate feature importance: the regime signal $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ accounts for 45\% of total importance, comparable to the combined contribution of all 12 momentum horizons, with its importance increasing from calm to panic months.

\input{tables/table_shap}

The split between calm and panic is informative: momentum's aggregate importance rises from 0.0174 to 0.0277 in panic, while $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$'s importance increases only modestly (0.0164 to 0.0187). This means the model relies more heavily on momentum signals during stress -- but as the term-structure analysis below reveals, it uses them in a fundamentally different way.

Figure~\ref{fig:term_structure} decomposes the aggregate SHAP importance into individual momentum horizons, split by regime and portfolio leg. The top panels plot the average cross-sectional z-score of stocks in the long and short legs at each lookback horizon from 1 to 12 months: a positive z-score means the selected stocks have higher momentum than the cross-sectional average at that horizon, and vice versa. Shaded bands show $\pm 1$ standard error. The bottom panels show each horizon's share of the model's total momentum SHAP contribution, indicating how much attention the model pays to each lookback when forming its predictions. Calm (left) and panic (right) are shown side by side.

\subsubsection{Calm: Momentum Continuation}\label{sec:calm}

The z-score curve for the calm long leg is humped: near zero at month~1, peaking at $+0.39$ at month~8, declining to $+0.17$ at month~12. SHAP peaks later, at month~11. The short leg mirrors this with negative z-scores, deepest at months 8--10 ($-0.27$ to $-0.30$). The portfolio characteristics confirm: the long side holds stocks with $+23.7\%$ trailing 12-month momentum; the short side holds stocks with weaker momentum ($+8.3\%$). The long-short spread is 1.33\% per month.

The humped shape reflects the life cycle of how information is incorporated into stock prices \citep{JegadeeshTitman1993}. At the shortest horizon, stock returns are dominated by microstructure noise and short-term mean reversion \citep{Jegadeesh1990}, so the model learns to largely ignore recent returns. Over the following months, firm-specific news -- earnings surprises, analyst revisions, changes in fundamentals -- diffuses gradually through the investor base \citep{HongStein1999, Barberis1998}, creating predictable continuation as the market slowly reprices. This process peaks at intermediate horizons: \citet{NovyMarx2012} shows that returns from 7 to 12 months prior are the primary driver of momentum profits, and the model's z-score peak at month~8 recovers this finding. Beyond month~8, the continuation effect fades as initial underreaction gives way to overreaction -- stocks that have risen for extended periods attract attention and begin to mean-revert \citep{LeeSwaminathan2000}. SHAP weight continues to rise after month~8 (peaking at month~11) even as z-scores decline.

\subsubsection{Panic: Reversal}\label{sec:panic}

In panic, the humped curve flattens: the long leg's z-score drops to $-0.09$ to $-0.19$ across all horizons, and the portfolio confirms this with $-19.5\%$ trailing 12-month momentum and $-5.2\%$ 1-month momentum. SHAP shifts modestly toward shorter horizons but months 7--12 still dominate. The short leg shows a distinct asymmetry at month~1: its z-score spikes to $+0.30$ while the long leg's is $-0.19$, and the short side holds stocks with positive 1-month momentum ($+3.7\%$). SHAP also tilts toward month~1 on the short side.

The economic basis for this inversion rests on three mechanisms captured by the HMM's stress indicators: \emph{liquidity spirals} \citep{BrunnermeierPedersen2009}, where margin calls force leveraged investors to sell regardless of fundamental value; \emph{institutional flow pressure} \citep{VayanosWoolley2013}, where fund redemptions create predictable downward price pressure; and \emph{investor overreaction} \citep{DeBondtThaler1985}, where panic selling pushes prices below intrinsic value. The month~1 SHAP increase in panic is consistent with \citet{Nagel2012}, who shows that returns to short-term reversal spike during crises when liquidity provision is most valuable. The resulting long-short spread in panic is 3.09\% per month -- more than double the calm-period spread of 1.33\% -- and comes entirely from cross-sectional stock selection (near-zero market beta).

\subsubsection{Discussion}\label{sec:novelty}

The analysis above reveals two findings that, to our knowledge, have not been documented in the prior literature.

\paragraph{Regime-conditional distribution selection}
Traditional momentum strategies apply a fixed rule -- buy winners, short losers -- regardless of market conditions. Conditional strategies such as \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} adapt the \emph{amount} of exposure but not the \emph{type} of stocks selected. M2 does something qualitatively different: it uses the same momentum horizons to select from the upper tail of the cross-sectional distribution in calm and the lower tail in panic. The regime signal does not change which features matter; it changes where in the distribution the model selects. This is only possible because XGBoost can condition the direction of its splits on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, an interaction that linear models cannot represent (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results} provides a quantitative comparison).

\paragraph{Term-structure-wide reversal}
In panic, the model buys stocks that are below average at \emph{every} horizon from 1 to 12 months. This differs from conventional short-term reversal \citep{Jegadeesh1990, Nagel2012}, which targets month~1 only, and from long-term contrarian strategies \citep{DeBondtThaler1985}, which operate at multi-year horizons. A stock that is uniformly below average across the entire term structure is more likely a victim of indiscriminate forced selling than of genuine fundamental decline, which would typically appear first at short horizons and propagate outward over time.

\subsubsection{Tree Path Analysis}\label{sec:tree_paths}

The analysis above treats each horizon independently. A natural question is whether the model relies on specific \emph{interactions} between horizons that the per-horizon decomposition would miss.

To test this, we trace 5{,}000 sampled stocks from each regime$\times$leg group through 50{,}000 individual trees, recording which momentum horizon groups each decision path splits on (see Appendix~\ref{app:combo_analysis} for details). Horizons are grouped into four bands: short (S, months 1--3), medium (M, months 4--6), intermediate (I, months 7--9), and long (L, months 10--12). Table~\ref{tab:combo_freq} reports the frequency of each of the 15 possible combinations, where each column sums to 100\%.

\input{tables/table_combo_freq}

The combination frequencies are remarkably stable across all four groups: I{+}L accounts for 13--15\% of paths everywhere, M{+}I{+}L for 10--11\%, and S{+}M for 7--8\%. No unexpected interaction emerges. The distribution is broadly consistent with what the per-horizon SHAP decomposition would predict: horizons with higher SHAP weight appear more frequently in tree paths, and they combine in proportion to their individual importance.

This clarifies the specific source of nonlinearity that makes XGBoost essential. The model's value does not come from discovering complex momentum-momentum interactions that differ across regimes -- the horizon combinations are regime-invariant. Rather, it comes from the ability to condition the \emph{direction} of selection on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$: the same tree structure that buys intermediate-horizon winners in calm buys intermediate-horizon losers in panic, depending on which branch the regime signal routes each stock through. A linear model cannot replicate this because it assigns a single fixed coefficient to each horizon; XGBoost effectively assigns coefficients whose sign flips with the regime. Table~\ref{tab:combo_long_cp} in Appendix~\ref{app:combo_analysis} confirms this quantitatively: the calm-minus-panic z-score difference is positive for all 39 entries in the long leg, with the largest shifts at the intermediate horizon ($+$0.36 to $+$0.55).

\subsubsection{Tree Depth and Feature Interactions}\label{sec:depth_analysis}

The tree depth analysis provides direct evidence for why multi-dimensional conditioning is required (Figure~\ref{fig:depth_sharpe}). Since XGBoost is trained to predict returns, we report annualised return by depth. Performance increases monotonically from depth~1 (7.3\%, no feature interactions) through depth~4 (21.9\%), with the largest gains at depths~2 and~3 where the model first gains access to multi-way conditioning. Annualised volatility is nearly constant across depths ($\approx$19.5\%), confirming that deeper trees improve signal quality rather than increasing risk-taking. A depth-2 model, which can only condition on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ and one momentum horizon per tree, achieves 15.0\% -- meaningful, but 31\% below the full model. Reading the term-structure shape requires comparing multiple horizons simultaneously, which is only possible at depth~$\geq 3$. Performance peaks at depth~4 and declines beyond depth~5.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}\label{sec:ablation_results}

While SHAP measures each feature's contribution to individual predictions within the trained model, the ablation studies in this section retrain the model without specific features entirely -- testing whether the model's \emph{learning} depends on them, not just its predictions. A feature could have high SHAP importance but be redundant in an ablation if other features compensate when the model is retrained.

\paragraph{Regime signal removal}
Removing the regime signal and retraining XGBoost on the 12 momentum features alone drops the Sharpe from 1.11 to 0.41 (Table~\ref{tab:regime_signal_ablation}), confirming that the HMM captures decision-relevant information that the momentum features cannot compensate for even when the model is free to adapt.

\input{tables/table_regime_signal_ablation}

\paragraph{Linear model confound test}\label{sec:target_confound}
Because M1 uses a classification target while M2 uses continuous returns, a natural concern is whether M1's failure reflects the choice of training target rather than linearity. A Ridge regression predicting continuous forward returns -- the same target as M2 -- achieves a Sharpe of $-$0.57, even worse than M1's $-$0.03 (Appendix~\ref{app:ridge}). Both linear models fail because they assign $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ a single coefficient that cannot capture the conditional logic required to adapt stock selection across regimes.

\paragraph{Comparison with \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}}
On the same data and with the same transaction costs, M2 (Sharpe 1.11, alpha 23.8\%, $t = 4.08$) outperforms the D\&M managed momentum strategy (Sharpe 0.49, alpha 10.3\%, $t = 1.86$). The comparison illustrates two mechanisms for using regime information: D\&M scale exposure to a fixed portfolio (how much to hold), while M2 changes which stocks are held (what to hold). The tree path analysis in Section~\ref{sec:tree_paths} provides structural evidence for this distinction: the nearly identical horizon combination frequencies across regimes confirm that M2 maintains the same attention structure in both calm and panic, reversing the selection direction rather than scaling exposure. Adding D\&M-style scaling on top of M2 does not improve performance, suggesting that M2 already internalises the regime information D\&M uses.


\subsection{Risk Aversion and Stress Testing}\label{sec:risk_aversion}

\subsubsection{Alternative Training Targets}

The preceding analysis established that the regime signal is essential for long-short momentum. A natural question is whether this finding depends on how the model's training objective balances return against risk. To test this, XGBoost is retrained under three families of risk-adjusted targets, each with varying intensity:

\input{tables/table_risk_aversion}

Two findings emerge. First, under mild risk adjustment ($r/\sigma^{0.5}$ or small $\gamma$), the regime signal's share of feature importance actually \emph{increases} (from 45\% to 57\%), while the Sharpe ratio barely changes. Mild risk penalisation removes the noisiest bets but preserves the regime-conditional structure -- the model still adapts its joint use of momentum horizons across regimes, just with less exposure to extreme outcomes.

Second, aggressive risk adjustment breaks the strategy. At $\gamma = 0.5$ the Sharpe drops to 0.75 and the regime signal's share falls to 35\%; at $a \geq 1$ the strategy collapses (Sharpe 0.36). The variance penalty overwhelms the long-short spread, steering the model toward low-volatility stocks regardless of regime and effectively abandoning the mechanism that makes M2 successful.

The implication is that detecting the regime-dependent structure of momentum requires the model to be free to learn flexible, regime-conditional feature interactions. Conservative risk objectives constrain this flexibility, forcing the model toward static low-volatility selections that obscure the very regime-dependent structure the regime signal is designed to capture.

\subsubsection{Stress Test: Prolonged Bear Market}\label{sec:stress_test}

The reversal mechanism documented in Section~\ref{sec:panic} rests on a specific premise: that price declines during panic are temporary (driven by liquidity pressure) rather than permanent (driven by fundamental deterioration). The historical stress periods in the test sample support this: M2 earned $+29.9\%$ during the COVID crash (February--April 2020) and $+21.5\%$ during the 2022 bear market (January--October 2022). In both cases, the decline was temporary and reversed within months.

But the premise can fail. In a sustained economic deterioration -- a depression or structural decline -- beaten-down stocks fall because their businesses are failing, not because of temporary mispricing. We call this \emph{reversal failure}: the regime classification is correct, but the momentum structure within that regime has changed. To quantify this vulnerability, we simulate recessions of increasing duration, replacing consecutive months with M2's average losing panic-month return ($-$3.51\% per month) at every possible insertion point.

\input{tables/table_stress_scenarios}

The strategy can absorb substantial downturns. A 12-month recession produces a worst-case drawdown of $-$51\%, after which calm-period continuation resumes. Only a depression-length downturn of 24 months produces a drawdown of $-$70\%. This mirrors the vulnerability identified by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} for traditional momentum, but on the opposite side: standard momentum crashes when winners stop winning; the regime-conditioned strategy would struggle when losers stop reverting. The hybrid approach combining stock selection with exposure scaling (Section~\ref{sec:future_work}) is designed for this contingency.

Taken together, the results provide converging evidence that, in the sample studied, momentum's cross-sectional predictability shifts between continuation and reversal across market regimes, and that exploiting this shift requires nonlinear conditioning on a regime signal. The signal's value lies not in predicting returns directly but in conditioning how the model jointly uses the full momentum term structure -- a multi-dimensional interaction that only tree-based models can learn.
