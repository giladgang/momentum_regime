\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

All strategies are evaluated out-of-sample on 2011--2025 (167 months), using the long-short construction described in Section~\ref{sec:stage2}. The central question is whether momentum's cross-sectional structure is regime-dependent, and whether exploiting this dependence requires nonlinear modelling.


\subsection{Portfolio Performance}\label{sec:performance_results}

\subsubsection{Strategy Comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:performance} reports the out-of-sample performance of all strategies.\footnote{All Sharpe ratios are computed using raw returns rather than excess returns above the risk-free rate. Since the same convention is applied to every strategy, relative comparisons are unaffected. The risk-free rate averaged roughly 2\% annualised over the 2011--2025 window, so absolute Sharpe ratios are overstated by approximately 0.05--0.10. Sharpe is computed from the arithmetic monthly mean annualised by $\sqrt{12}$, while annualised returns reported elsewhere are compound (geometric); under high volatility the two definitions of average return differ, so Sharpe should not be inferred from a table by dividing annualised return by annualised volatility (e.g.\ at depth~1, the reported Sharpe is $0.53$ but $8.7\% / 19.4\% = 0.45$).}

\input{tables/table_performance}

Momentum's cross-sectional predictability is regime-dependent, and exploiting this dependence requires both a probabilistic regime signal and a nonlinear model. XGBoost combined with the HMM filtered probability (M2, mom+$\pi$) achieves an annualised Sharpe of 1.11 with a six-factor alpha of 24.1\% ($t = 4.81$; Appendix~\ref{app:factor_alphas}). Block bootstrap paired tests reject equality of Sharpe ratios between M2 and each benchmark in Table~\ref{tab:performance} at $p < 0.025$; M2's own 95\% Sharpe confidence interval is $[0.67, 1.53]$, comfortably above zero, while every benchmark's interval straddles zero (Appendix~\ref{app:bootstrap}). An expanding-window backtest extending the OOS sample to 30 years (1995--2024) reproduces this Sharpe within sampling noise on the 167-month overlap with the main test period (0.88; see Section~\ref{sec:historical_stress}, which also covers the dot-com bust and GFC out-of-sample). Every other specification fails to produce economically meaningful long-short performance, and the pattern of failures is informative.

Unconditional 12-month momentum achieves a Sharpe of 0.06 with a maximum drawdown of $-$72.2\%, exposing the crash vulnerability documented by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} and \citet{BarrosoSantaClara2015}. A deterministic regime switch (M0) improves this marginally to 0.11. The logistic regression (M1), given identical features to M2, collapses to $-$0.01; a Ridge regression with the same 13 features but a continuous-return target (matching XGBoost's) fails more decisively, with Sharpe $-$0.57 and a coefficient on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ of $+0.003$ (Appendix~\ref{app:ridge}). The failure is therefore linearity itself, not the classification target. Hand-engineering the missing nonlinearity into M1 through $\pi^2$ and $\pi \times mom_k$ terms lifts it to 0.32, establishing that the regime-momentum interaction is real while also showing that pre-specified interactions recover less than a third of what XGBoost finds automatically. A fund-augmented variant (M2: mom+$\pi$+fund) adds ten firm-level fundamentals to M2's feature set; it reduces the Sharpe to 0.89 while lowering beta from 0.45 to 0.07 and tightening the maximum drawdown from $-$22.8\% to $-$18.3\%. Section~\ref{sec:fund_ablation} analyses this variant alongside several feature-set ablations, which together provide additional evidence that $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$'s role is specifically to condition the momentum term structure.

\subsubsection{Regime Signal Ablation}

The regime signal is essential. Removing $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ and retraining on the 12 momentum features alone drops the Sharpe to 0.43 (Table~\ref{tab:regime_signal_ablation}). Replacing $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ with the four raw stress indicators (DD, DISP, REL\_N, CS) performs even worse (Sharpe 0.26): with four additional dimensions the model must reconstruct the regime structure at each split, while the HMM distils those indicators into a single dimension that one tree split can condition on. The HMM's role is not to provide a convenient feature but to solve regime identification \emph{before} stock selection, freeing the cross-sectional model to use the regime signal rather than construct it. If the model cannot extract a meaningful regime signal even when given the four raw indicators directly, it certainly cannot do so from momentum features alone, which are one further step removed from the underlying regime dynamics.

\subsubsection{Feature-Set Ablation}\label{sec:fund_ablation}

The regime signal ablation shows that removing $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ reduces Sharpe by more than 60\%. A complementary question is whether adding widely used firm-level fundamentals strengthens the strategy, or whether the regime-momentum interaction is load-bearing on its own. This subsection tests both directions by adding, substituting, and removing feature blocks from the production configuration.

Ten firm-level fundamentals are tested: seven standard value-quality-size characteristics (book-to-market, return on equity, leverage, asset growth, gross profitability, earnings growth, and log market equity) and three cash-flow-based earnings-quality measures (operating cash flow over assets, free cash flow over assets, and the accrual component of earnings scaled by assets). All are computed from Compustat quarterly data with a four-month reporting lag to ensure real-time availability; definitions are given in Appendix~\ref{app:fundamental_features}. Each variant is trained at production settings (50 XGBoost seeds, depth 4, learning rate 0.05) and evaluated identically to M2.

\input{tables/table_fundamentals_ablation}

Adding the ten fundamentals to the baseline reduces Sharpe from 1.11 to 0.89 (Table~\ref{tab:fund_ablation}). The new features absorb 44\% of total SHAP attention, redirecting it away from the momentum term structure (54\% to 27\%) and the regime signal (46\% to 29\%) without net improvement in portfolio performance. Beta falls from 0.45 to 0.07 and maximum drawdown tightens from $-$22.8\% to $-$18.3\%; the strategy's excess-return Newey-West $t$-statistic falls from 4.37\textsuperscript{***} to 3.56\textsuperscript{***}, still highly significant but materially weaker. The fund-augmented variant therefore behaves more like a market-neutral low-beta strategy than a high-alpha regime-conditioned one.

The fund-only variant (ten fundamentals, no momentum, no regime signal) identifies the source of the dilution directly. Its calm Sharpe of 0.86 is essentially equal to the baseline's 0.84, but its panic Sharpe of 0.38 is a fraction of the baseline's 1.53. Firm-level characteristics describe stable attributes (leverage, profitability, valuation) that do not flip direction when the market enters panic. Adding the regime signal to fundamentals (fund + $\pi$, panic Sharpe 0.72) roughly doubles the fund-only panic Sharpe but remains well short of the full model, because without the momentum term structure there is no directional relationship for the regime signal to reorganise. Removing $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ from the momentum + fundamentals variant drops Sharpe to 0.63 (panic 0.57), close to fund-only's 0.59. Across both ablations, removing either the momentum term structure or the regime signal causes the same collapse: fundamentals cannot substitute for the regime-momentum interaction, which is the load-bearing mechanism.

These ablations support the interpretation that $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ is a context variable for the momentum term structure specifically, rather than a general-purpose conditioning input. Characteristics that describe stable firm quality are useful in calm markets, where the fund-only calm Sharpe matches the baseline's. But those same characteristics carry no information about regime shifts, and adding them to the joint model dilutes the signal that does. A regime-conditional feature-set design, in which the model uses different inputs in calm and panic, is discussed as future work in Section~\ref{sec:future_work}.


\subsection{The Regime-Momentum Interaction}\label{sec:regime_results}

M2 uses momentum differently across regimes (Table~\ref{tab:regime_sharpe}): Sharpe 1.53 in panic against 0.84 in calm. The regime signal adds the most value precisely when the model switches from continuation to reversal. Where exposure-scaling methods reduce positions during stress to limit losses, M2 leans into panic by reconstructing the portfolio to exploit the reversal (Section~\ref{sec:dm_comparison}). This composition-side mechanism leaves a measurable signature on the legs' market betas: M2's long-leg $\beta$ rises from 1.40 in calm to 1.65 in panic while the short-leg $\beta$ falls from 1.22 to 0.98 (Appendix~\ref{app:leg_betas}). The cross-leg spread $\beta_L - \beta_S$ widens from 0.18 to 0.67 but never inverts. Both legs remain positive-$\beta$ in panic, in contrast to the leg-beta inversion that drives traditional momentum's crashes \citep{DanielMoskowitz2016}.

\input{tables/table_regime_sharpe}

The regime signal is structurally embedded in the ensemble rather than applied selectively. Across the 25{,}000 trees of the 50-seed production ensemble, 18{,}463 (73.9\%) contain at least one split on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, and a typical long-leg stock's path through the ensemble passes through a $\pi$-split 55\% of the time: regime conditioning is built into roughly three-quarters of the model's decision rules and consulted in the majority of individual stock evaluations. The economically informative statistic is how this reliance is state-dependent. In panic months, 65\% of the long leg's total predicted return flows from $\pi$-splitting trees; in calm months, only 17\% does. The model leans on the regime signal predominantly during stress, and its calm-period predictions come mostly from trees that operate on momentum alone. This pattern is consistent with the Sharpe decomposition in Table~\ref{tab:regime_sharpe}: the regime signal adds the most value in exactly the months where it carries the most weight.

M2's predictions are decomposed using two complementary tools. SHAP values \citep{Lundberg2017} measure how much each horizon contributes to the model's prediction (definitions in Appendix~\ref{app:shap}). Cross-sectional z-scores measure where in the momentum distribution the model's selected stocks fall: for each stock in the long or short leg, the z-score at horizon $k$ is $(mom_k^s - \bar{mom}_k) / \sigma_k$, computed from the cross-sectional mean and standard deviation of all stocks that month. The z-score answers \emph{what} the model selects; SHAP answers \emph{what drives} the selection.

Aggregate feature importance is split almost evenly: $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ accounts for 46\% of total SHAP, the 12 momentum horizons together for 54\% (Table~\ref{tab:shap}). Between regimes, momentum's share rises from 50\% in calm to 58\% in panic while $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$'s share falls from 50\% to 42\%. The model leans on momentum more heavily during stress, but uses it in a fundamentally different way. The per-cluster decomposition is developed in Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity_picks} and the per-horizon decomposition in Section~\ref{sec:horizon_evidence}.

\subsubsection{How the Strategy Adapts Across Market States}\label{sec:heterogeneity_picks}

This subsection sharpens the regime-aggregate claim of Section~\ref{sec:regime_results}, which established that $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ accounts for $46\%$ of total SHAP and absorbs the leg-beta inversion that drives traditional momentum's crashes \citep{DanielMoskowitz2016}. The cluster decomposition we develop here moves the claim from ``$\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ matters on average'' to ``$\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ routes the model between structurally distinct selection modes that no fixed-rank or single-regime model could combine in one ranking''. We cluster each test month's long-leg 12-horizon z-profile directly. Figure~\ref{fig:zscore_long_heatmap} shows the raw landscape: 167 monthly z-curves stacked vertically, with structure visible to the eye but no clean decomposition. K-means with $k=4$ on the raw z-vectors (Euclidean distance, no preprocessing or regime pre-split) yields a partition that is highly stable: the same labels emerge across six independent random seeds for $163$ of $167$ months ($97.6\%$), with mean pairwise Adjusted Rand Index $0.96$. The four clusters nest perfectly inside a coarser two-fold partition: clusters 1 and 2 contain all 115 months with the long leg above the cross-section average (``above-average baskets'', $\bar\pi = 0.28$); clusters 3 and 4 contain all 52 months below (``below-average baskets'', $\bar\pi = 0.52$). The partition subsumes the calm-vs-panic dichotomy while exposing within-regime structure that the binary split obscures. The 2-fold split already differentiates performance: the above-average tier earns a Sharpe of $0.93$ over its 115 months versus $1.46$ over the below-average tier's 52 months (mean monthly return $1.42\%$ versus $2.64\%$, a ratio of $1.86\times$).

\paragraph{Four sub-clusters}
Decomposing the two-fold partition into four reveals additional structure within each tier (Figures~\ref{fig:zscore_l2_k4_panel_c0}--\ref{fig:zscore_l2_k4_panel_c3}, Table~\ref{tab:cluster_k4_descriptors}). Per-cluster sample sizes, regime composition, Sharpe with $95\%$ CI, hit rate, and maximum drawdown are reported in Table~\ref{tab:cluster_k4_descriptors}; the full per-cluster feature signature (cross-section context, raw long-leg pick momentum at each horizon, and long-leg z-curve at each horizon) is in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:cluster_k4_features_appendix}. The narrative below emphasises what each cluster \emph{is}: regime context, pick character, and when each occurs in the test period. The detailed metrics live in the tables.

\paragraph{Cluster 1 (calm continuation): a quiet, broadly trending market.}
In these 53 months the cross-section has been rising for a year. The average stock is up $3\%$ over the prior 1 month, $13\%$ over 6 months, and $23\%$ over 12 months. The regime signal sits at its lowest level of any cluster (current $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}=0.21$, prior-6-month frequency $0.27$) and the strategy enters with a trailing 12-month Sharpe of $1.22$, the highest of any cluster. Cross-section skewness is moderate at every horizon ($4.6$ short, $6.2$ mid, $6.7$ long), well below cluster 2's $7.2$ / $7.6$ / $8.1$, so the trend is broad and symmetric rather than driven by a few outliers. Peak frequency is 2013--2014, with stretches of up to 21 consecutive months.

With the entire market trending up and no recent disruption, the model picks classic momentum winners. The long-leg z-curve is uniformly positive and peaks at $+0.80$ at month 8, recovering the intermediate-horizon dominance documented by \citet{NovyMarx2012}. Raw pick momentum reaches $+60\%$ at month 9, reproducing the buy-side of \citet{JegadeeshTitman1993}'s twelve-minus-one strategy. The behavioural drift mechanism of \citet{HongStein1999} supplies the underlying explanation: continuation in trending markets reflects gradual information diffusion.

The Sharpe is moderate because the trend is mature. Continuation still pays, but the strategy is riding an established rally rather than catching a turning point. Cluster 1 is a sanity check rather than a new finding. That the model recovers classical momentum selection in this state confirms the regime-conditioning architecture is parsimonious: it adds regime-conditional behaviour without overriding the unconditional pattern that already works.

\paragraph{Cluster 2 (mild continuation): a choppy market with no broad direction.}
The cross-section is mildly positive (average stock $+2\%$ over the prior 1 month, $+8\%$ over 6 months, $+11\%$ over 12 months, roughly half the level of cluster 1) but has the highest skewness of any cluster ($7.2$ short, $7.6$ mid, $8.1$ long). A small number of extreme winners pull the mean while the median stock barely moves. The regime signal sits in an ambiguous middle band (current $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}=0.34$), and the strategy enters these months with a trailing 12-month Sharpe of $1.04$, the lowest of any cluster. The strategy itself has been working less well in the months leading in.

With no coherent direction in the cross-section, the model cannot differentiate strongly. The long leg sits only mildly above the cross-section (z-mean $+0.19$ versus cluster 1's $+0.57$), tracking the outlier-driven structure rather than a trend. This is consistent with \citet{Cooper2004}'s finding that momentum profits depend on the prior market state, and with the gradual-diffusion model of \citet{HongStein1999} in which momentum profits arise from a few names completing their information adjustment rather than from broad cross-sectional drift.

The cluster's best monthly return is March 2020 at $+26\%$, the test sample's largest single-month gain, and the median sits below the mean. Performance comes from occasional outlier capture rather than systematic selection. Cluster 2 is the largest of the four ($n=62$) but appears in short bursts of up to 4 consecutive months rather than long stretches. Its diagnostic contribution is to expose the limit of the regime-momentum mechanism: when the cross-section itself carries no coherent direction, the model cannot build a high-conviction long leg even with the regime signal in hand. An exposure-scaling overlay (Section~\ref{sec:dm_comparison}) would not address this either, because there is no risk to scale away from, only an absence of selection edge to amplify.

\paragraph{Cluster 3 (post-panic recovery): a recovering market with high long-horizon dispersion.}
These 31 months follow a panic-heavy year (12-month lagged $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ frequency $0.41$, the highest of any cluster) while current $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}=0.32$ is only moderate. The storm has passed but the cross-section is still re-pricing. They are exactly the setting in which \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} document momentum's worst crashes: post-bear-market rebounds where past losers (high-$\beta$ stocks) rally disproportionately and past winners (low-$\beta$ defensives) lag the recovery, so the cross-sectional ranking is about to invert. The cross-section's term structure rises sharply (the average stock is up $+1\%$ over the prior 1 month, $+11\%$ over 6 months, and $+27\%$ over 12 months), with recovery returns concentrated at the long horizon. Dispersion is the highest of any cluster at both medium and long horizons ($0.56$ and $0.85$), with stocks fanning out as some rebound and others lag. This dispersion shape echoes \citet{LeeSwaminathan2000}'s lifecycle reading of momentum signals evolving from continuation to reversal as information ages.

The model inverts the ranking on its own. It buys the laggards (long leg z-mean $-0.29$, raw picks $-3\%$ to $+1\%$ while the broader cross-section is up double digits), betting that names that have not yet rebounded will catch up. The cluster has the highest intra-cluster basket turnover (Jaccard $0.68$), reflecting the dynamic rotation as recovery candidates change. Cluster 3 is heavy in 2021 (9 of 31 months, the post-COVID rotation) and 2023.

The novelty here is twofold. First, the model exploits the D\&M crash mechanism as a feature rather than treating it as a risk to be scaled away. \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} reduce position size during crash-prone periods and \citet{BarrosoSantaClara2015} apply unconditional realised-volatility targeting that mechanically reduces exposure when post-crisis volatility is elevated. M2 instead keeps full exposure and reverses the selection direction, earning $1.21$ Sharpe on the same months that traditional momentum loses on. Second, the cluster identifies a previously unexplored selection regime, the dispersed-recovery state, where the optimal long leg sits below the cross-section average at \emph{every} horizon, not only at recent ones. This is qualitatively distinct from the short-horizon reversal of \citet{Jegadeesh1990} (one-month) and \citet{Lehmann1990} (weekly).

\paragraph{Cluster 4 (deep crisis): an active panic with the whole market falling.}
Sustained-stress months: current $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}=0.81$ (the highest of any cluster) and prior-6-month $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ frequency $0.51$ (also the highest). Critically, the prior-12-month $\pi$ frequency is only $0.30$, in line with the test-period average. These are sharp, short panics rather than prolonged year-long deteriorations, exactly the conditions in which the rebound thesis is most likely to pay off. The cross-section is uniformly negative: the average stock is down $5\%$ over the prior 1 month, $11\%$ over 6 months, and $7\%$ over 12 months. Dispersion is the lowest of any cluster at both medium and long horizons ($0.35$ and $0.45$), with cross-section skewness at short horizons also the lowest ($3.67$). Stocks are moving down together rather than fanning out.

Within that broad decline the model picks the most-beaten names. The long-leg z-curve sits between $-0.73$ and $-0.88$ across all 12 horizons and raw pick losses span $-15\%$ at month 1 to $-48\%$ at month 12. This is a term-structure-wide reversal portfolio with no direct analogue in the reversal literature. \citet{Jegadeesh1990} documents short-term reversal at the one-month horizon and \citet{Lehmann1990} at weekly horizons, with \citet{Nagel2012} showing that returns to short-term reversal spike during crises as compensation for liquidity provision. \citet{DeBondtThaler1985} document long-term contrarian reversal at 3--5 year horizons. Each targets a single horizon band, and cluster 4's uniform negative loading at every monthly horizon from 1 to 12 matches none of them.

The leg-beta amplification across the $K=4$ partition (Section~\ref{sec:cluster_triangulation}) is the cross-sectional realisation of \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}'s crash mechanism: high-$\beta$ losers in a falling market earn the rebound premium that defeats unconditional momentum. The novel claim is that the regime signal makes this loadable as a strategy. Mean monthly return $+3.13\%$, the highest of any cluster, is the reward for taking the contrarian bet during sustained sell-offs. That $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$'s SHAP share is \emph{lowest} in this cluster ($0.24$ combined) reflects the routing structure: once the model is in the deep-crisis branch, momentum splits alone produce the loser portfolio. Eleven of 21 months are in 2022 (Fed-hiking cycle), with corroborating instances at April 2020 (COVID trough), October 2015 and January 2016 (China devaluation, oil collapse), and January 2023.

\paragraph{Asymmetry across the two tiers}
Within the above-average tier (clusters 1 and 2), the two sub-clusters earn essentially identical Sharpes (point-estimate difference $0.01$, two-sided permutation $p = 0.99$): the strategy's performance is insensitive to the magnitude or shape of the long leg's positive deviation from the cross-section. Within the below-average tier (clusters 3 and 4), the same comparison yields a point-estimate difference of $0.61$ ($p = 0.50$, suggestive but not significant): the depth of the reversal materially affects performance. The model's regime-aware adaptation reveals itself in distinguishing kinds of reversal environments rather than kinds of trend-following environments. This asymmetry is consistent with the leg-beta finding presented in Section~\ref{sec:cluster_triangulation}: in the above-average tier, both sub-clusters reflect ordinary momentum continuation with similar market-exposure profiles; in the below-average tier, cluster 4 represents the high-$\beta$ deep-reversal selection that drives the cross-leg spread amplification.

\subsubsection{Three Lenses on the K=4 Partition}\label{sec:cluster_triangulation}

This subsection cross-validates the $K=4$ partition through three complementary lenses: the legs' market-beta exposure, $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$'s SHAP share, and the short-leg pick characteristics. Each lens decomposes a regime-aggregate finding from Section~\ref{sec:regime_results} across the four clusters.

\paragraph{Triangulation with leg-betas (parallel to D\&M)}
The leg-beta amplification documented at the regime level in Section~\ref{sec:regime_results} decomposes across the $K=4$ partition as a direct contrast against unconditional 12-month momentum (Table~\ref{tab:cluster_k4_leg_betas}). M2 retains long-leg $\beta >$ short-leg $\beta$ in every cluster (cluster spreads $+0.68$, $+0.40$, $+0.49$, $+0.43$ for clusters 1--4), while unconditional 12-month momentum exhibits leg-beta inversion in every cluster (spreads $-0.24$, $-0.87$, $-0.75$, $-0.42$). The contrast is sharpest in cluster 4, where M2's spread $+0.43$ mirrors fixed momentum's $-0.42$: in the cluster where unconditional ranking would fail most severely, M2 selects high-$\beta$ names whose recent drawdowns reflect market exposure rather than fundamental deterioration. This sharpens the M2-vs-D\&M comparison of Section~\ref{sec:dm_comparison}: where D\&M observed inversion in fixed momentum during specific stress episodes, the $K=4$ decomposition shows the inversion pattern across every cluster type while M2 escapes it everywhere through compositional re-ranking.

\input{tables/table_cluster_k4_leg_betas}

\paragraph{State-dependent role of $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$}
The aggregate $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ SHAP share documented at the regime level decomposes across the $K=4$ clusters (Table~\ref{tab:cluster_k4_shap_shares}). Combined long-and-short shares are $0.50$ in cluster 1, $0.46$ in cluster 2, $0.54$ in cluster 3, and $0.24$ in cluster 4. The long-leg-only contributions follow the same ordering: $0.34$, $0.27$, $0.28$, $0.11$. Weighted across the four clusters, the combined share recovers the overall figure of $0.46$ reported in Section~\ref{sec:regime_results}. The pattern matches the routing interpretation introduced in Section~\ref{sec:tree_paths}: $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$'s marginal value is highest in clusters where the cross-section is ambiguous (clusters 1--3, where the picks sit near or moderately above/below the cross-section), and lowest where the cross-section itself is unambiguously crisis-shaped (cluster 4). When the long-leg z-curve is uniformly between $-0.7$ and $-0.9$ across all twelve horizons, momentum splits alone suffice to make the selection; the regime signal contributes only marginal additional information. $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ is therefore a router whose marginal contribution scales with cross-sectional ambiguity, not a behaviour switch with constant influence.

\paragraph{The short leg is not a mirror of the long leg}
The $K=4$ partition was defined on long-leg z-curves; applying the same labels to the short leg (NYSE P10 of $score_{\text{xgb}}$, identical construction to the appendix leg-beta analysis) reveals that the short basket is structurally distinct from a negated long-leg in every cluster (Table~\ref{tab:cluster_k4_short_leg}). For each cluster we measure the correlation between the short-leg cluster centroid z-curve and the negated long-leg cluster centroid z-curve. A perfect ``mirror'' would yield correlation $+1.00$; the observed values are $+0.94$ in cluster 1, $+0.89$ in cluster 2, $+0.19$ in cluster 3, and $-0.84$ in cluster 4. The above-average tier (clusters 1 and 2) is roughly mirror-shaped at the long-horizon end with attenuation; the below-average tier breaks the mirror entirely. In cluster 3, the short leg sits above the cross-section average and the long leg sits below it: short-leg z-curve uniformly between $+0.18$ and $+0.37$, long-leg uniformly between $-0.22$ and $-0.36$. This is consistent with a healing market in which both buy and short candidates carry their own selection signal and the model's edge comes from relative selection within an upward-drifting cross-section. In cluster 4, the legs are anti-mirror: short-leg z spikes to $+0.57$ at month 1 and decays to $+0.11$ at month 12, while the long leg sits between $-0.73$ and $-0.88$ throughout. The month-1 short-leg spike replicates the asymmetric short-side sensitivity documented at the regime aggregate (\citet{Nagel2012} short-term reversal mechanism) and localises it to the deep-crisis cluster: in active panic with broadly negative momentum, the model shorts stocks whose recent (one-month) returns have started to recover, anticipating that those bounces are unsustained. Short-leg basket sizes are $391$ / $334$ / $382$ / $353$ stocks per month for clusters 1--4, all smaller than the corresponding long-leg baskets ($498$ / $530$ / $488$ / $533$).

\paragraph{Why the model needs $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$}
The three triangulations together upgrade the regime-aggregate evidence of Section~\ref{sec:regime_results} from ``$\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ matters on average'' to ``the model would not produce the $K=4$ cluster structure without $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$''. Unconditional 12-month momentum exhibits leg-beta inversion in every cluster while M2 escapes it in every cluster, the regime signal's cluster-level SHAP share spans $0.24$ to $0.54$ in proportion to cross-sectional ambiguity, and the long-leg z-curves swing from continuation in cluster 1 to uniform deep reversal in cluster 4. No fixed-rank or single-direction model can produce both shapes.

\subsubsection{Horizon-level Evidence}\label{sec:horizon_evidence}

The SHAP distribution by momentum horizon, taken at the regime level rather than per cluster, characterises which horizons drive the model's predictions. In the calm long leg, months 8--12 together account for $66.0\%$ of long-leg momentum SHAP, with months 11 and 12 the most influential individual horizons ($18.6\%$ and $16.8\%$). In the panic long leg, months 7--12 account for $67.2\%$, so roughly two-thirds of the prediction signal sits at the intermediate-to-long end in both regimes, mildly shifted toward the shorter end during panic. On the panic short side, month 1 SHAP rises to $10.6\%$ versus $5.3\%$ in calm: the model becomes more sensitive to recent returns when identifying stocks to short during stress.

\subsubsection{Discussion}\label{sec:novelty}

Prior work has established that momentum profits depend on market state \citep{Cooper2004}, that momentum crashes are tied to rebounds after stress \citep{DanielMoskowitz2016}, and that blending fast and slow momentum signals across market cycles can improve performance \citep{GHM2023}. Each cluster paragraph above places its specific finding within this prior literature; together, the four clusters establish three contributions that go beyond these results: (i) a term-structure-wide reversal portfolio in deep crisis with no direct analogue in the existing reversal literature, (ii) a regime signal whose marginal value scales with cross-sectional ambiguity rather than acting as a constant feature, and (iii) selection by momentum \emph{level} rather than momentum \emph{rank}, producing portfolios that no fixed-rank strategy could replicate.

That nonlinear models outperform linear ones in cross-sectional asset pricing is well established \citep{Gu2020}. What is specific here is \emph{where} the nonlinearity matters: in transforming a macrofinancial regime probability into a conditioning variable for multi-horizon momentum selection. Section~\ref{sec:nonlinearity} provides structural evidence that the nonlinearity is not generic feature interaction but specifically the ability of $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ to act as a context switch.

\subsection{Nonlinearity and Feature Interactions}\label{sec:nonlinearity}

\subsubsection{Tree Path Analysis}\label{sec:tree_paths}

The per-horizon decomposition in Section~\ref{sec:regime_results} treats each horizon independently, leaving open whether the model relies on specific \emph{interactions} between horizons. To test this, 5{,}000 sampled stocks from each regime$\times$leg group are traced through 50{,}000 individual trees, recording which momentum horizon groups each decision path splits on (Appendix~\ref{app:combo_analysis}). Horizons are grouped into four bands: short (S, months 1--3), medium (M, months 4--6), intermediate (I, months 7--9), and long (L, months 10--12). Table~\ref{tab:combo_freq} reports the frequency of each of the 15 possible combinations; each column sums to 100\%.

\input{tables/table_combo_freq}

The combination frequencies are stable across all four groups: I{+}L accounts for 13--15\% of paths, M{+}I{+}L for 10--11\%, and S{+}M for 7--8\%. No unexpected interaction emerges. The distribution matches what the per-horizon SHAP decomposition would predict: horizons with higher SHAP weight appear more frequently in tree paths and combine in proportion to their individual importance.

The model's value does not come from complex momentum-momentum interactions that differ across regimes; the horizon combinations are regime-invariant. It comes from $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ at the routing step. Table~\ref{tab:combo_long_cp} in Appendix~\ref{app:combo_analysis} quantifies this: the calm-minus-panic z-score difference is positive for all 39 long-leg entries, with the largest shifts at the intermediate horizon ($+$0.36 to $+$0.55).

\subsubsection{Tree Depth and Feature Interactions}\label{sec:depth_analysis}

Tree depth provides direct evidence for why multi-dimensional conditioning is required (Figure~\ref{fig:depth_sharpe}). Annualised return rises monotonically from depth~1 (8.7\%, no feature interactions) through depth~4 (21.7\%), with the largest gains at depths~2 and~3 where the model first gains multi-way conditioning. Volatility is nearly constant across depths ($\approx$19.5\%): deeper trees improve signal quality rather than amplifying risk-taking. A depth-2 model, which conditions on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ and one momentum horizon per tree, achieves 15.7\%, which is 28\% below the full model. Reading the term-structure shape requires comparing multiple horizons simultaneously, which only becomes possible at depth~$\geq 3$. Performance peaks at depth~4 and declines beyond depth~5.

The $K=4$ cluster decomposition (Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity_picks}) provides a specific account: distinguishing the four multi-horizon shapes within a $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$-conditional tree branch requires depth~1 for the regime gate plus at least depth~3 for within-regime shape reading. Depth 4 is exactly that budget.

\subsection{Risk Aversion and Stress Testing}\label{sec:risk_aversion}

\subsubsection{Risk-Aversion Control}

An investor may prefer lower portfolio risk at the cost of some expected return. We incorporate a risk-aversion parameter $\gamma$ into the strategy and trace the out-of-sample trade-off across $\gamma \in [0, 1]$.

The natural starting point is the mean-variance utility $r - \frac{\gamma}{2}\sigma^2$ \citep{Markowitz1952}, but it is the wrong lens for this problem for two reasons. First, mechanically, applying the penalty as a scoring function in a long-short construction produces a betting-against-beta portfolio: the penalty is subtracted symmetrically from every stock's score, which pushes the long book toward low-volatility names and the short book toward high-volatility names. Realised portfolio volatility then rises rather than falls as $\gamma$ grows. Appendix~\ref{app:alt_targets} confirms this pattern across several mean-variance variants. Second, economically, mean-variance penalises variance in \emph{absolute} terms: a stock at $\sigma = 30\%$ is penalised nine times more than one at $\sigma = 10\%$, and the penalty's importance scales with portfolio wealth. Real investors behave as if their risk aversion is \emph{relative} rather than absolute, with a stable tolerance for proportional risk that does not change with wealth level. Constant relative risk aversion captures this; mean-variance does not.

We therefore adopt a CRRA utility that penalises $\log\sigma$ instead of $\sigma^2$, and evaluate each stock's utility separately under a long direction and a short direction. The volatility penalty enters identically in both: a volatile stock is equally unattractive as a holding or as a short position, so the investor's aversion to risk does not flip sign with trading direction. Longs are the top decile of long-utility across the full cross-section; shorts are the top decile of short-utility. The full specification is given in Appendix~\ref{app:crra_construction}.

Figure~\ref{fig:risk_aversion} shows the resulting Pareto curve.\footnote{The $\gamma=0$ baseline reflects the CRRA dual-utility implementation rather than the headline M2 scoring; small numerical differences from Section~\ref{sec:performance_results} (volatility $19.50\%$, maximum drawdown $-22.8\%$) are within seed noise.} At $\gamma = 0.1$, Sharpe stays at 1.01 while maximum drawdown tightens from $-25\%$ to $-18\%$, a near-free reduction in tail risk. At $\gamma = 0.5$, annualised volatility halves from 19.7\% to 11.2\%, drawdown halves to $-12\%$, and Sharpe settles at 0.78. At $\gamma = 1.0$, volatility falls to 5.2\% (roughly a quarter of the market's) and Sharpe remains positive at 0.46. An investor can choose any point on this curve based on their volatility budget or drawdown tolerance. Beyond $\gamma \approx 1.5$, the risk penalty dominates the return signal and the long-short spread collapses into dollar-neutral noise. The framework does not blow up; it simply stops producing a directional bet.

\subsubsection{Stress Test: Prolonged Bear Market}\label{sec:stress_test}

The reversal mechanism documented in Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity_picks} (cluster 4) rests on a specific premise: that price declines during panic are temporary (driven by liquidity pressure) rather than permanent (driven by economic deterioration). The historical stress periods in the test sample support this: M2 earned $+29.9\%$ during the COVID crash (February--April 2020) and $+21.5\%$ during the 2022 bear market (January--October 2022). In both cases, the decline was temporary and reversed within months.

But the premise can fail. In a sustained economic deterioration (a depression or structural decline), beaten-down stocks fall because their businesses are failing, not because of temporary mispricing. We call this \emph{reversal failure}: the regime classification is correct, but the momentum structure within that regime has changed. To quantify this vulnerability, we simulate recessions of increasing duration, replacing consecutive months with M2's average losing panic-month return ($-$3.44\% per month) at every possible insertion point.

\input{tables/table_stress_scenarios}

The strategy can absorb substantial downturns. A 12-month recession produces a worst-case drawdown of $-$49\%, after which calm-period continuation resumes. Only a depression-length downturn of 24 months produces a drawdown of $-$69\%. This mirrors the vulnerability identified by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} for traditional momentum, but on the opposite side: standard momentum crashes when winners stop winning; the regime-conditioned strategy would struggle when losers stop reverting. The hybrid approach combining stock selection with exposure scaling (Section~\ref{sec:future_work}) is designed for this contingency. Section~\ref{sec:historical_stress} provides direct historical evidence on how the strategy actually behaved during the dot-com bust and the global financial crisis, complementing this synthetic stress test.

\subsubsection{Historical Stress: Out-of-Sample Evidence}\label{sec:historical_stress}

The simulation in Section~\ref{sec:stress_test} estimates worst-case behaviour under synthetic recessions. To complement it with direct historical evidence, the full pipeline (HMM + XGBoost) is re-estimated annually from 1995 onward, with each year's prediction made by a model trained only on data available through the prior year-end. This produces a 30-year out-of-sample monthly return series spanning 1995--2024 that includes the dot-com bust, the global financial crisis, and the 2020 COVID episode. Methodology details are in Appendix~\ref{app:expanding_window}; the rest of this subsection focuses on how the strategy actually behaved when stress arrived.

\input{tables/table_expanding_subperiods}

\paragraph{Worst observed stress: the dot-com bust}
The 2000--2002 dot-com bust produced the strategy's largest historical drawdown of $-64.8\%$ peak-to-trough. Restricted to the bear-market proper (March 2000--October 2002), the strategy lost $-43.8\%$ cumulative over 32 months (Sharpe $-0.39$). This is the historical realisation of the reversal-failure vulnerability described in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}: a prolonged sector-rotating bear in which beaten-down stocks did not promptly recover, so the panic-regime reversal selection bled. The strategy survived the episode and resumed positive returns thereafter (the 2003--2006 sub-sample produces Sharpe 0.70), but the realised drawdown is severe.

\paragraph{Stress at the targeted failure mode: the 2009 momentum crash}
The single most informative episode is March--December 2009, the period \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} document as a severe momentum crash. Under the same long-short construction used throughout this thesis (NYSE breakpoints, value-weighted, 10\,bps cost), unconditional 12-month momentum lost $-62.6\%$ cumulative during those ten months. Under expanding-window out-of-sample, the regime-conditional strategy gained $+20.5\%$ cumulative (Sharpe 0.73) during the same window. Across the full GFC episode (October 2007 -- June 2009) the strategy returned $+22.8\%$ cumulative (Sharpe 0.48). This is direct historical evidence that the regime mechanism survives the specific stress event that defeats unconditional momentum.

\paragraph{Synthetic and historical envelopes agree}
The synthetic stress test's worst-case bound ($-70\%$ drawdown at a 24-month synthetic recession; Section~\ref{sec:stress_test}) contains the realised dot-com drawdown of $-64.8\%$, so the simulation does not understate the historical risk.

\paragraph{Long-horizon survival}
Aggregating across all 30 years and all stress events, the strategy produces $+1{,}405\%$ cumulative return with a Sharpe of 0.50. Each historical drawdown is followed by recovery; no stress episode in the sample is unrecoverable. Sub-period samples are small enough that point estimates carry wide confidence intervals, so the qualitative pattern across periods is more informative than the levels.

\subsubsection{Comparison with \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}}\label{sec:dm_comparison}

On the same data and with the same transaction costs, M2 (Sharpe 1.11, CAPM alpha 23.4\%, $t = 4.08$) outperforms the D\&M managed momentum strategy (Sharpe 0.49, CAPM alpha 10.3\%, $t = 1.86$).\footnote{The CAPM alpha is used here rather than the six-factor alpha (24.1\%, $t = 4.81$) reported elsewhere so that M2 and D\&M are compared under the same one-factor specification used throughout the \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} evaluation. The two alphas differ by 0.9 percentage points for M2 because controlling for the momentum factor (UMD) and the FF5 factors leaves the cross-sectional-regime alpha essentially unchanged.} The two approaches use regime information differently: D\&M scale portfolio \emph{exposure} based on predicted crash risk (how much to hold), while M2 changes \emph{which stocks} are held by selecting from the opposite tail of the cross-sectional distribution (what to hold).

A natural extension is to combine the two: use M2's stock selection for portfolio construction and D\&M-style exposure scaling as a circuit breaker. Adding D\&M-style scaling on top of M2 does not improve performance in the current test period, because the condition it guards against (a sustained reversal failure where beaten-down stocks never recover) did not occur. Although the test period contains panic streaks of up to 15 consecutive months, M2 never loses more than 3 consecutive panic months. The stress test above (Table~\ref{tab:stress_scenarios}) shows that only a depression-length downturn of 24 months or more would produce drawdowns severe enough to justify exposure scaling. The hybrid approach remains a valuable contingency for deployment in environments where such prolonged deterioration is possible (Section~\ref{sec:future_work}). The $K=4$ cluster decomposition in Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity_picks} sharpens the complementarity: D\&M-style scaling would help most in cluster 4 (deep crisis), where the long leg sits term-structure-wide negative and the reversal bet is most exposed to failure; in the above-average baskets (clusters 1 and 2) it would unnecessarily reduce positions because the cross-section there resembles calm and the strategy continues to earn a calm-like Sharpe.

\subsection{International Robustness}\label{sec:international}

To test whether the cross-sectional regime-momentum mechanism extends beyond US data, we replicate the full pipeline on UK and Japanese equities (Compustat Global, 1990--2025).\footnote{Each region's HMM is fit on locally-selected stress features (DD+VOL+REL\_N for both, with BANK\_REL replacing the US-specific Moody's BAA-AAA spread in the candidate pool); details in Appendix~\ref{app:intl_feature_selection}. The cross-sectional model is unchanged from the US specification, except that long and short legs are formed from unconditional decile breakpoints across all listed stocks rather than NYSE-equivalent breakpoints (no analogous tier exists for either market).}

\input{tables/table_international_results}

Both regions support the mechanism. UK M2 attains a Sharpe of 0.84 over 2011--2025, well above unconditional 12-month momentum's 0.45 and the local market's 0.62. JP M2 attains 0.50, substantially above unconditional momentum's 0.07 but below the JP market's 0.86, consistent with the documented weakness of Japanese momentum \citep{Asness2013}. Maximum drawdown is reduced from $-$62.6\% (fixed momentum) to $-$21.4\% in UK, and from $-$66.3\% to $-$19.8\% in JP.

Taken together, the results show that momentum's cross-sectional predictability shifts between continuation and reversal across market regimes, and that exploiting this shift requires nonlinear conditioning on a regime signal. The signal's value lies not in predicting returns directly but in conditioning how the model jointly uses the full momentum term structure, a multi-dimensional interaction only tree-based models can learn.
