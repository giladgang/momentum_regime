\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

Before presenting the results, it is worth reiterating the thesis's primary objective: the goal is not to construct a strategy that maximises out-of-sample returns, but to understand how the regime signal interacts with momentum and what this interaction reveals about the structure of the anomaly. Portfolio performance is reported as a vehicle for this analysis, not as an end in itself.

All strategies are evaluated out-of-sample on the test period (January 2011 to November 2025, 167 months). Each method produces a long-only, value-weighted, top-decile portfolio rebalanced monthly with one-way transaction costs of 10 basis points (i.e., 10 basis points charged on each buy or sell trade). Portfolio construction details and performance metric definitions are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:portfolio}. Feature importance is assessed via SHAP values \citep{Lundberg2017} for XGBoost and coefficient analysis for logistic regression; the methodological details are in Appendix~\ref{app:feature_importance}.

The analysis proceeds in four stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Section~\ref{sec:performance_results} compares the overall performance of all strategies.
    \item Section~\ref{sec:regime_results} decomposes performance by market regime, revealing that the main benefit of the regime-conditioned models lies in calm-period stock selection rather than crash avoidance.
    \item Section~\ref{sec:decomposition} isolates the contributions of lookback flexibility, fundamental characteristics, and the regime signal $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ to the observed Sharpe ratio improvement, supported by coefficient analysis, SHAP values, and ablation studies. It establishes the central insight that $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ functions as a context variable governing which momentum horizons are informative in each market state.
    \item Section~\ref{sec:risk_aversion} examines how varying the investor's risk aversion in the model's training objective affects portfolio outcomes and the regime signal's importance.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Portfolio Performance}\label{sec:performance_results}

Table~\ref{tab:performance} reports out-of-sample performance for all strategies. The regime-conditioned methods (M1 and M2) substantially outperform both the market benchmark and the unconditional momentum baselines.

\input{tables/table_performance}

The logistic regression (M1) achieves a Sharpe ratio of 1.03 with the lowest annualised volatility among all active strategies (14.5\%), matching the market's volatility while delivering 14.8\% annualised returns versus 11.9\% for the market. Its maximum drawdown of $-$25.2\% is comparable to the market's $-$24.7\%, indicating effective downside protection. Importantly, M1's Sharpe ratio is statistically significant: the Newey--West $t$-statistic for its excess return over the market is 2.92 ($p < 0.01$), and the block bootstrap 95\% confidence interval on its Sharpe ratio ($[0.55, 1.61]$) excludes zero.

The XGBoost regressor (M2) generates the highest raw returns at 21.6\% annualised, with a Sharpe ratio of 1.01. Relative to M1, it trades higher returns for higher volatility (21.8\%), but its maximum drawdown of $-$26.1\% remains well below the fixed momentum and M0 strategies (which suffer drawdowns of $-$29.7\% to $-$36.5\%). M2's Newey--West $t$-statistic is 2.78 ($p < 0.01$), confirming that its outperformance is not a statistical artefact.

The deterministic formula (M0), which uses $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ only to select a momentum lookback horizon, achieves a Sharpe of 0.73. While this modestly exceeds fixed 12-month momentum (0.70), it falls well short of the machine learning methods. The formula cannot capture the nonlinear interactions between the regime signal and the full feature set that M1 and M2 exploit.

The GHM benchmark strategies, which use backward-looking market return momentum to classify regimes, achieve Sharpe ratios between 0.70 and 0.80. The best GHM variant (DYN, Sharpe 0.80) is outperformed by both M1 and M2, suggesting that real-time stress indicators (volatility, return dispersion, drawdown, market participation) provide a richer regime signal than trailing market returns alone.

\subsection{Regime-Conditional Performance}\label{sec:regime_results}

Table~\ref{tab:regime_sharpe} decomposes performance by market regime. A striking pattern emerges: all strategies perform substantially better during panic months than during calm months. The market itself achieves a Sharpe ratio of 1.15 in panic versus 0.67 in calm, and even unconditional 12-month momentum achieves a Sharpe of 1.25 during panic compared to only 0.38 during calm.

\input{tables/table_regime_sharpe}

This pattern arises because the HMM classifies months based on the \emph{level} of stress indicators, not on the sign of contemporaneous returns.\footnote{For example, in March 2009 the S\&P 500 returned +8.8\%, yet the HMM classified this month as panic because drawdown, volatility, and return dispersion remained deeply elevated. Such recovery months contribute strongly positive returns to the panic-period Sharpe ratio.} Market drawdown (DD), the dominant feature, measures how far the market stands below its 12-month peak. After a sharp decline, the market may rally strongly, yet DD remains deeply negative until a new peak is reached, which can take many months of positive returns. Similarly, volatility and return dispersion remain elevated during early recoveries. As a result, many months classified as ``panic'' are actually recovery months with strong positive returns, occurring while the stress indicators have not yet normalised. All long-only strategies benefit from these rebound months, explaining why panic-period Sharpe ratios are uniformly high.

The regime-conditioned methods (M1 and M2) do achieve modestly higher panic-period Sharpes (1.43--1.45) than unconditional momentum (1.24--1.25), reflecting better stock selection during recoveries. However, the economically meaningful gap lies in calm periods: unconditional momentum strategies achieve Sharpe ratios of only 0.38--0.42 during the 112 calm months that constitute two-thirds of the test period, while M1 and M2 achieve 0.77--0.80. Since the portfolio spends most of its life in calm markets, this improvement drives the bulk of the overall Sharpe gain.

The two regimes select different types of stocks. In calm periods, the models favour large, profitable firms with strong intermediate-horizon momentum (8--11 months) and attractive valuations -- essentially high-quality momentum winners. Short-term return signals receive little weight, because the cross-sectional spread is compressed and recent price moves are noisy. In panic periods, the stock selection shifts: 1-month reversal becomes the dominant momentum signal, the models tilt toward recently beaten-down names poised for recovery, and fundamental screens (return on equity, book-to-market) increase in importance as quality differentiation widens during stress. This regime-dependent rotation in stock characteristics is invisible to unconditional momentum strategies that apply the same lookback horizon regardless of market conditions, and explains why their calm-period performance is so much weaker. The detailed mechanism behind this rotation is explored in Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}.

This finding contrasts with the momentum crash literature, which frames regime detection as a tool for \emph{reducing} exposure during downturns \citep{DanielMoskowitz2016, BarrosoSantaClara2015}. Reducing exposure during panic months, when all strategies achieve their highest Sharpe ratios (1.24--1.45), would have been counterproductive in this sample. The regime signal's value lies elsewhere: improving stock selection during the calm periods that constitute two-thirds of the test period.

\subsection{Decomposing the Sources of Improvement}\label{sec:decomposition}\label{sec:feature_results}\label{sec:ablation}\label{sec:context_results}

The ten strategies evaluated in this thesis differ along three dimensions: the momentum lookback structure (single horizon versus multiple horizons), the inclusion of fundamental characteristics, and the use of the regime signal $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$. This section proceeds in three steps. First, it uses Sharpe ratio comparisons to isolate the contribution of each component. Second, it examines the feature-level evidence (logistic regression coefficients and XGBoost SHAP values) to explain \emph{why} each component matters. Third, it uses ablation studies to test the robustness of the regime signal and connect the findings to the existing literature.

\subsubsection{Step 1: Isolating the Three Components}

\paragraph{Lookback flexibility}
Comparing fixed 12-month momentum (calm Sharpe 0.38) with M0 (calm Sharpe 0.41) isolates the effect of using $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ to select a single lookback horizon, without fundamentals. The improvement is negligible. The GHM DYN strategy, which blends two momentum horizons based on backward-looking market returns, achieves a calm Sharpe of 0.50, a modest improvement but far short of M1 and M2. Lookback flexibility alone, without fundamental characteristics, does not substantially improve calm-period stock selection.

\paragraph{Fundamental characteristics}
The large jump occurs from GHM DYN (calm Sharpe 0.50) to M1 (calm Sharpe 0.80). M1 includes the regime signal but, as shown below, effectively ignores it. The improvement therefore comes almost entirely from fundamental characteristics acting as a quality screen: among stocks with strong trailing momentum, M1 selects those that are also profitable, reasonably valued, and not overleveraged. In calm markets, where the raw momentum signal is noisy and the spread between winners and losers is compressed, this quality filter is especially valuable.

\paragraph{The regime signal}
Comparing M1 (calm Sharpe 0.80) with M2 (calm Sharpe 0.77) isolates the marginal contribution of using $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ within a nonlinear model. The Sharpe ratio does not improve -- M2 is, if anything, marginally lower than M1 in both calm and panic periods. What the regime signal does change is the \emph{return profile}: M2 generates 21.6\% annualised returns versus M1's 14.8\%, offset by higher volatility (21.8\% versus 14.5\%). Over 14 years, \$1 invested in M2 grows to \$14.70, more than double M1's \$6.80.

\paragraph{Interim summary}
Fundamental characteristics, not the regime signal, drive the risk-adjusted improvement. But the regime signal enables substantially higher absolute returns. The next two steps explain why.

\subsubsection{Step 2: Feature-Level Evidence}

To understand why fundamentals improve the Sharpe ratio and why the regime signal affects only the return profile, this step examines which features each model actually uses.

\paragraph{What the logistic regression relies on}

Table~\ref{tab:lr_coef} reports the standardised logistic regression coefficients. The dominant features are log market equity ($\hat{\beta} = 0.072$), 11-month momentum ($0.053$), and book-to-market ($0.046$) -- large, attractively valued momentum winners. Among the 12 momentum horizons, the model loads most heavily on intermediate lookbacks (mom$_{8}$ and mom$_{11}$), capturing the classic momentum continuation effect where stocks that have trended upward over the past 8--11 months tend to keep rising. The coefficients on 1-month momentum ($-0.016$) and 12-month momentum ($-0.041$) are both negative, reflecting two distinct phenomena: at the short end, last month's winners tend to reverse as temporary price pressure unwinds;\footnote{Short-term reversal at the monthly frequency was first documented by \citet{Jegadeesh1990}.} at the long end, stocks that have risen for a full year tend to mean-revert as the trend becomes overextended.\footnote{Long-horizon mean reversion in stock prices was established by \citet{DeBondtThaler1985}.} The positive momentum signal therefore lives in the intermediate range, flanked by negative effects at both extremes. In aggregate, the sum of absolute momentum coefficients ($\sum_{k=1}^{12}|\hat{\beta}_{mom_k}| = 0.22$) exceeds the sum of absolute fundamental coefficients ($0.16$), confirming that momentum remains the primary signal even in the presence of quality screens. The fundamental characteristics (gross profitability, asset growth, return on equity, earnings growth, leverage) all receive statistically significant coefficients, confirming their role as quality screens. The regime signal $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ ranks 17th out of 20 features, with a coefficient of $-0.005$ -- statistically significant ($z = -2.60$) but economically negligible.

\input{tables/table_lr_coef}

The logistic regression thus applies a single, fixed recipe: select large, profitable, attractively valued stocks with strong intermediate-horizon momentum. This recipe does not change with market conditions.

\paragraph{What XGBoost relies on}

Table~\ref{tab:shap} and Figure~\ref{fig:shap_importance} report the mean absolute SHAP values for the XGBoost model. The contrast with the logistic regression is striking: while $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ is essentially irrelevant in the linear model, it is the \emph{second most important feature} in XGBoost, trailing only aggregate momentum. Its SHAP importance increases from 0.015 during calm months to 0.020 during panic months, indicating that the model relies more heavily on the regime signal precisely when markets are stressed.

\paragraph{Why the two models diverge}

The divergence arises because $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ is not a direct return predictor -- it does not forecast whether stocks will go up or down. Rather, it functions as a \emph{context variable} that modulates which other features are informative. A linear model assigns $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ a single coefficient that must hold across all market conditions, which is near zero precisely because the signal has no unconditional predictive power. XGBoost, through its tree-based splits, conditions on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ and then branches on different momentum horizons within each regime, without requiring explicit interaction terms. The partial dependence plot (Figure~\ref{fig:pdp_pi}) confirms this: the nonlinear, non-monotonic shape shows that $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$'s effect on predicted returns depends on the values of other features, which is why it is invisible to the linear model but central to the tree-based one.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering

    \caption{Partial dependence plot of the XGBoost predicted return on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$. The nonlinear, non-monotonic shape confirms that $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ functions as a context variable rather than a linear predictor: its effect on predicted returns depends on the values of other features (particularly momentum horizons), which is why the logistic regression coefficient is near zero while XGBoost treats it as a top feature.}
    \label{fig:pdp_pi}
\end{figure}

In practical terms, the logistic regression finds one effective recipe and applies it uniformly. XGBoost uses the regime signal to switch recipes: it loads on intermediate-horizon momentum continuation in calm markets and shifts toward short-term reversal plays in panic. This regime-adaptive switching generates higher returns when the regime classification is correct, but produces a more volatile portfolio because the stock characteristics change substantially from month to month, and the panic-period selections (recently crashed names poised for recovery) are inherently more volatile than the steady quality-momentum picks that dominate in calm periods.

The fundamental characteristics (book-to-market, log market equity, return on equity) also appear among the top XGBoost features, consistent with their quality-screening role. Their SHAP values are smaller than the regime signal's, suggesting that in the nonlinear model the regime-momentum interaction dominates the quality screen in explanatory power.

\subsubsection{Step 3: Ablation Studies}

The preceding analysis establishes that XGBoost relies heavily on the HMM-based regime signal while the logistic regression does not. Two natural questions follow: is the HMM signal superior to simpler alternatives, and which of the four HMM input features drives its value?

\paragraph{HMM vs GHM vs no regime signal}

Table~\ref{tab:regime_signal_ablation} replaces $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ with the GHM market-cycle variable (a numeric encoding of the Bull/Correction/Bear/Rebound classification from trailing market returns) or removes the regime signal entirely, holding all other features and XGBoost hyperparameters fixed.

\input{tables/table_regime_signal_ablation}

The HMM signal improves the Sharpe ratio by +0.125 over the GHM cycle (1.007 vs 0.882). The GHM cycle adds nothing over no regime signal at all (0.882 vs 0.888), confirming that the backward-looking market return classification of \citet{GHM2023} does not provide useful conditioning information for a nonlinear model that already observes 12 momentum lookback horizons. The GHM cycle is essentially redundant with the momentum features themselves, since it is constructed from the same underlying market returns. The HMM signal, built from stress indicators (drawdown, volatility, dispersion, market participation) that are not spanned by cross-sectional momentum, provides genuinely complementary information.

\paragraph{Which HMM input features matter}

Table~\ref{tab:hmm_feature_ablation} tests each HMM input feature in isolation. For each variant, a single-feature HMM is estimated via Gibbs sampling (3 seeds averaged), and the resulting $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ is fed into both M1 and M2.

\input{tables/table_hmm_feature_ablation}

The logistic regression (M1) is completely insensitive to the quality of the regime signal: its Sharpe ratio varies by only 0.006 across all variants (1.032 to 1.038), confirming once more that M1 effectively ignores $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$.

XGBoost (M2) shows clear differentiation. Market drawdown (DD) alone produces a Sharpe of 0.997, nearly matching the full four-feature baseline (1.007). DD measures how far the market stands below its trailing peak, providing a clean, monotonic signal of market stress that directly captures the regime shifts that alter momentum's term structure. Return dispersion (DISP) is the second most informative single feature (0.955), capturing cross-sectional disagreement that signals changes in the correlation structure affecting momentum. Realised volatility (VOL, 0.855) and relative market participation (REL\_N, 0.810) are weaker in isolation, though their inclusion in the full model contributes a modest incremental improvement by smoothing the regime signal and reducing false classifications.

\paragraph{Relation to \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}}
The dominance of DD connects directly to the momentum crash literature. \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} show that the conditional volatility and mean of momentum returns are strongly related to past market declines, and propose scaling momentum exposure based on backward-looking volatility. The finding that DD alone nearly matches the full HMM (0.997 vs 1.007) is consistent with their insight: drawdown captures the same market stress that drives momentum crashes. However, this thesis uses DD differently. Rather than scaling overall momentum exposure, the HMM translates DD (along with VOL, DISP, and REL\_N) into a continuous regime probability that conditions \emph{which} momentum horizons XGBoost relies on. The result is not crash avoidance but regime-adaptive stock selection, which improves performance primarily during calm periods (Section~\ref{sec:regime_results}) rather than during the crashes that \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} focus on.

\paragraph{Summary}
The Sharpe ratio improvement from unconditional momentum (0.70) to the best strategy (M1, 1.03) is driven primarily by fundamental characteristics, not by the regime signal. Quality screening -- combining momentum with profitability, valuation, and size -- is the single most important source of risk-adjusted performance improvement. The regime signal $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ contributes differently: it does not improve Sharpe ratios beyond what a linear model with fundamentals achieves, but it enables a higher-return, higher-volatility portfolio by functioning as a context variable that governs which momentum horizons XGBoost uses in each market state.

\subsection{Risk Aversion and the Value of the Regime Signal}\label{sec:risk_aversion}

The preceding analysis established that the regime signal enables higher absolute returns at the cost of higher volatility, without improving risk-adjusted performance. A natural follow-up question is whether this finding depends on the investor's risk preferences. To investigate, XGBoost is retrained with a mean-variance utility target that penalises stock-level variance:
\begin{equation}
    y_t^s = r_{t+1}^s - \frac{\gamma}{2}\,\sigma_s^2,
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_s^2$ is the trailing 12-month realised variance of stock $s$'s monthly returns and $\gamma \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots, 10\}$ is the risk-aversion parameter. At $\gamma = 0$ this recovers the baseline M2 model (pure return maximisation); as $\gamma$ increases, the model learns to favour lower-volatility stocks. All other features, hyperparameters, and portfolio construction rules are held fixed.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{risk_aversion_analysis.png}
    \caption{Effect of risk aversion on XGBoost portfolio performance and feature importance. \textbf{Top row}: out-of-sample Sharpe ratio, annualised return, and annualised volatility as a function of $\gamma$. \textbf{Bottom row}: relative SHAP feature importance (normalised to 100\%) across $\gamma$ values. The regime signal $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ accounts for nearly 30\% of total importance at $\gamma = 0$ but falls to approximately 6\% at $\gamma = 10$, while momentum's share rises correspondingly.}
    \label{fig:risk_aversion}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:risk_aversion} reveals three findings. First, the risk-neutral model ($\gamma = 0$) achieves the highest Sharpe ratio (1.01). Introducing any variance penalty reduces risk-adjusted performance: the Sharpe ratio drops to 0.91 at $\gamma = 1$ and stabilises around 0.70--0.77 for $\gamma \geq 2$. Portfolio volatility falls sharply from 21.8\% to approximately 16\% by $\gamma = 1$ and remains flat thereafter, but returns fall faster, from 21.6\% to 10--12\%. The model quickly learns to avoid the most volatile stocks, but this eliminates precisely the high-conviction, regime-adaptive bets that drive M2's outperformance.

Second, and most importantly, the relative importance of the regime signal declines substantially with risk aversion. At $\gamma = 0$, $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ accounts for nearly 30\% of total SHAP importance, making it nearly as important as all momentum features combined. By $\gamma = 10$, its share falls to approximately 6\%. This pattern has a clear economic interpretation: the regime signal's value lies in enabling aggressive, regime-conditional reweighting of momentum horizons. A risk-neutral model exploits this fully, shifting between intermediate-horizon continuation and short-term reversal depending on the market state. A risk-averse model, penalised for selecting volatile stocks, cannot act on this information as aggressively, and defaults to a safer, momentum-dominated strategy that looks similar regardless of the regime.

Third, the stability of momentum's dominance across all $\gamma$ values (75--80\% of relative importance for $\gamma \geq 2$) confirms that the momentum anomaly itself is robust to risk preferences. What changes is the model's ability to \emph{condition} its use of momentum on the regime. The regime signal is most valuable precisely when the investor is willing to bear risk -- it is a tool for aggressive stock selection, not for risk reduction.
