\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

All strategies are evaluated out-of-sample on 2011--2025 (167 months), using the long-short construction described in Section~\ref{sec:stage2}. The central question is whether momentum's cross-sectional structure is regime-dependent, and whether exploiting this dependence requires nonlinear modelling. The analysis proceeds from aggregate performance (Section~\ref{sec:performance_results}), to regime-conditional decomposition and model-free confirmation (Section~\ref{sec:regime_results}), to feature importance analysis (Section~\ref{sec:shap_results}), ablation studies and benchmarking against \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results}), and finally the effect of risk aversion on the regime signal (Section~\ref{sec:risk_aversion}).


\subsection{Portfolio Performance}\label{sec:performance_results}

Table~\ref{tab:performance} reports the out-of-sample performance of all strategies.\footnote{All Sharpe ratios are computed using raw returns rather than excess returns above the risk-free rate. Since the same convention is applied to every strategy, relative comparisons are unaffected. The risk-free rate averaged roughly 2\% annualised over the 2011--2025 window, so absolute Sharpe ratios are overstated by approximately 0.05--0.10.}

\input{tables/table_performance}

The central finding is that the regime-momentum interaction is inherently nonlinear: only a model capable of learning regime-conditional feature interactions (XGBoost, M2) produces meaningful long-short returns, while a linear model with identical features (logistic regression, M1) collapses. This divergence -- not M2's absolute Sharpe level -- is the key result: it establishes that momentum's cross-sectional structure shifts across regimes in a way that cannot be captured by a single set of feature weights. M2 achieves an annualised Sharpe of 1.11 (returns 21.9\%, volatility 19.7\%, maximum drawdown $-$24.8\%), while M1 achieves $-$0.03 with identical input information. Fixed 12-month momentum is effectively dead (Sharpe 0.06, maximum drawdown $-$72.3\%), confirming the momentum crash phenomenon documented by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}. M0 (deterministic formula), which switches from 12-month to 1-month momentum based on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, achieves a Sharpe of only 0.11 -- an improvement over fixed momentum but far short of M2. M0's failure despite the clean regime-dependent IC pattern (Section~\ref{sec:ic_analysis}) reveals what simple switching misses: M0 uses a single horizon at a time, while M2 uses all 12 simultaneously and adapts their relative weights gradually as a function of the \emph{level} of $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, not just whether it exceeds a threshold. The transition between continuation and reversal is not a binary switch but a nonlinear reweighting that M0's linear interpolation cannot capture.

M2's Newey--West $t$-statistic for mean returns (1.83) is not individually significant, which is expected for a market-neutral long-short strategy with 19.7\% annualised volatility. The economically meaningful test is the factor model alpha, which is highly significant across all specifications ($t > 4$; see below). M1's $t$-statistic of $-$2.50 is significantly \emph{negative}, indicating that the linear model systematically selects the wrong stocks.

Factor model regressions confirm that M2's returns are not explained by known risk factors. The CAPM alpha is 23.8\% annualised ($t = 4.08$), and the alpha remains significant across all standard specifications: Fama--French three-factor (23.7\%, $t = 4.46$), Carhart four-factor (24.9\%, $t = 4.74$), Fama--French five-factor (23.4\%, $t = 4.56$), and the six-factor model including momentum (24.7\%, $t = 4.78$). The alpha \emph{increases} as more factors are added, because M2 has near-zero loadings on all factors: the strategy is approximately market-neutral and its returns are not repackaged exposure to value, size, profitability, investment, or momentum factors.

\subsection{Regime-Conditional Performance and Economic Mechanism}\label{sec:regime_results}

Decomposing performance by market regime reveals why M2 succeeds where other strategies fail (Table~\ref{tab:regime_sharpe}).

\input{tables/table_regime_sharpe}

M2 achieves a Sharpe ratio of 1.56 during panic months compared to 0.82 during calm months. This panic-period dominance contrasts sharply with the long-only literature, where calm-period improvement typically drives overall performance. The difference arises because long-short construction exposes a dimension of skill that long-only construction masks: the ability to correctly position the short side.

\subsubsection{Calm Months: Momentum Continuation}\label{sec:calm}

In calm markets, the standard momentum continuation effect documented by \citet{JegadeeshTitman1993} dominates: past winners continue to outperform past losers. The economic mechanism is well understood: firm-specific good news -- strong earnings, positive analyst revisions, improving fundamentals -- diffuses gradually through the market as investors update their beliefs with a delay \citep{Hong1999, Barberis1998}. A stock that has risen over the past 6--12 months is likely still in the process of being re-priced upward, and the reverse holds for declining stocks.

M2 exploits this by going long stocks with the strongest upward trends and shorting stocks with weaker momentum. The portfolio characteristics confirm this: M2's long side holds stocks with strong positive trailing momentum (+23.7\% over 12 months), moderate size (median market cap \$327M), and relatively low book-to-market ratios (0.43). The short side holds larger stocks (median \$601M) with weaker positive momentum (+8.3\% over 12 months) -- these are lagging names that have underperformed the long side, consistent with M2 betting on continued relative outperformance of strong-momentum stocks. The resulting long-short spread of 1.33\% per month is modest but consistent. Because the portfolio is approximately market-neutral ($\beta \approx 0$), this spread is not driven by broad market movements -- it reflects M2's ability to rank stocks correctly within each cross-section.

\subsubsection{Panic Months: Reversal}\label{sec:panic}

In panic months, M2 achieves a Sharpe of 1.56 because it correctly identifies a fundamentally different cross-sectional structure. The regime signal enables the model to recognise that the predictive relationship between past returns and future returns has inverted: stocks that have fallen the most -- across both short and long lookback horizons -- are now more likely to outperform, not less.

The economic basis for this inversion rests on three reinforcing mechanisms, each of which is captured by the HMM's stress indicators. First, \emph{liquidity spirals} \citep{BrunnermeierPedersen2009}: during market stress, leveraged investors face margin calls and are forced to liquidate positions regardless of fundamental value, pushing prices temporarily below intrinsic value. When the selling pressure subsides, these stocks snap back. The HMM detects this through drawdown (DD), which measures the depth of the market decline, and the credit spread (CS), which widens as corporate distress and fire-sale risk increase. Second, \emph{institutional flow pressure} \citep{VayanosWoolley2013}: mutual funds experiencing redemptions sell their holdings, creating predictable downward price pressure that reverses once outflows stabilise. Return dispersion (DISP) captures this mechanism, as forced selling fractures the cross-sectional consensus and causes individual stocks to move in divergent directions. Third, \emph{investor overreaction} \citep{DeBondtThaler1985}: panic selling reflects fear-driven extrapolation of recent losses, causing prices to overshoot on the downside. Relative market participation (REL\_N) detects the structural market damage that accompanies severe overreaction episodes -- delistings, trading halts, and elevated acquisition rates.

On the other side, stocks that held up during the decline -- typically large, low-beta, ``defensive'' names -- become temporarily overvalued as investors crowd into perceived safe havens. When risk appetite returns, capital rotates back into beaten-down names.

The portfolio characteristics confirm this inversion. In panic months, M2's long side holds beaten-down stocks with negative 12-month momentum ($-$19.5\%) and negative 1-month momentum ($-$5.2\%), smaller size (median \$238M), and higher book-to-market (0.62). The short side holds larger stocks (median \$855M) that have held up during the decline, with positive 1-month momentum (+3.7\%) -- the ``defensive'' names that the strategy bets against during recoveries.

M2 learns this regime-specific cross-sectional structure through tree splits that condition jointly on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ and the full set of momentum horizons. The model does not simply reverse the slope of any single horizon; rather, it reweights the entire 12-dimensional momentum term structure as a function of the regime signal, an interaction that cannot be decomposed into per-horizon changes. Figure~\ref{fig:regime_decomp} shows the result: during panic, M2's long side earns 4.46\% per month while its short side earns only 1.38\%, producing a spread of 3.09\% -- more than double the calm-period spread of 1.33\%. This widening reflects the model's improved ability to distinguish future outperformers from underperformers when the regime signal indicates stress. The spread comes entirely from cross-sectional stock selection (the portfolio has near-zero market beta), confirming that the regime signal adds information about the \emph{relative} prospects of individual stocks, not about the market's direction.

\subsubsection{Stress Test: Prolonged Bear Market}\label{sec:stress_test}

The regime signal's value during panic rests on a specific economic premise: that price declines are temporary (driven by liquidity pressure) rather than permanent (driven by fundamental deterioration). When the premise holds, M2 correctly identifies which stocks are temporarily dislocated and which are not, producing a panic-month long-short spread of 3.09\% per month -- evidence that the regime signal captures meaningful cross-sectional information during stress.

But the premise can fail. In a genuine economic deterioration -- a depression, a banking crisis, or a structural decline -- beaten-down stocks fall because their businesses are failing, not because of temporary mispricing. In this scenario, the regime signal still identifies the market as stressed, but the cross-sectional pattern it conditions on (reversal) no longer holds. This is what we call \emph{reversal failure}: the regime classification is correct, but the momentum structure within that regime has changed.

To answer this, we simulate recessions of increasing duration. In each simulation, a block of consecutive months is replaced with the average return M2 earns during its actual losing panic months ($-$3.51\% per month), while all other months retain their observed returns. The simulation is repeated at every possible insertion point to capture the worst-case timing.

The strategy can absorb substantial downturns. Even a 12-month recession -- roughly the length of the 2008--2009 crisis -- produces a worst-case drawdown of $-$51\%, after which the strategy recovers as calm-period continuation resumes. Appending a simulated 6-month recession at the end of the test period (i.e., extending the sample with six months of losses at $-$3.51\% per month) still leaves the overall Sharpe ratio positive, because the preceding 167 months of returns provide a sufficient buffer. Only a depression-length downturn of 24 months produces a drawdown of $-$70\%, which would be painful for any momentum strategy.

The historical stress periods in the test sample confirm the reversal mechanism: M2 earned +30.3\% during the COVID crash (February--April 2020) and +14.9\% during the 2022 bear market (January--October 2022). In both cases, the decline was temporary and reversed within months.

The scenario that would truly break the strategy -- a sustained fundamental deterioration where beaten-down stocks never recover, as in the Great Depression or Japan's lost decades -- has not occurred in the 2011--2025 test period. This mirrors the vulnerability identified by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} for traditional momentum, but on the opposite side: standard momentum crashes when winners stop winning; the regime-conditioned strategy would struggle when losers stop reverting. The hybrid approach combining stock selection with exposure scaling (Section~\ref{sec:future_work}) is designed precisely for this contingency.

\subsubsection{Model-Free Confirmation: Information Coefficient Analysis}\label{sec:ic_analysis}

A natural concern is whether the regime-dependent behaviour described above reflects genuine economic structure or model-specific overfitting. To rule this out, Table~\ref{tab:ic_rotation} examines the raw predictive power of each momentum horizon in calm versus panic months, without involving any model. The information coefficient (IC) -- the Spearman rank correlation between a momentum signal and subsequent stock returns, computed cross-sectionally each month -- directly measures whether ranking stocks by a given horizon correctly identifies future outperformers.

\input{tables/table_ic_rotation}

The pattern is striking. In calm months, all 12 horizons have positive, significant ICs ($t > 2.5$), with the strongest power at intermediate horizons ($mom_8$ through $mom_{10}$, IC $\approx 0.03$): past winners keep winning. In panic months, the pattern inverts -- short-horizon signals ($mom_1$ through $mom_3$) develop \emph{negative} ICs ($-$0.012 to $-$0.010), meaning that stocks which dropped most recently are more likely to bounce back than to keep falling. Longer horizons retain weakly positive ICs but none is individually significant ($t < 1.6$), reflecting the limited sample of 59 panic months. The key finding is the systematic \emph{contrast}: all 12 horizons are significant in calm, none in panic, and the short horizons flip sign. This confirms that the continuation-to-reversal shift M2 exploits is a real feature of the data, not an artefact of the model. It also explains why fixed momentum and M1 collapse in long-short construction: they assign static weights to each horizon, making them unable to adapt when the cross-sectional structure inverts.


\subsection{Feature Importance Decomposition}\label{sec:shap_results}

To understand how XGBoost uses each feature, we apply SHAP \citep[SHapley Additive exPlanations;][]{Lundberg2017}, a method that assigns each feature a contribution to every individual prediction based on cooperative game theory. The mean absolute SHAP value measures a feature's overall importance: a higher value means the feature has a larger average effect on the model's output (Table~\ref{tab:shap} and Figure~\ref{fig:shap_importance}).

\input{tables/table_shap}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{cs_feature_importance.png}
    \caption{Mean absolute SHAP values for the XGBoost model (M2), decomposed by calm and panic months. The regime signal $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ is the second most important feature after aggregate momentum, and all features increase in importance during panic.}
    \label{fig:shap_importance}
\end{figure}

The regime signal $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ is the second most important feature after aggregate momentum, with importance that increases from calm to panic months. This confirms that XGBoost relies more heavily on the regime signal precisely when markets are stressed, consistent with the IC analysis documented above.

An important implication is that the regime-momentum interaction cannot be decomposed into per-horizon changes. SHAP dependence analysis (examining each momentum horizon's SHAP contribution as a function of its value, separately for calm and panic) shows that individual horizons do not exhibit a clean slope reversal between regimes. Instead, the model selects stocks based on the \emph{shape} of their momentum term structure -- the pattern across all 12 horizons jointly -- conditioned on the regime signal.

Figure~\ref{fig:term_structure} makes this visible. The top panels show the cross-sectional z-score of the stocks M2 selects at each horizon: how far above or below the average stock the selected stocks are at each lookback. The bottom panels show the share of total momentum $|$SHAP$|$ at each horizon (each set sums to 100\%), measuring how the model distributes its attention across lookbacks. Three patterns emerge.

First, in calm, the long leg's z-score curve is not flat but \emph{humped}: near zero at 1-month momentum, peaking at $+0.39$ standard deviations at month~8, then declining to $+0.17$ at month~12. This shape is consistent with the known structure of momentum: the 1-month neutrality reflects short-term reversal avoidance \citep{Jegadeesh1990}, the peak at intermediate horizons matches the finding of \citet{NovyMarx2012} that months 7--12 are the primary driver of momentum profits, and the decline at month~12 is consistent with the momentum life cycle documented by \citet{LeeSwaminathan2000}. The model independently recovers these patterns from the data without any prior knowledge of the momentum literature.

Second, in panic, the long leg's z-score curve is \emph{flat} at approximately $-0.15$ standard deviations across all horizons: the model selects stocks that are uniformly below average at every lookback. This contrasts sharply with the humped calm curve and indicates that the model is not exploiting any specific horizon but rather identifying stocks that have been falling consistently -- a signature of temporary liquidity-driven dislocation rather than horizon-specific reversal. The short leg shows the opposite pattern: sharply positive at 1-month ($+0.30$), indicating that the model shorts stocks that rallied during the stress episode.

Third, the $|$SHAP$|$ distribution across horizons (bottom panels) is broadly similar in calm and panic -- the model pays attention to the same horizons in both regimes, with horizons 8--12 dominating. What changes is not \emph{which} horizons the model focuses on, but \emph{which side of the distribution} it selects from at those horizons. This distinction is critical: it means the regime signal does not reroute the model to different features but rather changes the direction of the selection conditional on the same features.

To illustrate concretely: consider two stocks with identical 12-month momentum of $+30\%$. Stock~A has $mom_1 = +5\%$, $mom_6 = +20\%$ -- a steady uptrend. Stock~B has $mom_1 = -3\%$, $mom_6 = +25\%$ -- recently stalled. In calm, the model favours Stock~A (the trend is intact). In panic, neither would be selected because the model wants stocks that are \emph{below} average. Now consider two stocks that are both down $-20\%$ over 12 months. Stock~C has $mom_1 = -15\%$ (sharp recent crash after mild earlier decline). Stock~D has $mom_1 = -3\%$ (steady, prolonged decline). In panic, the model favours Stock~C -- the sudden crash suggests a liquidity event with reversal potential, while the gradual decline suggests fundamental deterioration. These distinctions require reading the \emph{shape} across multiple horizons, not just the level at any single one.

The tree depth analysis provides direct evidence for why multi-dimensional conditioning is required (Figure~\ref{fig:depth_sharpe}). Performance increases monotonically from depth~1 (Sharpe~0.46, no feature interactions) through depth~4 (Sharpe~1.11), with the largest gains at depths~2 and~3 where the model first gains access to multi-way conditioning. A depth-2 model, which can only condition on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ and one momentum horizon per tree, achieves a Sharpe of only~0.82 -- meaningful, but 26\% below the full model. Reading the term-structure shape (distinguishing Stock~A from Stock~B, or Stock~C from Stock~D) requires comparing multiple horizons simultaneously, which is only possible at depth~$\geq 3$. Performance peaks at depth~4 and declines beyond, as overfitting to higher-order noise offsets any additional signal.

The partial dependence plot (Figure~\ref{fig:pdp_pi}) completes the picture by showing how the regime signal itself enters the model's predictions. If $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ were a conventional return predictor, the plot would show a monotonic relationship. Instead, it displays a staircase pattern, with predicted returns rising in some ranges and falling in others. Each step corresponds to a tree-split threshold where XGBoost adjusts how it jointly weighs the momentum features. This non-monotonic structure is precisely why the logistic regression assigns $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ a near-zero coefficient: a single linear slope through this staircase averages to approximately zero.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}\label{sec:ablation_results}

While SHAP measures each feature's contribution to individual predictions within the trained model, the ablation studies in this section retrain the model without specific features entirely -- testing whether the model's \emph{learning} depends on them, not just its predictions. A feature could have high SHAP importance but be redundant in an ablation if other features compensate when the model is retrained.

\paragraph{Regime signal removal}
Removing the regime signal and retraining XGBoost on the 12 momentum features alone drops the Sharpe from 1.11 to 0.41, confirming that the HMM captures decision-relevant information that the momentum features cannot compensate for even when the model is free to adapt.

\paragraph{Linear model confound test}\label{sec:target_confound}
Because M1 uses a classification target while M2 uses continuous returns, a natural concern is whether M1's failure reflects the choice of training target rather than linearity. A Ridge regression predicting continuous forward returns -- the same target as M2 -- achieves a Sharpe of $-$0.57, even worse than M1's $-$0.03 (Appendix~\ref{app:ridge}). Both linear models fail because they assign $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ a single coefficient that cannot capture the conditional logic required to adapt stock selection across regimes.

\paragraph{Regime signal alternatives}
Table~\ref{tab:regime_signal_ablation} compares XGBoost performance under three regime signal specifications: the baseline HMM $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, the GHM market-cycle variable \citep{GHM2023}, and no regime signal. The HMM signal produces the highest Sharpe ratio (1.11), exceeding the GHM cycle (0.95) and substantially exceeding the no-signal baseline (0.41). The GHM cycle does improve over no signal, but the HMM's real-time stress indicators capture regime information that trailing market returns alone cannot. All four GHM market-state strategies also collapse in long-short construction (Appendix~\ref{app:ghm}), because their deterministic blending of two horizons cannot adapt the short side to changing regimes.

\input{tables/table_regime_signal_ablation}

\paragraph{Comparison with \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}}
On the same data and with the same transaction costs, M2 (Sharpe 1.11, alpha 23.8\%, $t = 4.08$) outperforms the D\&M managed momentum strategy (Sharpe 0.49, alpha 10.3\%, $t = 1.86$).\footnote{Adding seven fundamental characteristics (book-to-market, ROE, gross profitability, leverage, asset growth, earnings growth, log market equity) to the mom+$\pi$ specification reduces the Sharpe from 1.11 to 0.98, with the loss concentrated in calm months. Fundamentals dilute the momentum signal without improving the reversal mechanism; the regime-momentum interaction, not stock quality, drives long-short performance.} The comparison illustrates the difference between two mechanisms for using regime information: D\&M scale exposure to a fixed portfolio (how much to hold), while M2 changes which stocks are held (what to hold). Adding D\&M-style scaling on top of M2 does not improve performance, suggesting that M2 already internalises the regime information D\&M uses.


\subsection{Risk Aversion and the Value of the Regime Signal}\label{sec:risk_aversion}

The preceding analysis established that the regime signal is essential for long-short momentum. A natural question is whether this finding depends on how the model's training objective balances return against risk. To test this, XGBoost is retrained under three families of risk-adjusted targets, each with varying intensity:

\centering

Two findings emerge. First, under mild risk adjustment ($r/\sigma^{0.5}$ or small $\gamma$), the regime signal's share of feature importance actually \emph{increases} (from 45\% to 57\%), while the Sharpe ratio barely changes. Mild risk penalisation removes the noisiest bets but preserves the regime-conditional structure -- the model still adapts its joint use of momentum horizons across regimes, just with less exposure to extreme outcomes.

Second, aggressive risk adjustment breaks the strategy. At $\gamma = 0.5$ the Sharpe drops to 0.75 and the regime signal's share falls to 35\%; at $a \geq 1$ the strategy collapses (Sharpe 0.36). The variance penalty overwhelms the long-short spread, steering the model toward low-volatility stocks regardless of regime and effectively abandoning the mechanism that makes M2 successful.

The implication is that detecting the regime-dependent structure of momentum requires the model to be free to learn flexible, regime-conditional feature interactions. Conservative risk objectives constrain this flexibility, forcing the model toward static low-volatility selections that obscure the very regime-dependent structure the regime signal is designed to capture.

Taken together, the results provide converging evidence that, in the sample studied, momentum's cross-sectional predictability shifts between continuation and reversal across market regimes, and that exploiting this shift requires nonlinear conditioning on a regime signal. The signal's value lies not in predicting returns directly but in conditioning how the model jointly uses the full momentum term structure -- a multi-dimensional interaction that only tree-based models can learn. The next section summarises these findings and discusses their implications.
