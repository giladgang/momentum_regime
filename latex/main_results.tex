\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

All strategies are evaluated out-of-sample on 2011--2025 (167 months), using the long-short construction described in Section~\ref{sec:stage2}. The central question is whether momentum's cross-sectional structure is regime-dependent, and whether exploiting this dependence requires nonlinear modelling.


\subsection{Portfolio Performance}\label{sec:performance_results}

\subsubsection{Strategy Comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:performance} reports the out-of-sample performance of all strategies.\footnote{All Sharpe ratios are computed using raw returns rather than excess returns above the risk-free rate. Since the same convention is applied to every strategy, relative comparisons are unaffected. The risk-free rate averaged roughly 2\% annualised over the 2011--2025 window, so absolute Sharpe ratios are overstated by approximately 0.05--0.10. Sharpe is computed from the arithmetic monthly mean annualised by $\sqrt{12}$, while annualised returns reported elsewhere are compound (geometric); under high volatility the two definitions of average return differ, so Sharpe should not be inferred from a table by dividing annualised return by annualised volatility (e.g.\ at depth~1, the reported Sharpe is $0.53$ but $8.7\% / 19.4\% = 0.45$).}

\input{tables/table_performance}

Momentum's cross-sectional predictability is regime-dependent, and exploiting this dependence requires both a probabilistic regime signal and a nonlinear model. XGB, the XGBoost regressor combined with the HMM filtered probability (mom+$\pi$), achieves an annualised Sharpe of 1.11 with a six-factor alpha of 24.1\% ($t = 4.81$; Appendix~\ref{app:factor_alphas}). Block bootstrap paired tests reject equality of Sharpe ratios between XGB and each benchmark in Table~\ref{tab:performance} at $p < 0.025$; XGB's own 95\% Sharpe confidence interval is $[0.67, 1.53]$, comfortably above zero, while every benchmark's interval straddles zero (Appendix~\ref{app:bootstrap}). An expanding-window backtest extending the OOS sample to 30 years (1995--2024) reproduces this Sharpe within sampling noise on the 167-month overlap with the main test period (0.88; see Section~\ref{sec:historical_stress}, which also covers the dot-com bust and GFC out-of-sample). Every other specification fails to produce economically meaningful long-short performance, and the pattern of failures is informative.

Unconditional 12-month momentum achieves a Sharpe of 0.06 with a maximum drawdown of $-$72.2\%, exposing the crash vulnerability documented by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} and \citet{BarrosoSantaClara2015}. A deterministic regime switch (DET) improves this marginally to 0.11. The logistic regression (LR), given identical features to XGB, collapses to $-$0.01; a Ridge regression with the same 13 features but a continuous-return target (matching XGBoost's) fails more decisively, with Sharpe $-$0.57 and a coefficient on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ of $+0.003$ (Appendix~\ref{app:ridge}). The failure is therefore linearity itself, not the classification target. Hand-engineering the missing nonlinearity into LR through $\pi^2$ and $\pi \times mom_k$ terms lifts it to 0.32, establishing that the regime-momentum interaction is real while also showing that pre-specified interactions recover less than a third of what XGBoost finds automatically. A fund-augmented variant (XGB: mom+$\pi$+fund) adds ten firm-level fundamentals to XGB's feature set; it reduces the Sharpe to 0.89 while lowering beta from 0.45 to 0.07 and tightening the maximum drawdown from $-$22.8\% to $-$18.3\%. Section~\ref{sec:fund_ablation} analyses this variant alongside several feature-set ablations, which together provide additional evidence that $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$'s role is specifically to condition the momentum term structure.

\subsubsection{Regime Signal Ablation}

The regime signal is essential. Removing $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ and retraining on the 12 momentum features alone drops the Sharpe to 0.43 (Table~\ref{tab:regime_signal_ablation}). Replacing $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ with the four raw stress indicators (DD, DISP, REL\_N, CS) performs even worse (Sharpe 0.26): with four additional dimensions the model must reconstruct the regime structure at each split, while the HMM distils those indicators into a single dimension that one tree split can condition on. The HMM's role is not to provide a convenient feature but to solve regime identification \emph{before} stock selection, freeing the cross-sectional model to use the regime signal rather than construct it. If the model cannot extract a meaningful regime signal even when given the four raw indicators directly, it certainly cannot do so from momentum features alone, which are one further step removed from the underlying regime dynamics.

\subsubsection{Feature-Set Ablation}\label{sec:fund_ablation}

The regime signal ablation shows that removing $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ reduces Sharpe by more than 60\%. A complementary question is whether adding widely used firm-level fundamentals strengthens the strategy, or whether the regime-momentum interaction is load-bearing on its own. This subsection tests both directions by adding, substituting, and removing feature blocks from the production configuration.

Ten firm-level fundamentals are tested: seven standard value-quality-size characteristics (book-to-market, return on equity, leverage, asset growth, gross profitability, earnings growth, and log market equity) and three cash-flow-based earnings-quality measures (operating cash flow over assets, free cash flow over assets, and the accrual component of earnings scaled by assets). All are computed from Compustat quarterly data with a four-month reporting lag to ensure real-time availability; definitions are given in Appendix~\ref{app:fundamental_features}. Each variant is trained at production settings (50 XGBoost seeds, depth 4, learning rate 0.05) and evaluated identically to XGB.

\input{tables/table_fundamentals_ablation}

Adding the ten fundamentals to the baseline reduces Sharpe from 1.11 to 0.89 (Table~\ref{tab:fund_ablation}). The new features absorb 44\% of total SHAP attention, redirecting it away from the momentum term structure (54\% to 27\%) and the regime signal (46\% to 29\%) without net improvement in portfolio performance. Beta falls from 0.45 to 0.07 and maximum drawdown tightens from $-$22.8\% to $-$18.3\%; the strategy's excess-return Newey-West $t$-statistic falls from 4.37\textsuperscript{***} to 3.56\textsuperscript{***}, still highly significant but materially weaker. The fund-augmented variant therefore behaves more like a market-neutral low-beta strategy than a high-alpha regime-conditioned one.

The fund-only variant (ten fundamentals, no momentum, no regime signal) identifies the source of the dilution directly. Its calm Sharpe of 0.86 is essentially equal to the baseline's 0.84, but its panic Sharpe of 0.38 is a fraction of the baseline's 1.53. Firm-level characteristics describe stable attributes (leverage, profitability, valuation) that do not flip direction when the market enters panic. Adding the regime signal to fundamentals (fund + $\pi$, panic Sharpe 0.72) roughly doubles the fund-only panic Sharpe but remains well short of the full model, because without the momentum term structure there is no directional relationship for the regime signal to reorganise. Removing $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ from the momentum + fundamentals variant drops Sharpe to 0.63 (panic 0.57), close to fund-only's 0.59. Across both ablations, removing either the momentum term structure or the regime signal causes the same collapse: fundamentals cannot substitute for the regime-momentum interaction, which is the load-bearing mechanism.

These ablations support the interpretation that $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ is a context variable for the momentum term structure specifically, rather than a general-purpose conditioning input. Characteristics that describe stable firm quality are useful in calm markets, where the fund-only calm Sharpe matches the baseline's. But those same characteristics carry no information about regime shifts, and adding them to the joint model dilutes the signal that does. A regime-conditional feature-set design, in which the model uses different inputs in calm and panic, is discussed as future work in Section~\ref{sec:future_work}.


\subsection{Cross-Sectional Selection Across Regimes}\label{sec:regime_results}

XGB selects different stocks in different regimes (Table~\ref{tab:regime_sharpe}): Sharpe 1.53 in panic against 0.84 in calm. What the model picks flips between regimes, from cross-sectional winners in calm to cross-sectional laggards in panic, with the regime signal acting as the gate. Where exposure-scaling methods reduce positions during stress to limit losses, XGB leans into panic by reconstructing the portfolio to exploit the reversal (Section~\ref{sec:stress_test}).

\input{tables/table_regime_sharpe}

XGB's predictions are decomposed using two complementary tools. SHAP values \citep{Lundberg2017} measure how much each input contributes to the model's prediction (definitions in Appendix~\ref{app:shap}). Cross-sectional z-scores measure where in the momentum distribution the model's selected stocks fall: for each stock in the long or short leg, the z-score at horizon $k$ is $(mom_k^s - \bar{mom}_k) / \sigma_k$, computed from the cross-sectional mean and standard deviation of all stocks that month. The z-score answers \emph{what} the model selects; SHAP answers \emph{what drives} the selection.

Aggregate feature importance is split almost evenly: $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ accounts for 46\% of total SHAP, the 12 momentum horizons together for 54\% (Table~\ref{tab:shap}). Between regimes, momentum's share rises from 50\% in calm to 58\% in panic while $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$'s share falls from 50\% to 42\%: the model leans on momentum more heavily during stress, but uses it in a fundamentally different way. Two decompositions follow: by momentum horizon at the regime aggregate (Section~\ref{sec:horizon_evidence}, immediately below), then by month-level selection mode (Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity_picks}).

\subsubsection{Per-horizon momentum SHAP}\label{sec:horizon_evidence}

The SHAP distribution by momentum horizon characterises which horizons drive the model's predictions. In the calm long leg, months 8--12 together account for $66.0\%$ of long-leg momentum SHAP, with months 11 and 12 the most influential individual horizons ($18.6\%$ and $16.8\%$). In the panic long leg, months 7--12 account for $67.2\%$, so roughly two-thirds of the prediction signal sits at the intermediate-to-long end in both regimes, mildly shifted toward the shorter end during panic. On the panic short side, month 1 SHAP rises to $10.6\%$ versus $5.3\%$ in calm: the model becomes more sensitive to recent returns when identifying stocks to short during stress.

Figure~\ref{fig:zscore_long_heatmap} shows that selection is not uniform across months. Patches of colour clearly recur, but with 167 rows there is no way to read off which patterns are stable selection modes and which are one-off months. The model is making different selections in different states of the world, and we want to identify those states.

We pick the heatmap apart with clustering. Treating each month as a single point given by its 12-horizon profile, K-means with $k=4$ groups months whose profiles look alike. The partition is highly stable: the same labels emerge across six independent random seeds for $163$ of $167$ months ($97.6\%$), with mean pairwise Adjusted Rand Index $0.96$. The four groups nest inside a coarser two-fold partition: clusters 1 and 2 are the 115 months in which the long leg sits above the cross-section average (``above-average baskets'', $\bar\pi = 0.28$); clusters 3 and 4 are the 52 months below (``below-average baskets'', $\bar\pi = 0.52$). The four cluster centroids are well-separated in 12-dimensional z-space: pairwise distances between them range from $1.37$ (clusters 1 and 2, both above-average) to $4.90$ (clusters 1 and 4, the trend-vs-deep-crisis extremes), against a typical within-cluster scatter of $0.6$ to $0.9$.

Each cluster paragraph follows the same skeleton. The opening paragraph names the regime context (current panic probability $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ and the prior 6- and 12-month panic frequencies) and connects it to the model's selection: the long-leg z-curve, which measures how far the picks sit from the cross-section average at each horizon, and raw pick momentum, the picks' absolute trailing returns. The middle paragraph fills in the broader market state: cross-section trailing returns at short, medium, and long horizons; cross-section dispersion (how spread out stocks are around the mean); cross-section skewness (whether returns are pulled by a few outliers); the strategy's own trailing 12-month Sharpe; and when the cluster occurs in the test sample. The closing paragraph reports the cluster's Sharpe and its role in the partition.

\paragraph{Cluster 1 (calm continuation): a quiet, broadly trending market.}
With current $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ at $0.21$ (the lowest of any cluster), the model has high confidence the regime is calm and commits to its standard winner-selection mode. The long-leg z-curve is humped: picks sit only mildly above average at short horizons ($z \approx +0.26$ at month 1) but are clearly differentiated at intermediate horizons (peak $+0.80$ at month 8). Because short-term moves are noisier, the model leans on the more stable 6 to 12 month signal, recovering \citet{NovyMarx2012}'s intermediate-horizon dominance (consistent with Section~\ref{sec:horizon_evidence}: months 8--12 carry $66\%$ of calm long-leg momentum SHAP). In these 53 months the average stock has gained at every horizon: $3\%$ over the prior 1 month, $13\%$ over 6 months, and $23\%$ over 12 months. Most common in 2013--2014, with stretches up to 21 consecutive months.

Cluster 1 earns a Sharpe of $0.92$, the lowest of the four. The Sharpe is moderate because the trend is mature: continuation still pays, but the strategy is riding an established rally rather than catching a turning point. Cluster 1 is a sanity check on the architecture: the model recovers classical momentum selection here, confirming the regime-conditioning architecture adds new behaviour during stress without overriding the unconditional pattern that already works in calm.

With current $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ in an ambiguous middle band ($0.34$, neither clearly calm nor clearly panic), the model cannot commit to either winner-selection or laggard-selection. The long-leg z-curve has the same humped shape as cluster 1 but at a much lower amplitude (z-mean $+0.19$ versus cluster 1's $+0.57$, peak $+0.32$ at month 8): the model picks up the same intermediate-horizon signal, but it is weaker here. Performance comes from occasional outlier capture rather than systematic selection.

The cross-section is mildly positive (average stock $+2\%$/$+8\%$/$+11\%$ over 1/6/12 months, roughly half the level of cluster 1) but has the highest skewness of any cluster ($7.2$/$7.6$/$8.1$): a small number of extreme winners pull the mean while the median stock barely moves. Cluster 2 earns a Sharpe of $0.93$, statistically indistinguishable from cluster 1, and exposes the limit of the regime-momentum mechanism: when the cross-section carries no coherent direction, the model cannot build a high-conviction long leg even with the regime signal available. The strategy treads water in these months and earns its return through occasional outliers rather than systematic selection.

\paragraph{Cluster 3 (post-panic recovery): a recovering market with high long-horizon dispersion.}
Moderate current $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ ($0.32$) combined with the highest 12-month prior frequency ($0.41$) signals to the model that a panic has just resolved and the cross-section is in the post-bear-rebound state, prompting it to invert the ranking and buy laggards. The long-leg z-curve sits below the cross-section average at every horizon (z-mean $-0.29$), with the deepest below-average at month 4 ($-0.36$) and month 12 ($-0.35$). Raw picks range from $-6\%$ to $+1\%$ while the broader cross-section is up double digits. The cluster has the highest intra-cluster basket turnover (Jaccard $0.68$), reflecting dynamic rotation as recovery candidates change.

These 31 months follow a panic-heavy year ($41\%$ of the prior 12 months were panic, the highest of any cluster) while the current probability of panic is only moderate ($\pi_t^{\text{filter}}=0.32$). The cross-section's term structure rises sharply: the average stock is up $+1\%$/$+11\%$/$+27\%$ over 1/6/12 months. Dispersion is the highest of any cluster at both medium and long horizons ($0.56$ and $0.85$), with stocks fanning out as some rebound and others lag. Cluster 3 is heavy in 2021 (9 of 31 months, the post-COVID rotation) and 2023. These are exactly the months in which \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} document momentum's worst crashes: post-bear-market rebounds where past losers (high-$\beta$ stocks) rally disproportionately and past winners (low-$\beta$ defensives) lag, so the cross-sectional ranking is about to invert. The dispersion shape echoes \citet{LeeSwaminathan2000}'s lifecycle reading of momentum signals evolving from continuation to reversal as information ages.

Cluster 3 earns a Sharpe of $1.21$ on these laggard picks. The novelty is twofold. First, the model exploits the D\&M crash mechanism as a feature rather than treating it as a risk to be scaled away. Where \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} and \citet{BarrosoSantaClara2015} reduce position size during crash-prone or high-volatility periods, XGB keeps full exposure and reverses the selection direction, earning that $1.21$ Sharpe in the same months in which traditional momentum loses money. Second, the cluster identifies a previously unexplored selection regime, the dispersed-recovery state, where the optimal long leg sits below the cross-section average at \emph{every} horizon, not only at recent ones. This is qualitatively distinct from the short-horizon reversal of \citet{Jegadeesh1990} (one-month) and \citet{Lehmann1990} (weekly).

\paragraph{Cluster 4 (deep crisis): an active panic with the whole market falling.}
The market has been falling for a sustained period: the probability of panic is at its highest ($\pi_t^{\text{filter}}=0.81$), $51\%$ of the prior 6 months were also panic (the highest of any cluster), and the cross-section is uniformly negative, with the average stock down $5\%$/$11\%$/$7\%$ over 1/6/12 months. With $\pi$ at this extreme, the model has unambiguous panic detection and commits fully to deep-crisis laggard-selection. In this falling market it picks the most beaten-down names: the long-leg z-curve sits uniformly between $-0.73$ and $-0.88$ across all 12 horizons (deepest around month 9), with raw pick losses spanning $-15\%$ at month 1 to $-48\%$ at month 12. The picks are roughly 0.7 to 0.9 standard deviations below the cross-section mean at every horizon. The bet is a rebound: stocks that have fallen the furthest in the panic are typically those with the highest market beta, and these high-$\beta$ losers rally disproportionately when the stress resolves. This is the same high-$\beta$-rebound mechanism that drives cluster 3, but in a different setting: cluster 3 picks lag-recovery names in a market that has already started rising, whereas cluster 4 picks deep-loser names while the panic is still active, betting on a future rebound rather than catching one already underway.

Critically, only $30\%$ of the prior 12 months were panic, in line with the test-period average: these are sharp, short panics rather than prolonged year-long deteriorations. Dispersion is the lowest of any cluster at both medium and long horizons ($0.35$ and $0.45$), with short-horizon skewness also the lowest ($3.67$): stocks are moving down together rather than fanning out. Eleven of 21 months are in 2022 (Fed-hiking cycle), with corroborating instances in April 2020 (COVID trough), October 2015 and January 2016 (China devaluation, oil collapse), and January 2023.

Cluster 4 earns the highest Sharpe of any cluster ($1.82$, $95\%$ block-bootstrap CI $[0.98, 2.68]$), with a mean monthly return of $+3.13\%$. The novelty is twofold. First, this is a term-structure-wide reversal portfolio with no direct analogue in the reversal literature: \citet{Jegadeesh1990} (one-month) and \citet{Lehmann1990} (weekly) document short-term reversal, \citet{Nagel2012} shows it spikes during crises as compensation for liquidity provision, and \citet{DeBondtThaler1985} document long-term contrarian reversal at 3--5 year horizons. Each targets a single horizon band; cluster 4's uniform loading at every horizon from 1 to 12 matches none. Second, where \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} document the beta-reversion mechanism as a \emph{risk} to be scaled away from, XGB exploits it as a strategy: the regime signal makes the contrarian bet loadable, and it pays off precisely because these panics are sharp and short rather than prolonged. That $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$'s SHAP share is \emph{lowest} here ($0.24$ combined) reflects the routing structure: once in the deep-crisis branch, momentum splits alone produce the loser portfolio. Cluster-level leg-beta and SHAP-share diagnostics for the full $K=4$ partition are in Appendix~\ref{app:cluster_cross_validation}.

\subsubsection{Discussion}\label{sec:novelty}

Prior work has established that momentum profits depend on market state \citep{Cooper2004}, that momentum crashes are tied to rebounds after stress \citep{DanielMoskowitz2016}, and that blending fast and slow momentum signals across market cycles can improve performance \citep{GHM2023}. Each cluster paragraph above places its specific finding within this prior literature; together, the four clusters establish three contributions that go beyond these results: (i) a term-structure-wide reversal portfolio in deep crisis with no direct analogue in the existing reversal literature, (ii) a regime signal whose marginal value scales with cross-sectional ambiguity rather than acting as a constant feature, and (iii) selection by momentum \emph{level} rather than momentum \emph{rank}, producing portfolios that no fixed-rank strategy could replicate. The model's regime-aware adaptation also concentrates on the reversal side: clusters 3 and 4 differ by $0.61$ in Sharpe, while clusters 1 and 2 are essentially indistinguishable ($0.01$). The strategy distinguishes kinds of reversal environments more sharply than kinds of trend-following environments.

\subsection{Risk Aversion and Stress Testing}\label{sec:risk_aversion}

\subsubsection{Risk-Aversion Control}

An investor may prefer lower portfolio risk at the cost of some expected return. We incorporate a risk-aversion parameter $\gamma$ into the strategy and trace the out-of-sample trade-off across $\gamma \in [0, 1]$.

The natural starting point is the mean-variance utility $r - \frac{\gamma}{2}\sigma^2$ \citep{Markowitz1952}, but it is the wrong lens here. Mechanically, applied as a scoring function the penalty is subtracted symmetrically from every stock's score, pushing the long book toward low-volatility names and the short book toward high-volatility names: a betting-against-beta portfolio whose realised volatility rises rather than falls as $\gamma$ grows (Appendix~\ref{app:alt_targets} confirms this across several variants). Economically, mean-variance penalises variance in \emph{absolute} terms, but real investors behave as if risk aversion is \emph{relative}, with a stable tolerance for proportional risk that does not change with wealth. Constant relative risk aversion captures this; mean-variance does not.

We therefore adopt a CRRA utility that penalises $\log\sigma$ instead of $\sigma^2$, and evaluate each stock's utility separately under a long direction and a short direction. The volatility penalty enters identically in both: a volatile stock is equally unattractive as a holding or as a short position, so the investor's aversion to risk does not flip sign with trading direction. Longs are the top decile of long-utility across the full cross-section; shorts are the top decile of short-utility. The full specification is given in Appendix~\ref{app:crra_construction}.

Figure~\ref{fig:risk_aversion} shows the resulting Pareto curve.\footnote{The $\gamma=0$ baseline reflects the CRRA dual-utility implementation rather than the headline XGB scoring; small numerical differences from Section~\ref{sec:performance_results} (volatility $19.50\%$, maximum drawdown $-22.8\%$) are within seed noise.} At $\gamma = 0.1$, Sharpe stays at 1.01 while maximum drawdown tightens from $-25\%$ to $-18\%$, a near-free reduction in tail risk. At $\gamma = 0.5$, annualised volatility halves from 19.7\% to 11.2\%, drawdown halves to $-12\%$, and Sharpe settles at 0.78. At $\gamma = 1.0$, volatility falls to 5.2\% (roughly a quarter of the market's) and Sharpe remains positive at 0.46. An investor can choose any point on this curve based on their volatility budget or drawdown tolerance. Beyond $\gamma \approx 1.5$, the risk penalty dominates the return signal and the long-short spread collapses into dollar-neutral noise. The framework does not blow up; it simply stops producing a directional bet.

\subsubsection{Stress Test: Prolonged Bear Market}\label{sec:stress_test}

The reversal mechanism documented in Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity_picks} (cluster 4) rests on a specific premise: that price declines during panic are temporary (driven by liquidity pressure) rather than permanent (driven by economic deterioration). The historical stress periods in the test sample support this: XGB earned $+29.9\%$ during the COVID crash (February--April 2020) and $+21.5\%$ during the 2022 bear market (January--October 2022). In both cases, the decline was temporary and reversed within months.

But the premise can fail. In a sustained economic deterioration (a depression or structural decline), beaten-down stocks fall because their businesses are failing, not because of temporary mispricing. We call this \emph{reversal failure}: the regime classification is correct, but the momentum structure within that regime has changed. To quantify this vulnerability, we simulate recessions of increasing duration, replacing consecutive months with XGB's average losing panic-month return ($-$3.44\% per month) at every possible insertion point.

\input{tables/table_stress_scenarios}

The strategy can absorb substantial downturns. A 12-month recession produces a worst-case drawdown of $-$49\%, after which calm-period continuation resumes. Only a depression-length downturn of 24 months produces a drawdown of $-$69\%. This mirrors the vulnerability identified by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} for traditional momentum, but on the opposite side: standard momentum crashes when winners stop winning; the regime-conditioned strategy would struggle when losers stop reverting.

\citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}'s own response to momentum's crash risk is exposure scaling. The two approaches use regime information differently: D\&M scale \emph{exposure} (how much to hold), XGB changes \emph{which stocks} are held. On the same data and transaction costs, XGB (Sharpe $1.11$, CAPM alpha $23.4\%$) outperforms D\&M managed momentum (Sharpe $0.49$, CAPM alpha $10.3\%$). A hybrid that adds D\&M-style scaling on top of XGB as a circuit breaker (Section~\ref{sec:future_work}) does not improve performance in the current test period because no prolonged downturn occurred, but would help most in cluster 4, where the long leg sits term-structure-wide negative and the reversal bet is most exposed to failure. Section~\ref{sec:historical_stress} provides direct historical evidence on how the strategy actually behaved during the dot-com bust and the GFC, complementing this synthetic stress test.

\subsubsection{Historical Stress: Out-of-Sample Evidence}\label{sec:historical_stress}

The simulation in Section~\ref{sec:stress_test} estimates worst-case behaviour under synthetic recessions. To complement it with direct historical evidence, the full pipeline (HMM + XGBoost) is re-estimated annually from 1995 onward, with each year's prediction made by a model trained only on data available through the prior year-end. This produces a 30-year out-of-sample monthly return series spanning 1995--2024 that includes the dot-com bust, the global financial crisis, and the 2020 COVID episode. Methodology details are in Appendix~\ref{app:expanding_window}; the rest of this subsection focuses on how the strategy actually behaved when stress arrived.

\paragraph{Worst observed stress: the dot-com bust}
The 2000--2002 dot-com bust produced the strategy's largest historical drawdown of $-64.8\%$ peak-to-trough. Restricted to the bear-market proper (March 2000--October 2002), the strategy lost $-43.8\%$ cumulative over 32 months (Sharpe $-0.39$). This is the historical realisation of the reversal-failure vulnerability described in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}: a prolonged sector-rotating bear in which beaten-down stocks did not promptly recover, so the panic-regime reversal selection bled. The strategy survived the episode and resumed positive returns thereafter (the 2003--2006 sub-sample produces Sharpe 0.70), but the realised drawdown is severe.

\paragraph{Stress at the targeted failure mode: the 2009 momentum crash}
The single most informative episode is March--December 2009, the period \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} document as a severe momentum crash. Under the same long-short construction used throughout this thesis (NYSE breakpoints, value-weighted, 10\,bps cost), unconditional 12-month momentum lost $-62.6\%$ cumulative during those ten months. Under expanding-window out-of-sample, the regime-conditional strategy gained $+20.5\%$ cumulative (Sharpe 0.73) during the same window. Across the full GFC episode (October 2007 -- June 2009) the strategy returned $+22.8\%$ cumulative (Sharpe 0.48). This is direct historical evidence that the regime mechanism survives the specific stress event that defeats unconditional momentum.

\paragraph{Synthetic and historical envelopes agree}
The synthetic stress test's worst-case bound ($-70\%$ drawdown at a 24-month synthetic recession; Section~\ref{sec:stress_test}) contains the realised dot-com drawdown of $-64.8\%$, so the simulation does not understate the historical risk.

\paragraph{Long-horizon survival}
Aggregating across all 30 years and all stress events, the strategy produces $+1{,}405\%$ cumulative return with a Sharpe of 0.50. Each historical drawdown is followed by recovery; no stress episode in the sample is unrecoverable. Sub-period samples are small enough that point estimates carry wide confidence intervals, so the qualitative pattern across periods is more informative than the levels.

\subsection{International Robustness}\label{sec:international}

To test whether the cross-sectional regime-momentum mechanism extends beyond US data, we replicate the full pipeline on UK and Japanese equities (Compustat Global, 1990--2025).\footnote{Each region's HMM is fit on locally-selected stress features (DD+VOL+REL\_N for both, with BANK\_REL replacing the US-specific Moody's BAA-AAA spread in the candidate pool); details in Appendix~\ref{app:intl_feature_selection}. The cross-sectional model is unchanged from the US specification, except that long and short legs are formed from unconditional decile breakpoints across all listed stocks rather than NYSE-equivalent breakpoints (no analogous tier exists for either market).}

Both regions support the mechanism. UK XGB attains a Sharpe of 0.84 over 2011--2025, well above unconditional 12-month momentum's 0.45 and the local market's 0.62. JP XGB attains 0.50, substantially above unconditional momentum's 0.07 but below the JP market's 0.86, consistent with the documented weakness of Japanese momentum \citep{Asness2013}. Maximum drawdown is reduced from $-$62.6\% (fixed momentum) to $-$21.4\% in UK, and from $-$66.3\% to $-$19.8\% in JP.

Taken together, the results show that momentum's cross-sectional predictability shifts between continuation and reversal across market regimes, and that exploiting this shift requires nonlinear conditioning on a regime signal. The signal's value lies not in predicting returns directly but in conditioning how the model jointly uses the full momentum term structure, a multi-dimensional interaction only tree-based models can learn.
