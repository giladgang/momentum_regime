\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

All strategies are evaluated out-of-sample on 2011--2025 (167 months), using the long-short construction described in Section~\ref{sec:stage2}. The central question is whether momentum's cross-sectional structure is regime-dependent, and whether exploiting this dependence requires nonlinear modelling.


\subsection{Portfolio Performance}\label{sec:performance_results}

\subsubsection{Strategy Comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:performance} reports the out-of-sample performance of all strategies.\footnote{All Sharpe ratios are computed using raw returns rather than excess returns above the risk-free rate. Since the same convention is applied to every strategy, relative comparisons are unaffected. The risk-free rate averaged roughly 2\% annualised over the 2011--2025 window, so absolute Sharpe ratios are overstated by approximately 0.05--0.10.}

\input{tables/table_performance}

Momentum's cross-sectional predictability is regime-dependent, and exploiting this dependence requires both a probabilistic regime signal and a nonlinear model. XGBoost combined with the HMM filtered probability (M2, mom+$\pi$) achieves an annualised Sharpe of 1.11 with a six-factor alpha of 24.7\% ($t = 4.78$; Appendix~\ref{app:factor_alphas}). Block bootstrap paired tests reject equality of Sharpe ratios between M2 and each benchmark in the table below at $p < 0.025$ (Appendix~\ref{app:bootstrap}). Every other specification fails to produce economically meaningful long-short performance, and the pattern of failures is informative.

Unconditional 12-month momentum achieves a Sharpe of 0.06 with a maximum drawdown of $-$72.3\%, exposing the crash vulnerability documented by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} and \citet{BarrosoSantaClara2015}. A deterministic regime switch (M0) improves this marginally to 0.11. The logistic regression (M1), given identical features to M2, collapses to $-$0.03. Hand-engineering the missing nonlinearity into M1 through $\pi^2$ and $\pi \times mom_k$ terms lifts it to 0.32, establishing that the regime-momentum interaction is real while also showing that pre-specified interactions recover less than a third of what XGBoost finds automatically. Adding seven fundamental characteristics to M2 reduces the Sharpe from 1.11 to 0.97: the new features absorb 38\% of SHAP importance and dilute the regime-momentum signal that drives performance.

\subsubsection{Regime Signal Ablation}

The regime signal is essential. Removing $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ and retraining on the 12 momentum features alone drops the Sharpe to 0.41 (Table~\ref{tab:regime_signal_ablation}). Replacing $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ with the four raw stress indicators (DD, DISP, REL\_N, CS) performs even worse (Sharpe 0.30): with four additional dimensions the model must reconstruct the regime structure at each split, while the HMM distils those indicators into a single dimension that a single tree split can condition on. The HMM's role is not to provide a convenient feature but to solve regime identification \emph{before} stock selection, freeing the cross-sectional model to use the regime signal rather than construct it. If the model cannot extract a meaningful regime signal even when given the four raw indicators directly, it certainly cannot do so from momentum features alone -- which are one further step removed from the underlying regime dynamics.

\subsection{The Regime-Momentum Interaction}\label{sec:regime_results}

M2 uses momentum differently across regimes (Table~\ref{tab:regime_sharpe}): Sharpe 1.56 in panic against 0.82 in calm. The regime signal adds the most value precisely when the model switches from continuation to reversal. Where exposure-scaling methods reduce positions during stress to limit losses, M2 leans into panic by reconstructing the portfolio to exploit the reversal (Section~\ref{sec:dm_comparison}).

\input{tables/table_regime_sharpe}

The regime signal dominates the tree ensemble. Of the 500 production trees, 379 (75.8\%) split on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ at least once, and a typical stock passes through such a split in 54\% of its tree paths. The regime-conditioned paths carry disproportionate weight in the model's output: 67\% of the panic long leg's total predicted return comes from trees that split on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, against only 20\% in calm.

M2's predictions are decomposed using two complementary tools. SHAP values \citep{Lundberg2017} measure how much each horizon contributes to the model's prediction (definitions in Appendix~\ref{app:shap}). Cross-sectional z-scores measure where in the momentum distribution the model's selected stocks fall: for each stock in the long or short leg, the z-score at horizon $k$ is $(mom_k^s - \bar{mom}_k) / \sigma_k$, computed from the cross-sectional mean and standard deviation of all stocks that month. Figure~\ref{fig:term_structure} reports these measures averaged across all selected stocks and all months in each regime.

Aggregate feature importance is split almost evenly: $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ accounts for 46\% of total SHAP, the 12 momentum horizons together for 54\% (Table~\ref{tab:shap}). Between regimes, momentum's share rises from 50\% in calm to 58\% in panic while $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$'s share falls from 50\% to 42\%. The model leans on momentum more heavily during stress, but uses it in a fundamentally different way.

\subsubsection{Calm: Momentum Continuation}\label{sec:calm}

\paragraph{Z-scores: characteristics of selected stocks}
The z-score curve for the calm long leg is humped: near zero at month~1, peaking at $+0.39$ at month~8, declining to $+0.17$ at month~12. The short leg mirrors this with negative z-scores, deepest at months 8--10 ($-0.27$ to $-0.30$). The long-short spread is 1.33\% per month. Notably, the peak z-score of $+0.39$ is well above average but far from the distribution's upper tail -- the model does not select the highest-momentum stocks (Section~\ref{sec:novelty} discusses this).

\paragraph{SHAP: where the model discriminates}
SHAP is concentrated at the long end: months 11 and 12 are the most influential individual horizons (18.5\% and 18.0\% of long-leg SHAP), with months 8--12 together accounting for 67\%. Months 1--7 each contribute between 3.7\% and 6.2\%. The model receives 12 undifferentiated momentum horizons and independently recovers the intermediate-to-long-horizon dominance documented by \citet{NovyMarx2012}. The z-score peak at month~8 and the SHAP peak at months 11--12 capture different things: z-scores show where the momentum signal is \emph{clearest} (the widest long-short separation), while SHAP shows where the signal is \emph{most influential} for the model's prediction.

\subsubsection{Panic: Reversal}\label{sec:panic}

\paragraph{Z-scores: characteristics of selected stocks}
The humped curve flattens in panic. The long leg's z-score drops to $-0.09$ to $-0.19$ across all horizons ($-19.5\%$ trailing 12-month momentum, $-5.2\%$ at one month). The uniform depression is consistent with non-discretionary selling pressure \citep{BrunnermeierPedersen2009, VayanosWoolley2013} that depresses returns at every lookback simultaneously, with constrained arbitrage capital \citep{ShleiferVishny1997} allowing the dislocation to persist.

The short leg is asymmetric at month~1: its z-score spikes to $+0.30$ against the long leg's $-0.19$. The model shorts stocks with recent short-term recoveries ($+3.7\%$ at one month) but negative longer-horizon momentum, consistent with unsustained stress rallies \citep{DeBondtThaler1985}. The long-short spread in panic is 3.09\% per month, more than double the calm-period spread.

\paragraph{Crash versus recovery within panic}
The z-score profiles reported above average across all 59 panic months. Decomposing panic by concurrent market direction reveals that this average masks two distinct selection patterns (Figure~\ref{fig:panic_subtypes}). In the 18 months where the market declined during panic (crash months), the long leg is below the short leg at every horizon (spread of $-0.15$ to $-0.41$), and the strategy loses money ($-0.49\%$ per month). In the 41 months where the market rose during panic (recovery months), the pattern changes: the long leg remains below the short leg at months 1--5, but flips above it at months 7--12 (spread of $+0.08$ to $+0.15$), and the strategy earns $+4.42\%$ per month (Sharpe 2.35).

The overall panic Sharpe of 1.56 is driven entirely by recovery months. The model applies the same learned function to both sub-types, but during recoveries some stocks have already begun to rebound at longer horizons, providing separation that the model exploits. During crashes, stocks continue to fall and the reversal bet fails. This provides direct evidence for the reversal failure vulnerability discussed in Section~\ref{sec:stress_test}.

\paragraph{SHAP: where the model discriminates}
The SHAP distribution shifts modestly toward shorter horizons compared to calm, but months 7--12 still dominate (accounting for roughly 63\% of the long leg's momentum SHAP). Month~1 SHAP rises notably on the short side (10.1\%, compared to 5.0\% in calm), indicating that the model's predictions become more sensitive to recent returns when identifying stocks to short during stress. This is consistent with \citet{Nagel2012}, who shows that returns to short-term reversal spike during crises when liquidity provision is most valuable.

\subsubsection{Discussion}\label{sec:novelty}

Prior work has established that momentum profits depend on market state \citep{Cooper2004}, that momentum crashes are tied to rebounds after stress \citep{DanielMoskowitz2016}, and that blending fast and slow momentum signals across market cycles can improve performance \citep{GHM2023}. The analysis above contributes three findings that go beyond these results.

\paragraph{The panic long leg is a term-structure-wide loser portfolio}
In panic, the model's long leg is below the cross-sectional average at \emph{every} horizon from 1 to 12 months. This flat, negative z-score profile is qualitatively different from short-term reversal \citep{Jegadeesh1990, Nagel2012}, which targets month~1 only, and from long-term contrarian strategies \citep{DeBondtThaler1985}, which operate at multi-year horizons. In calm, the model recovers the intermediate-horizon continuation shape of \citet{NovyMarx2012}. The contrast -- humped in calm, flat-negative in panic -- has not, to the best of our knowledge, been documented in this form.

\paragraph{The regime signal reorganises the full momentum term structure}
The regime does not flip one horizon from continuation to reversal or shift weight between a fast and a slow signal. It alters how the model uses the \emph{entire} 12-horizon term structure: buying from the upper tail at intermediate horizons in calm and from the lower tail at all horizons in panic. The SHAP distribution remains stable across regimes (Table~\ref{tab:zscore_shap_detail}): the model attends to the same horizons in both states. What changes is the direction of selection at those horizons.

\paragraph{Selection by momentum level, not momentum rank}
Traditional momentum strategies rank stocks by trailing return at a fixed lookback \citep{JegadeeshTitman1993}. Here, the portfolio ranks on \emph{predicted forward return} -- a learned function of all 12 horizons and the regime signal. The model learns which momentum \emph{level} is associated with future outperformance. In calm, this distinction is modest: the long leg's z-scores peak at $+0.39$, above average but far from the upper tail. In panic, it becomes decisive: the long leg selects stocks \emph{below} the cross-sectional average at every horizon, a selection no fixed-rank strategy would produce.

\paragraph{The role of nonlinearity}
That nonlinear models outperform linear ones in cross-sectional asset pricing is well established \citep{Gu2020}. What is specific here is \emph{where} the nonlinearity matters: in transforming a macrofinancial regime probability into a conditioning variable for multi-horizon momentum selection. Section~\ref{sec:nonlinearity} provides structural evidence that the nonlinearity is not generic feature interaction but specifically the ability of $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ to act as a context switch.

\subsection{Nonlinearity and Feature Interactions}\label{sec:nonlinearity}

\subsubsection{Tree Path Analysis}\label{sec:tree_paths}

The per-horizon decomposition in Section~\ref{sec:regime_results} treats each horizon independently, leaving open whether the model relies on specific \emph{interactions} between horizons. To test this, 5{,}000 sampled stocks from each regime$\times$leg group are traced through 50{,}000 individual trees, recording which momentum horizon groups each decision path splits on (Appendix~\ref{app:combo_analysis}). Horizons are grouped into four bands: short (S, months 1--3), medium (M, months 4--6), intermediate (I, months 7--9), and long (L, months 10--12). Table~\ref{tab:combo_freq} reports the frequency of each of the 15 possible combinations; each column sums to 100\%.

\input{tables/table_combo_freq}

The combination frequencies are stable across all four groups: I{+}L accounts for 13--15\% of paths, M{+}I{+}L for 10--11\%, and S{+}M for 7--8\%. No unexpected interaction emerges. The distribution matches what the per-horizon SHAP decomposition would predict: horizons with higher SHAP weight appear more frequently in tree paths and combine in proportion to their individual importance.

This locates the specific source of nonlinearity that makes XGBoost essential. The model's value does not come from complex momentum-momentum interactions that differ across regimes -- the horizon combinations are regime-invariant. It comes from conditioning the \emph{direction} of selection on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$: the same tree structure that buys intermediate-horizon winners in calm buys intermediate-horizon losers in panic, depending on which branch the regime signal routes each stock through. A linear model assigns a single fixed coefficient to each horizon; XGBoost effectively assigns coefficients whose sign flips with the regime. Table~\ref{tab:combo_long_cp} in Appendix~\ref{app:combo_analysis} quantifies this: the calm-minus-panic z-score difference is positive for all 39 entries in the long leg, with the largest shifts at the intermediate horizon ($+$0.36 to $+$0.55).

\subsubsection{Tree Depth and Feature Interactions}\label{sec:depth_analysis}

Tree depth provides direct evidence for why multi-dimensional conditioning is required (Figure~\ref{fig:depth_sharpe}). Annualised return rises monotonically from depth~1 (7.3\%, no feature interactions) through depth~4 (21.9\%), with the largest gains at depths~2 and~3 where the model first gains multi-way conditioning. Volatility is nearly constant across depths ($\approx$19.5\%): deeper trees improve signal quality rather than amplifying risk-taking. A depth-2 model, which conditions on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ and one momentum horizon per tree, achieves 15.0\% -- 31\% below the full model. Reading the term-structure shape requires comparing multiple horizons simultaneously, which only becomes possible at depth~$\geq 3$. Performance peaks at depth~4 and declines beyond depth~5.

\subsection{Risk Aversion and Stress Testing}\label{sec:risk_aversion}

\subsubsection{Risk-Aversion Control via CRRA Dual-Utility}

An investor may prefer lower portfolio risk at the cost of some expected return. Introducing a risk-aversion parameter $\gamma$ requires deciding \emph{where} in the pipeline it acts. The algorithmic choice matters: a textbook mean-variance training target $y_t^s = r_{t+1}^s - \frac{\gamma}{2}\sigma_s^2$ \citep{Markowitz1952} degrades out-of-sample performance for any $\gamma > 0$ in long-short construction. The penalty is subtracted from every stock's score, so the top decile (longs) is pushed toward low-$\sigma$ names and the bottom decile (shorts) toward high-$\sigma$ names; the long-short portfolio becomes a betting-against-beta trade in disguise, and realised portfolio volatility rises rather than falls as $\gamma$ grows. At larger $\gamma$, the model also learns $-\gamma\log\sigma$ from features rather than the return signal, dissolving the regime-conditional mechanism documented in Sections~\ref{sec:tree_paths}--\ref{sec:panic}. Appendix~\ref{app:alt_targets} confirms this degradation is not specific to the mean-variance functional form.

The specification used here separates the return signal from the risk penalty using a two-model architecture and a constant-relative-risk-aversion (CRRA) utility. An XGBoost ensemble predicts the expected forward return $\hat r_t^s$. A second XGBoost ensemble, trained on the realised forward volatility $\sigma^s_{[t+1,t+12]} = \mathrm{std}(r^s_{t+1}, \ldots, r^s_{t+12})$, predicts $\hat\sigma_t^s$. Each stock is then evaluated under both trading directions:
\[
\begin{aligned}
u_{\text{long}}^s  &= +\,\mathrm{sign}(\hat r^s)\,\log\!\big(1 + |\hat r^s|/\varepsilon\big) \;-\; \gamma\,\log(\hat\sigma^s),\\
u_{\text{short}}^s &= -\,\mathrm{sign}(\hat r^s)\,\log\!\big(1 + |\hat r^s|/\varepsilon\big) \;-\; \gamma\,\log(\hat\sigma^s),
\end{aligned}
\]
with $\varepsilon = 0.01$. The $-\gamma\log(\hat\sigma)$ term appears with the \emph{same sign} in both directions: a low-$\sigma$ stock is preferred whether considered for the long or the short book. Both legs therefore converge on low-volatility names as $\gamma$ grows, eliminating the betting-against-beta tilt. Longs are the top decile of $u_{\text{long}}$ across the full cross-section, and shorts are the top decile of $u_{\text{short}}$, both with NYSE breakpoints and value weighting.

Why CRRA rather than mean-variance? The log penalty is scale-invariant: a multiplicative shift in all stocks' volatilities leaves rankings unchanged, and $\gamma$ has the interpretable meaning of the \emph{relative} price of volatility. The $\sigma^2$ penalty grows quadratically in absolute vol and over-weights high-vol stocks arbitrarily as $\gamma$ rises. Combined with the dual-utility construction, the log form allows $\gamma$ to act as a smooth concentration dial rather than a signal-corrupting filter.

Figure~\ref{fig:risk_aversion} sweeps $\gamma \in [0, 2]$ with 50 XGBoost seeds per model. At $\gamma = 0.1$ the Sharpe ratio is 1.01, essentially unchanged from the $\gamma = 0$ baseline of 1.11, while maximum drawdown improves from $-25\%$ to $-18\%$: a near-free reduction in tail risk. At $\gamma = 0.5$, annualised volatility halves from 19.7\% to 11.2\%, Sharpe is 0.78, and maximum drawdown falls to $-12\%$. At $\gamma = 1.0$, volatility is 5.2\% (roughly one-quarter of the market), and Sharpe remains positive at 0.46. Beyond $\gamma \approx 1.5$ the $\sigma$ penalty dominates the return term: low-$\hat\sigma$ stocks occupy the top deciles of both utility rankings simultaneously, and the long-short spread collapses to dollar-neutral noise. The useful range is $\gamma \in [0, 1]$, within which $\gamma$ traces a smooth Pareto trade-off between Sharpe and drawdown.

\subsubsection{Stress Test: Prolonged Bear Market}\label{sec:stress_test}

The reversal mechanism documented in Section~\ref{sec:panic} rests on a specific premise: that price declines during panic are temporary (driven by liquidity pressure) rather than permanent (driven by fundamental deterioration). The historical stress periods in the test sample support this: M2 earned $+29.9\%$ during the COVID crash (February--April 2020) and $+21.5\%$ during the 2022 bear market (January--October 2022). In both cases, the decline was temporary and reversed within months. The panic sub-type decomposition in Section~\ref{sec:panic} corroborates this: the 18 panic months with negative concurrent market returns produced an average loss of $-0.49\%$ per month, while the 41 recovery months generated $+4.42\%$.

But the premise can fail. In a sustained economic deterioration -- a depression or structural decline -- beaten-down stocks fall because their businesses are failing, not because of temporary mispricing. We call this \emph{reversal failure}: the regime classification is correct, but the momentum structure within that regime has changed. To quantify this vulnerability, we simulate recessions of increasing duration, replacing consecutive months with M2's average losing panic-month return ($-$3.51\% per month) at every possible insertion point.

\input{tables/table_stress_scenarios}

The strategy can absorb substantial downturns. A 12-month recession produces a worst-case drawdown of $-$51\%, after which calm-period continuation resumes. Only a depression-length downturn of 24 months produces a drawdown of $-$70\%. This mirrors the vulnerability identified by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} for traditional momentum, but on the opposite side: standard momentum crashes when winners stop winning; the regime-conditioned strategy would struggle when losers stop reverting. The hybrid approach combining stock selection with exposure scaling (Section~\ref{sec:future_work}) is designed for this contingency.

\subsubsection{Comparison with \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}}\label{sec:dm_comparison}

On the same data and with the same transaction costs, M2 (Sharpe 1.11, CAPM alpha 23.8\%, $t = 4.08$) outperforms the D\&M managed momentum strategy (Sharpe 0.49, CAPM alpha 10.3\%, $t = 1.86$). The two approaches use regime information differently: D\&M scale portfolio \emph{exposure} based on predicted crash risk (how much to hold), while M2 changes \emph{which stocks} are held by selecting from the opposite tail of the cross-sectional distribution (what to hold).

A natural extension is to combine the two: use M2's stock selection for portfolio construction and D\&M-style exposure scaling as a circuit breaker. Adding D\&M-style scaling on top of M2 does not improve performance in the current test period, because the condition it guards against -- a sustained reversal failure where beaten-down stocks never recover -- did not occur. Although the test period contains panic streaks of up to 15 consecutive months, M2 never loses more than 3 consecutive panic months. The stress test above (Table~\ref{tab:stress_scenarios}) shows that only a depression-length downturn of 24 months or more would produce drawdowns severe enough to justify exposure scaling. The hybrid approach remains a valuable contingency for deployment in environments where such prolonged deterioration is possible (Section~\ref{sec:future_work}).

Taken together, the results show that momentum's cross-sectional predictability shifts between continuation and reversal across market regimes, and that exploiting this shift requires nonlinear conditioning on a regime signal. The signal's value lies not in predicting returns directly but in conditioning how the model jointly uses the full momentum term structure -- a multi-dimensional interaction only tree-based models can learn.
