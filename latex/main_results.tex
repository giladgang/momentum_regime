\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

The primary objective of this thesis is not to maximise out-of-sample returns but to understand how regime information interacts with cross-sectional momentum. Specifically, it asks whether regime information can be used for \emph{stock selection} (changing which stocks are held) rather than \emph{exposure scaling} (changing how much of a fixed portfolio to hold), and whether this alternative mechanism requires nonlinear modelling. Portfolio performance is the vehicle for this analysis, not an end in itself.

All strategies are evaluated out-of-sample on the test period (January 2011 to November 2025, 167 months). Each method produces a long-short portfolio: the top decile of stocks (the \emph{long side}) is bought, and the bottom decile (the \emph{short side}) is sold. Both sides are value-weighted using NYSE breakpoints, rebalanced monthly with one-way transaction costs of 10 basis points. Long-short construction is the standard in the momentum literature \citep{JegadeeshTitman1993, DanielMoskowitz2016, BarrosoSantaClara2015} because it isolates the momentum anomaly from market beta, providing a cleaner test of stock selection skill.

The analysis proceeds in seven stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Section~\ref{sec:performance_results} compares the overall performance of all strategies, establishing that only the nonlinear model (M2) survives long-short construction.
    \item Section~\ref{sec:regime_results} decomposes performance by market regime.
    \item Section~\ref{sec:ic_analysis} examines the information coefficient of each momentum horizon in calm versus panic, providing model-free evidence for the regime-dependent term structure of momentum.
    \item Section~\ref{sec:shap_results} analyses SHAP feature importance, including decompositions by regime and portfolio side.
    \item Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results} presents ablation studies and the comparison with \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}.
    \item Section~\ref{sec:risk_aversion} examines how risk aversion affects the regime signal's importance.
    \item Section~\ref{sec:economic_mechanism} analyses the economic mechanism behind the regime-dependent stock selection.
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{Portfolio Performance}\label{sec:performance_results}

Table~\ref{tab:performance} reports the out-of-sample performance of all strategies.

\input{tables/table_performance}

The central finding is stark: in long-short construction, only M2 (XGBoost) produces economically meaningful risk-adjusted returns. M2 achieves an annualised Sharpe ratio of 0.97 with returns of 16.9\%, volatility of 17.7\%, and a maximum drawdown of $-$19.6\%. All other strategies collapse. M1 (logistic regression) achieves a Sharpe of only 0.05 (returns $-$0.5\%), despite having the same input features as M2. Fixed 12-month momentum is effectively dead (Sharpe 0.06, maximum drawdown $-$72.3\%), confirming the momentum crash phenomenon documented by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}.

The collapse of M1 is the first piece of evidence for the thesis's central claim: the regime-momentum interaction is inherently nonlinear. A linear model assigns $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ a single coefficient that cannot capture the conditional logic required to adapt both the long and short sides across regimes. XGBoost's tree splits learn regime-conditional rules that are invisible to a linear specification (even with polynomial and interaction augmentations; see Appendix~\ref{app:m1_poly}).

Factor model regressions confirm that M2's returns are not explained by known risk factors. The CAPM alpha is 17.7\% annualised ($t = 3.33$, $p < 0.001$), and the alpha remains significant across all standard specifications: Fama--French three-factor (17.0\%, $t = 3.26$), Carhart four-factor (17.8\%, $t = 3.32$), Fama--French five-factor (16.4\%, $t = 3.26$), and the six-factor model including momentum (17.4\%, $t = 3.35$). All alphas are significant at the 1\% level. The strategy is approximately market-neutral (CAPM $\beta = -0.04$) and its returns are not repackaged exposure to value, size, profitability, investment, or momentum factors. The mom+$\pi$ variant (excluding fundamentals) produces even higher alphas (CAPM 23.8\%, $t = 4.08$; six-factor 24.7\%, $t = 4.78$), because it has higher returns at the cost of higher volatility.

Removing the regime signal drops M2's Sharpe from 0.97 to 0.48 (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results}), confirming that the HMM captures decision-relevant information not spanned by the momentum features themselves.

A spanning test formalises the comparison between M1 and M2. Regressing M2's monthly returns on M1's returns yields an alpha of 17.0\% ($t = 3.47$, $p < 0.001$), confirming that M2 captures information that M1 cannot. The reverse regression yields an insignificant alpha of $-$2.9\% ($t = -0.71$, $p = 0.48$), indicating that M1 adds nothing beyond what M2 already provides.

\subsection{Regime-Conditional Performance}\label{sec:regime_results}

Decomposing performance by market regime reveals why M2 succeeds where other strategies fail (Table~\ref{tab:regime_sharpe}).

\input{tables/table_regime_sharpe}

M2 achieves a Sharpe ratio of 1.50 during panic months compared to 0.61 during calm months. This panic-period dominance contrasts sharply with the long-only literature, where calm-period improvement typically drives overall performance. The difference arises because long-short construction exposes a dimension of skill that long-only construction masks: the ability to correctly position the short side.

\paragraph{Calm months: momentum continuation}
In calm markets, the standard momentum continuation effect documented by \citet{JegadeeshTitman1993} dominates: past winners continue to outperform past losers. The economic mechanism is well understood: firm-specific good news -- strong earnings, positive analyst revisions, improving fundamentals -- diffuses gradually through the market as investors update their beliefs with a delay \citep{Hong1999, Barberis1998}. A stock that has risen over the past 6--12 months is likely still in the process of being re-priced upward, and the reverse holds for declining stocks. M2 exploits this by buying stocks riding established upward trends and shorting stocks in sustained decline. The resulting long-short spread of 0.84\% per month is modest but consistent. Because the portfolio is approximately market-neutral ($\beta \approx 0$), this spread is not driven by broad market movements -- it reflects M2's ability to rank stocks correctly within each cross-section.

\paragraph{Panic months: short-term reversal}
In panic months, M2 achieves a Sharpe of 1.50 because it correctly adapts to a fundamentally different market environment. The IC analysis (Section~\ref{sec:ic_analysis}) confirms that momentum's predictive structure inverts during panic: short-horizon signals ($mom_1$ through $mom_3$) develop negative ICs, meaning that recent losers \emph{outperform} recent winners over the following month.

The economic intuition for this reversal rests on three reinforcing mechanisms. First, \emph{liquidity spirals} \citep{BrunnermeierPedersen2009}: during market stress, leveraged investors (hedge funds, proprietary desks) face margin calls and are forced to liquidate positions regardless of fundamental value. This forced selling concentrates in the most beaten-down names, pushing their prices temporarily below intrinsic value. When the selling pressure subsides, these stocks snap back. Second, \emph{institutional flow pressure} \citep{VayanosWoolley2013}: mutual funds experiencing redemptions during downturns sell their holdings, creating predictable downward price pressure that reverses once outflows stabilise. Third, \emph{investor overreaction} \citep{DeBondtThaler1985}: panic selling reflects fear-driven extrapolation of recent losses, causing prices to overshoot on the downside.

On the other side of the trade, stocks that held up during the decline -- typically large, low-beta, ``defensive'' names -- become temporarily overvalued as investors crowd into perceived safe havens. When the panic subsides and risk appetite returns, these defensive stocks underperform as capital rotates back into beaten-down, higher-beta names.

M2 learns this regime-specific pattern through its tree splits: it buys beaten-down stocks that are likely to snap back and shorts the defensive names that are likely to lag during recovery. The stocks M2 buys earn 4.07\% per month on average, while those it shorts earn only 1.27\%, producing a long-short spread of 2.80\%. Crucially, this spread does not come from market exposure (the portfolio has near-zero beta) but from M2 correctly distinguishing which stocks will recover and which will lag.

The holding period analysis (Section~\ref{sec:economic_mechanism}) confirms that this reversal is a short-lived phenomenon: profitable at the 1-month horizon but negative at 3 and 6 months. This time structure is consistent with the liquidity-driven explanation -- the price dislocations created by forced selling are temporary and reverse within weeks, not months. If the reversal reflected fundamental mispricing, it would persist over longer horizons.

\paragraph{Why other strategies fail in panic}
Other strategies cannot adapt to this regime shift. Fixed momentum holds past winners long and past losers short -- which is catastrophic when momentum reverses during market rebounds, precisely the ``momentum crash'' documented by \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}. When the market recovers from a panic, past losers (high-beta beaten-down stocks) rally sharply while past winners (defensive low-beta stocks) lag. A fixed momentum strategy is short the stocks that rally most and long the stocks that lag, producing severe losses. M1 (logistic regression) assigns fixed linear weights to each momentum horizon, so it cannot switch from continuation to reversal signals as the regime changes. This failure confirms that the regime-momentum interaction is inherently nonlinear: exploiting it requires a model that can learn conditional rules such as ``in panic, buy recent losers and short recent winners; in calm, do the opposite.''


\subsection{The Momentum-Regime Interaction}\label{sec:ic_analysis}

The preceding analysis shows that XGBoost exploits the regime signal to produce significant long-short returns. A natural concern is whether this reflects genuine economic structure or model-specific overfitting. This section provides \emph{model-free} evidence that the regime-momentum interaction exists in the raw data, independent of any machine learning model or hyperparameter choice.

The information coefficient (IC) measures the Spearman rank correlation between a momentum signal and subsequent stock returns, computed cross-sectionally each month. By examining the average IC of each of the 12 momentum horizons separately in calm and panic months, we can test directly whether momentum's predictive structure changes with the regime -- without involving XGBoost, logistic regression, or any other model.

Table~\ref{tab:ic_rotation} reports the average IC and its $t$-statistic for each of the 12 momentum lookback horizons, split by regime.

\input{tables/table_ic_rotation}

The results reveal a striking pattern. In calm months, all 12 horizons have positive, statistically significant ICs ($t > 2.5$), with the strongest predictive power at intermediate-to-long horizons ($mom_8$ through $mom_{10}$, IC $\approx 0.03$). This is the standard momentum continuation effect documented by \citet{JegadeeshTitman1993}: past winners continue to outperform.

In panic months, the pattern inverts. Short-horizon signals ($mom_1$ through $mom_3$) develop \emph{negative} ICs ($-$0.012 to $-$0.010), indicating that recent losers \emph{outperform} recent winners, consistent with the short-term reversal effect and the liquidity spiral mechanism of \citet{BrunnermeierPedersen2009}. Longer horizons ($mom_{10}$ through $mom_{12}$) retain weakly positive ICs (0.020 to 0.026), but none is individually statistically significant ($t < 1.6$). The lack of significance in the panic column reflects the limited sample (59 panic months); the economically relevant finding is not the significance of any single panic IC but the systematic \emph{contrast} between the two regimes: all 12 horizons are significant in calm, none in panic, and the short horizons flip sign.

This IC rotation is the model-free evidence for the thesis's central claim: momentum is not a single anomaly but a family of horizon-specific effects whose composition rotates with market conditions. In calm markets, intermediate-term continuation dominates. In panic, short-term reversal dominates. The regime signal $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ governs this rotation. A linear model cannot exploit this rotation because it assigns fixed coefficients to each horizon. XGBoost's tree splits condition the use of each horizon on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, effectively learning a different momentum strategy for each regime.

The partial dependence plot (Figure~\ref{fig:pdp_pi}) reveals the non-monotonic relationship between $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ and predicted returns. If $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ were a conventional return predictor, the plot would show a monotonic relationship. Instead, it displays a staircase pattern, with predicted returns rising in some ranges and falling in others. Each step corresponds to a tree-split threshold where XGBoost switches which momentum horizons it emphasises. This non-monotonic structure is precisely why the logistic regression assigns $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ a near-zero coefficient: a single linear slope through this staircase averages to approximately zero.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}\label{sec:ablation_results}

\paragraph{Regime signal alternatives}
Table~\ref{tab:regime_signal_ablation} compares XGBoost performance under three regime signal specifications: the baseline HMM $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, the GHM market-cycle variable \citep{GHM2023}, and no regime signal. The HMM signal produces the highest Sharpe ratio, substantially exceeding both the GHM cycle and the no-signal baseline. The GHM cycle, constructed from the same underlying market returns as the momentum features, provides no incremental information for a nonlinear model.

\paragraph{HMM feature ablation}
The include-one and leave-one-out experiments (Table~\ref{tab:hmm_feature_ablation}) isolate each HMM feature's contribution. Market drawdown (DD) alone achieves the highest standalone Sharpe, confirming its role as the dominant stress indicator. In the leave-one-out analysis, removing DD or REL\_N causes the largest performance drops, while DISP and CS each contribute marginally. Despite their small marginal contributions, DISP and CS improve the stability of the regime signal across different seed combinations.

\input{tables/table_hmm_feature_ablation}

\paragraph{Comparison with \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}}
The D\&M managed momentum strategy, which scales exposure to a fixed WML portfolio based on a binary bear indicator, achieves a Sharpe ratio of 0.49 with a CAPM alpha of 10.3\% ($t = 1.86$) on the same data and with the same transaction costs. M2's alpha of 23.8\% ($t = 4.08$) is both larger and more statistically significant. The two approaches exploit the same type of regime information through fundamentally different channels: D\&M adjust \emph{how much} of a fixed portfolio to hold (exposure scaling), while M2 adjusts \emph{which stocks} to hold (stock selection). Within the test period, adding D\&M-style exposure scaling on top of M2's stock selection does not improve performance, suggesting that M2 already internalises the regime information that D\&M uses for scaling. However, the two mechanisms address different vulnerabilities: M2's stock selection exploits short-lived reversal premia that dissipate beyond one month (Section~\ref{sec:economic_mechanism}), making it vulnerable to prolonged bear markets without recovery. D\&M's exposure scaling, by contrast, is specifically designed to reduce position size during sustained downturns. A hybrid approach that combines M2's stock selection with an exposure-scaling circuit breaker for prolonged stress is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:future_work}.



\subsection{Risk Aversion and the Value of the Regime Signal}\label{sec:risk_aversion}

The preceding analysis established that the regime signal is essential for long-short momentum. A natural question is whether this finding depends on the investor's risk preferences. XGBoost is retrained with a mean-variance utility target that penalises stock-level variance:
\begin{equation}
    y_t^s = r_{t+1}^s - \frac{\gamma}{2}\,\sigma_s^2,
\end{equation}
where $\gamma \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots, 10\}$ is the risk-aversion parameter. At $\gamma = 0$ this recovers the baseline model; as $\gamma$ increases, the model favours lower-volatility stocks.

The results parallel the long-only findings: the regime signal's relative SHAP importance declines with risk aversion. At $\gamma = 0$, $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ is among the most important features. At high $\gamma$, the model defaults to a momentum-dominated strategy that ignores the regime. The regime signal is most valuable when the investor is willing to bear risk, because it enables the aggressive, regime-conditional reweighting of momentum horizons that drives M2's long-short performance.


\subsection{Economic Mechanism}\label{sec:economic_mechanism}

The analyses above establish \emph{that} the regime signal improves long-short momentum through nonlinear stock selection. This section examines \emph{why}, by analysing the characteristics of the stocks selected in each regime.

\paragraph{Portfolio characteristics by regime}
In calm months, M2's long side holds stocks with positive trailing momentum (+31.3\% over 12 months), moderate size (median market cap \$291M), and relatively low book-to-market ratios (0.70). These are momentum continuation candidates riding established trends. The short side holds similar stocks with slightly higher momentum (+32.6\%), reflecting the compressed cross-sectional spread in calm markets.

In panic months, the profile inverts. The long side holds beaten-down stocks with negative 12-month momentum ($-$5.3\%) and negative 1-month momentum ($-$1.4\%), smaller size (median \$218M), and higher book-to-market (1.00). The short side holds larger stocks (median \$629M) that have held up during the decline, with positive 1-month momentum (+5.1\%). These are the ``defensive'' names that the strategy bets against during recoveries.

\paragraph{Forward return decomposition}
In calm months, the long-short spread earns 0.84\% per month, 100\% of which comes from stock selection (alpha over the market). In panic months, the spread is 2.80\% per month, also entirely from stock selection. The long side earns 2.37\% alpha over the market, while the short side earns $-$0.43\% alpha. The regime signal's value lies in identifying which beaten-down stocks will recover (long side) and which apparently defensive stocks are overpriced (short side).

\paragraph{Holding period analysis}
The reversal mechanism has a clear time structure. At the 1-month horizon, panic reversal bets are profitable: the long-short spread is +0.60\%. However, at 3 months the spread turns sharply negative ($-$4.85\%), and at 6 months it is $-$5.70\%. This indicates that the reversal effect is a short-lived phenomenon, likely driven by temporary liquidity dislocations rather than fundamental mispricing. Monthly rebalancing is essential: holding the panic portfolio for longer than one month would transform a profitable strategy into a losing one. This finding is consistent with the liquidity spiral mechanism of \citet{BrunnermeierPedersen2009}, in which forced selling creates temporary price dislocations that reverse within weeks.

\paragraph{Stress test}
A formal stress test reveals that M2's return distribution has positive skewness (+0.81), meaning the strategy is not crash-prone despite its use of reversal bets. The 95\% Value-at-Risk is $-$5.0\% per month, with a Conditional Value-at-Risk (expected shortfall) of $-$8.0\%. The longest losing streak is 6 months. A bootstrap simulation of prolonged bear markets (6 to 24 consecutive panic months) indicates potential cumulative losses, but the positive skewness and limited drawdown duration suggest that the strategy is more resilient than a static long-short momentum portfolio, which suffers from severe negative skewness \citep{DanielMoskowitz2016}.

\paragraph{Vulnerability}
The strategy's primary vulnerability is a prolonged fundamental deterioration where beaten-down stocks do not recover. The holding period analysis confirms this: reversal bets fail beyond one month. During extended bear markets, the strategy would need to rebalance monthly to capture the short-lived reversal premium, and prolonged periods where this premium is absent would result in sustained losses. This limitation, and a potential credit-spread-based circuit breaker to address it, is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:future_work}.
