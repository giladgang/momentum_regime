\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

Before presenting the results, it is worth reiterating the thesis's primary objective: the goal is not to construct a strategy that maximises out-of-sample returns, but to understand how the regime signal interacts with momentum and what this interaction reveals about the structure of the anomaly. Portfolio performance is reported as a vehicle for this analysis, not as an end in itself.

All strategies are evaluated out-of-sample on the test period (January 2011 to November 2025, 167 months). Each method produces a long-only, value-weighted, top-decile portfolio rebalanced monthly with one-way transaction costs of 10 basis points (i.e., 10 basis points charged on each buy or sell trade). Portfolio construction details and performance metric definitions are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:portfolio}. Feature importance is assessed via SHAP values \citep{Lundberg2017} for XGBoost and coefficient analysis for logistic regression; the methodological details are in Appendix~\ref{app:feature_importance}.

The analysis proceeds in five stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Section~\ref{sec:performance_results} compares the overall performance of all strategies.
    \item Section~\ref{sec:regime_results} decomposes performance by market regime, revealing that the main benefit of the regime-conditioned models lies in calm-period stock selection rather than crash avoidance.
    \item Section~\ref{sec:decomposition} isolates the contributions of lookback flexibility, fundamental characteristics, and the regime signal $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ to the observed Sharpe ratio improvement.
    \item Section~\ref{sec:feature_results} examines which features drive the predictions through coefficient analysis, SHAP values, and ablation studies that isolate the sources of the regime signal's value.
    \item Section~\ref{sec:context_results} synthesises these findings into the central insight of the thesis: that $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ functions as a context variable that reveals the regime-dependent structure of cross-sectional momentum.
    \item Section~\ref{sec:risk_aversion} examines how varying the investor's risk aversion in the model's training objective affects portfolio outcomes and feature importance.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Portfolio Performance}\label{sec:performance_results}

Table~\ref{tab:performance} reports out-of-sample performance for all strategies. The regime-conditioned methods (M1 and M2) substantially outperform both the market benchmark and the unconditional momentum baselines.

\input{tables/table_performance}

The logistic regression (M1) achieves a Sharpe ratio of 1.03 with the lowest annualised volatility among all active strategies (14.5\%), matching the market's volatility while delivering 14.8\% annualised returns versus 11.9\% for the market. Its maximum drawdown of $-$25.2\% is comparable to the market's $-$24.7\%, indicating effective downside protection. Importantly, M1's Sharpe ratio is statistically significant: the Newey--West $t$-statistic for its excess return over the market is 2.92 ($p < 0.01$), and the block bootstrap 95\% confidence interval on its Sharpe ratio ($[0.55, 1.61]$) excludes zero.

The XGBoost regressor (M2) generates the highest raw returns at 21.6\% annualised, with a Sharpe ratio of 1.01. Relative to M1, it trades higher returns for higher volatility (21.8\%), but its maximum drawdown of $-$26.1\% remains well below the fixed momentum and M0 strategies (which suffer drawdowns of $-$29.7\% to $-$36.5\%). M2's Newey--West $t$-statistic is 2.78 ($p < 0.01$), confirming that its outperformance is not a statistical artefact.

The deterministic formula (M0), which uses $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ only to select a momentum lookback horizon, achieves a Sharpe of 0.73. While this modestly exceeds fixed 12-month momentum (0.70), it falls well short of the machine learning methods. The formula cannot capture the nonlinear interactions between the regime signal and the full feature set that M1 and M2 exploit.

The GHM benchmark strategies, which use backward-looking market return momentum to classify regimes, achieve Sharpe ratios between 0.70 and 0.80. \subsection{Regime-Conditional Performance}\label{sec:regime_results}

Table~\ref{tab:regime_sharpe} decomposes performance by market regime. A striking pattern emerges: all strategies perform substantially better during panic months than during calm months. The market itself achieves a Sharpe ratio of 1.15 in panic versus 0.67 in calm, and even unconditional 12-month momentum achieves a Sharpe of 1.25 during panic compared to only 0.38 during calm.

\input{tables/table_regime_sharpe}

This pattern arises because the HMM classifies months based on the \emph{level} of stress indicators, not on the sign of contemporaneous returns.\footnote{For example, in March 2009 the S\&P 500 returned +8.8\%, yet the HMM classified this month as panic because drawdown, volatility, and return dispersion remained deeply elevated. Such recovery months contribute strongly positive returns to the panic-period Sharpe ratio.} Market drawdown (DD), the dominant feature, measures how far the market stands below its 12-month peak. After a sharp decline, the market may rally strongly, yet DD remains deeply negative until a new peak is reached, which can take many months of positive returns. Similarly, volatility and return dispersion remain elevated during early recoveries. As a result, many months classified as ``panic'' are actually recovery months with strong positive returns, occurring while the stress indicators have not yet normalised. All long-only strategies benefit from these rebound months, explaining why panic-period Sharpe ratios are uniformly high.

The regime-conditioned methods (M1 and M2) do achieve modestly higher panic-period Sharpes (1.43--1.45) than unconditional momentum (1.24--1.25), reflecting better stock selection during recoveries. However, the economically meaningful gap lies in calm periods: unconditional momentum strategies achieve Sharpe ratios of only 0.38--0.42 during the 112 calm months that constitute two-thirds of the test period, while M1 and M2 achieve 0.77--0.80. Since the portfolio spends most of its life in calm markets, this improvement drives the bulk of the overall Sharpe gain.

This finding contrasts sharply with the momentum crash literature, which frames regime detection as a tool for \emph{reducing} investment during downturns \citep{DanielMoskowitz2016, BarrosoSantaClara2015}. In their framework, the optimal response to elevated stress is to scale down momentum exposure, thereby limiting losses when past winners reverse. This thesis proposes a fundamentally different use of the regime signal: rather than investing \emph{less} during stress, the regime-conditioned models invest \emph{differently}, rotating from intermediate-horizon momentum toward short-term reversal stocks that benefit from the subsequent recovery. Panic periods are not a risk to hedge but an opportunity to capture, provided the portfolio is positioned for the rebound rather than for continued momentum. The data support this view: reducing exposure during panic months, when all strategies achieve their highest Sharpe ratios (1.24--1.45), would have been counterproductive. The regime signal's primary contribution is therefore twofold: enabling opportunistic stock selection during stress, and improving quality screening during the calm periods that constitute the majority of the sample.

\subsection{Decomposing the Sources of Improvement}\label{sec:decomposition}

The ten strategies evaluated in this thesis differ along three dimensions: the momentum lookback structure (single horizon versus multiple horizons), the inclusion of fundamental characteristics, and the use of the regime signal $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$. By comparing strategies that vary along one dimension while holding the others fixed, it is possible to isolate the contribution of each component.

\paragraph{The role of momentum horizon flexibility.}
Comparing fixed 12-month momentum (calm Sharpe 0.38) with M0 (calm Sharpe 0.41) isolates the effect of using $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ to select a single lookback horizon, without fundamentals. The improvement is negligible. The GHM DYN strategy, which blends two momentum horizons based on backward-looking market returns, achieves a calm Sharpe of 0.50, a modest improvement but far short of M1 and M2. Lookback flexibility alone, without fundamental characteristics, does not substantially improve calm-period stock selection.

\paragraph{The role of fundamental characteristics.}
The large jump occurs from GHM DYN (calm Sharpe 0.50) to M1 (calm Sharpe 0.80). M1 uses the same regime signal as M2 but gives $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ a coefficient of $-$0.005 (rank 17th out of 20 features), effectively ignoring it. Its top features by coefficient magnitude are log market equity, 11-month momentum, book-to-market, gross profitability, and asset growth (Table~\ref{tab:lr_coef}). The improvement therefore comes almost entirely from fundamental characteristics acting as a quality screen: among stocks with strong trailing momentum, M1 selects those that are also profitable, reasonably valued, and not overleveraged. In calm markets, where the raw momentum signal is noisy and the spread between winners and losers is compressed, this quality filter is especially valuable.

\paragraph{The role of $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$.}
Comparing M1 (calm Sharpe 0.80) with M2 (calm Sharpe 0.77) isolates the marginal contribution of using $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ as a context variable within a nonlinear model. The calm-period Sharpe ratio does not improve; M2 is, if anything, marginally lower than M1 in calm periods. The same pattern holds in panic periods: M1 achieves 1.45 versus M2's 1.43. The regime signal does not improve risk-adjusted returns in either regime relative to a well-specified linear model with fundamentals.

What $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ does change is the \emph{return profile}. M2 generates substantially higher annualised returns than M1 (21.6\% versus 14.8\%), offset by proportionally higher volatility (21.8\% versus 14.5\%). Over the 14-year test period, \$1 invested in M2 grows to \$14.70, more than double M1's terminal wealth of \$6.80. The regime signal enables XGBoost to take more concentrated, regime-dependent bets, shifting aggressively toward short-term reversal stocks during panic and toward intermediate-horizon momentum winners during calm, that generate higher absolute returns at the cost of higher risk.

\paragraph{Summary.}
The Sharpe ratio improvement from unconditional momentum (0.70) to the best strategy (M1, 1.03) is driven primarily by fundamental characteristics, not by the regime signal. Quality screening, combining momentum with profitability, valuation, and size, is the single most important source of risk-adjusted performance improvement in this framework. The regime signal $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ contributes differently: it does not improve Sharpe ratios beyond what a linear model with fundamentals achieves, but it enables a higher-return, higher-volatility portfolio that may be attractive to investors with greater risk tolerance.

\subsection{Feature Importance and Ablation}\label{sec:feature_results}\label{sec:ablation}

To understand \emph{why} the regime-conditioned models outperform, this section first examines which features drive the predictions through coefficient analysis and SHAP values, then uses ablation studies to isolate the sources of the regime signal's value.

\subsubsection{Logistic Regression Coefficients}

Table~\ref{tab:lr_coef} reports the standardised coefficient estimates for the logistic regression (Method~1). Since all features are standardised prior to fitting, the absolute coefficient magnitudes are directly comparable.

\input{tables/table_lr_coef}

The dominant features are log market equity ($\hat{\beta} = 0.072$), 11-month momentum ($0.053$), and book-to-market ($0.046$). These reflect the model's primary strategy: select large, high-momentum stocks that are also attractively valued. Among the momentum horizons, the model loads most heavily on intermediate lookbacks (mom$_{8}$ and mom$_{11}$) while assigning a negative coefficient to 12-month momentum ($-0.041$) and 1-month momentum ($-0.016$). This pattern reflects the well-documented short-term reversal and long-horizon mean-reversion effects that bookend the intermediate-term continuation premium. In aggregate, the sum of absolute momentum coefficients ($\sum_{k=1}^{12}|\hat{\beta}_{mom_k}| = 0.22$) exceeds the sum of absolute fundamental coefficients ($0.16$), confirming that momentum remains the primary signal even in the presence of quality screens.

The fundamental characteristics (gross profitability, asset growth, return on equity, earnings growth, leverage) all receive statistically significant coefficients, confirming their role as quality screens. The regime signal $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ ranks 17th out of 20 features by absolute magnitude, with a coefficient of $-0.005$. While statistically significant ($z = -2.60$), its economic contribution to the linear prediction is negligible.

\subsubsection{SHAP Feature Importance for XGBoost}

Table~\ref{tab:shap} reports the mean absolute SHAP values for the XGBoost model (Method~2), computed on the test set. The 12 individual momentum lookbacks are aggregated into a single momentum entry.

\input{tables/table_shap}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{cs_feature_importance.png}
    \caption{Mean absolute SHAP values for the XGBoost model (M2), decomposed by calm and panic months. Momentum features (aggregated) dominate overall, but $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ is the second most important individual feature. Its importance increases from 0.015 in calm to 0.020 in panic, indicating that the model relies more heavily on the regime signal during stress.}
    \label{fig:shap_importance}

The most striking finding is the contrast with the logistic regression: while $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ is essentially irrelevant in the linear model, it is the \emph{second most important feature} in XGBoost, trailing only aggregate momentum. Its SHAP importance increases from 0.015 during calm months to 0.020 during panic months, indicating that the model relies more heavily on the regime signal precisely when markets are stressed. This pattern, combined with the negligible logistic regression coefficient, provides direct evidence that $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ contributes through nonlinear interactions rather than as a linear predictor. The mechanism is that XGBoost uses $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ as a tree-split variable to condition its reliance on different momentum horizons: when $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ is high, the model shifts weight from intermediate-horizon momentum toward short-term reversal, a regime-dependent reweighting that a linear model with fixed coefficients cannot represent.

The fundamental characteristics (book-to-market, log market equity, return on equity) also appear in the top features, consistent with the quality-screening role identified in the decomposition analysis. Their SHAP values are smaller than the regime signal's, suggesting that in the nonlinear model, the regime-momentum interaction dominates the quality screen in explanatory power.

\subsubsection{Regime Signal Comparison: HMM vs GHM vs None}

Given that XGBoost relies heavily on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, a natural question is whether the HMM-based signal is superior to simpler alternatives. Table~\ref{tab:regime_signal_ablation} replaces $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ with the GHM market-cycle variable (a numeric encoding of the Bull/Correction/Bear/Rebound classification from trailing market returns) or removes the regime signal entirely, holding all other features and XGBoost hyperparameters fixed.

\input{tables/table_regime_signal_ablation}

The HMM signal improves the Sharpe ratio by +0.125 over the GHM cycle (1.007 vs 0.882). The GHM cycle, by contrast, adds nothing over no regime signal at all (Sharpe 0.882 vs 0.888), confirming that the backward-looking market return classification used in \citet{GHM2023} does not provide useful conditioning information for a nonlinear model that already observes 12 momentum lookback horizons. The GHM cycle is essentially redundant with the momentum features themselves, since it is constructed from the same underlying market returns. The HMM signal, built from stress indicators (drawdown, volatility, dispersion, market participation) that are not spanned by cross-sectional momentum, provides genuinely complementary information.

\subsubsection{HMM Input Feature Ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:hmm_feature_ablation} tests how much each of the four HMM input features contributes to the regime signal's downstream value. For each variant, a single-feature HMM is estimated via Gibbs sampling (3 seeds averaged), and the resulting $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ is fed into both M1 and M2.

\input{tables/table_hmm_feature_ablation}

The results reveal two important patterns. First, the logistic regression (M1) is completely insensitive to the quality of the regime signal: its Sharpe ratio varies by only 0.006 across all variants (1.032 to 1.038), confirming once more that M1 effectively ignores $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$.

Second, XGBoost (M2) shows clear differentiation. Market drawdown (DD) alone produces a Sharpe of 0.997, nearly matching the full four-feature baseline (1.007). This is intuitive: DD measures how far the market stands below its trailing peak, providing a clean, monotonic signal of market stress that directly captures the regime shifts that alter momentum's term structure. Return dispersion (DISP) is the second most informative single feature (Sharpe 0.955), consistent with the finding that cross-sectional return dispersion rises during stress and signals changes in the correlation structure that affect momentum strategies. Realised volatility (VOL, Sharpe 0.855) and relative market participation (REL\_N, Sharpe 0.810) are weaker in isolation, though their inclusion in the full model contributes a modest incremental improvement.

The dominance of DD is consistent with the economic mechanism proposed in Section~\ref{sec:context_results}: the regime signal's value lies in identifying when long-horizon momentum breaks down and short-term reversal becomes informative. Market drawdown is the most direct measure of this transition, as it captures the sustained price declines that trigger momentum crashes and the subsequent recoveries where reversal strategies thrive.

\paragraph{Relation to \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016}}
The ablation results clarify how this framework relates to the momentum crash literature. \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} show that the conditional volatility and mean of momentum returns are strongly related to past market declines, and propose scaling momentum exposure based on backward-looking volatility. The finding that DD alone nearly matches the full four-feature HMM (Sharpe 0.997 vs 1.007) is consistent with their insight: drawdown captures the same market stress that drives momentum crashes. However, this thesis uses DD differently. Rather than scaling overall momentum exposure, the HMM translates DD (along with VOL, DISP, and REL\_N) into a continuous regime probability that conditions \emph{which} momentum horizons XGBoost relies on. The result is not crash avoidance but regime-adaptive stock selection, which improves performance primarily during calm periods (Section~\ref{sec:regime_results}) rather than during the crashes that \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} focus on. The additional features (DISP, VOL, REL\_N) contribute modest incremental value beyond DD, but their primary role is to smooth the regime signal and reduce false positives, producing a more stable $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ that the downstream models can condition on reliably.

\subsection{The Regime Signal as a Context Variable}\label{sec:context_results}

The regime-conditional performance analysis (Section~\ref{sec:regime_results}) showed \emph{where} the regime signal adds value; the SHAP and coefficient analysis above showed \emph{which} features matter. This section addresses the deeper question: \emph{why} does $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ matter for XGBoost but not for logistic regression?

The answer is that $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ is not a direct return predictor. It does not forecast whether stocks will go up or down. Rather, it is a \emph{context variable} that modulates which other features are informative. A linear model assigns $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ a single coefficient that must hold across all market conditions, which is near zero precisely because the signal has no unconditional predictive power. XGBoost, through its tree-based splits, conditions on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ and then branches on different momentum horizons within each regime, without requiring explicit interaction terms.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{cs_pdp_pi_filter.png}
    \caption{Partial dependence plot of the XGBoost predicted return on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$. The nonlinear, non-monotonic shape confirms that $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ functions as a context variable rather than a linear predictor: its effect on predicted returns depends on the values of other features (particularly momentum horizons), which is why the logistic regression coefficient is near zero while XGBoost treats it as a top feature.}
    \label{fig:pdp_pi}
\end{figure}

This distinction, between a feature that predicts returns directly and one that governs the \emph{relevance} of other features, is central to understanding why the regime signal contributes to XGBoost but not to logistic regression. The SHAP decomposition confirms this mechanism: the momentum term structure is not stable, and $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ is the variable that governs which lookback horizons are informative in each market state.

\subsection{Risk Aversion and the Value of the Regime Signal}\label{sec:risk_aversion}

The preceding analysis establishes that the regime signal enables XGBoost to generate higher absolute returns through regime-adaptive stock selection. A natural follow-up question is whether this finding depends on the investor's risk preferences. To investigate, XGBoost is retrained with a mean-variance utility target that penalises stock-level variance:
\begin{equation}
    y_t^s = r_{t+1}^s - \frac{\gamma}{2}\,\sigma_s^2,
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_s^2$ is the trailing 12-month realised variance of stock $s$'s monthly returns and $\gamma \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots, 10\}$ is the risk-aversion parameter. At $\gamma = 0$ this recovers the baseline M2 model (pure return maximisation); as $\gamma$ increases, the model learns to favour lower-volatility stocks. All other features, hyperparameters, and portfolio construction rules are held fixed.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{risk_aversion_analysis.png}
    \caption{Effect of risk aversion on XGBoost portfolio performance and feature importance. \textbf{Top row}: out-of-sample Sharpe ratio, annualised return, and annualised volatility as a function of $\gamma$. \textbf{Bottom row}: relative SHAP feature importance (normalised to 100\%) across $\gamma$ values. The regime signal $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ accounts for nearly 30\% of total importance at $\gamma = 0$ but falls to approximately 6\% at $\gamma = 10$, while momentum's share rises correspondingly.}
    \label{fig:risk_aversion}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:risk_aversion} reveals three findings. First, the risk-neutral model ($\gamma = 0$) achieves the highest Sharpe ratio (1.01). Introducing any variance penalty reduces risk-adjusted performance: the Sharpe ratio drops to 0.91 at $\gamma = 1$ and stabilises around 0.70--0.77 for $\gamma \geq 2$. Portfolio volatility falls sharply from 21.8\% to approximately 16\% by $\gamma = 1$ and remains flat thereafter, but returns fall faster, from 21.6\% to 10--12\%. The model quickly learns to avoid the most volatile stocks, but this eliminates precisely the high-conviction, regime-adaptive bets that drive M2's outperformance.

Second, and most importantly, the relative importance of the regime signal declines substantially with risk aversion. At $\gamma = 0$, $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ accounts for nearly 30\% of total SHAP importance, making it nearly as important as all momentum features combined. By $\gamma = 10$, its share falls to approximately 6\%. This pattern has a clear economic interpretation: the regime signal's value lies in enabling \emph{aggressive}, regime-conditional reweighting of momentum horizons. A risk-neutral model exploits this fully, shifting between intermediate-horizon continuation and short-term reversal depending on the market state. A risk-averse model, penalised for selecting volatile stocks, cannot act on this information as aggressively, and defaults to a safer, momentum-dominated strategy that looks similar regardless of the regime.

Third, the stability of momentum's dominance across all $\gamma$ values (75--80\% of relative importance for $\gamma \geq 2$) confirms that the momentum anomaly itself is robust to risk preferences. What changes is the model's ability to \emph{condition} its use of momentum on the regime. The regime signal is most valuable precisely when the investor is willing to bear risk -- it is a tool for aggressive stock selection, not for risk reduction.
