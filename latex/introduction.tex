\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Cross-sectional momentum, the tendency for recent stock market winners to continue outperforming and losers to continue underperforming, is one of the most robust and widely documented anomalies in empirical finance. Since the seminal work of \citet{JegadeeshTitman1993}, momentum strategies have been shown to generate significant risk-adjusted returns across markets, asset classes, and time periods.

Yet momentum is also one of the most fragile anomalies: it is subject to severe and sudden crashes, most notably during market stress episodes such as the 2009 rebound from the global financial crisis, when momentum portfolios suffered losses exceeding 50\% in a matter of months \citep{DanielMoskowitz2016}. This tension between the strategy's long-run profitability and its vulnerability to regime shifts motivates the central question of this thesis: how does a real-time regime signal interact with the cross-sectional momentum anomaly, and what does this interaction reveal about momentum's underlying structure?

Two prominent responses in the recent literature address momentum's fragility. The first, exemplified by \citet{BarrosoSantaClara2015}, scales momentum exposure inversely with recent volatility, treating risk management as a post-construction overlay. The second, pursued by \citet{GHM2023}, conditions the momentum signal itself on the market state, blending short-term and long-term lookback horizons according to a classification of market cycles derived from trailing market returns. Both approaches improve upon unconditional momentum, but both rely on backward-looking indicators (recent portfolio volatility or past market returns) to infer the current market regime.

This thesis proposes an alternative: using a Bayesian Hidden Markov Model (HMM) estimated on real-time equity market stress indicators to produce a real-time regime signal, and then feeding this signal into machine learning models that learn how to adapt cross-sectional stock selection to the current regime. The specific combination -- a Bayesian HMM on real-time equity stress indicators, fed into cross-sectional ML models alongside 12 momentum horizons, and interpreted via SHAP as a context variable conditioning momentum's term structure -- does not appear in the existing literature. Each component is well established; the contribution lies in their integration and in the economic interpretation that emerges from comparing linear and nonlinear models' use of the regime signal.

The approach differs from prior work in three respects. First, the regime classification is based on real-time stress indicators (market drawdown, cross-sectional return dispersion, relative market participation, and credit spread) rather than trailing returns, providing earlier detection of regime shifts. Second, the regime signal enters the stock selection models as one feature among many, allowing the models to learn its optimal use rather than imposing a fixed rule for how regime information should affect portfolio construction. Third, by comparing a linear model (logistic regression) with a nonlinear model (XGBoost), the framework provides evidence for the thesis's central claim: the regime signal is not a direct return predictor; it is a \emph{context variable} -- a feature that does not predict returns directly but governs which momentum horizons are informative in a given market state.

\subsection{Preview of Main Findings}\label{sec:preview}

Out-of-sample evaluation on 167 months of U.S. equity data (2011--2025) yields three main findings. First, only a nonlinear model (XGBoost) produces meaningful long-short returns (Sharpe 1.08, six-factor alpha 24.7\%, $t = 4.78$); a linear model with identical features collapses (Sharpe $-$0.03), establishing that the regime-momentum interaction is inherently nonlinear. Second, the regime signal is essential -- removing it halves performance -- and functions as a context variable: in calm markets the model exploits intermediate-horizon continuation, while in panic it switches to short-horizon reversal, with the long-short spread widening from 1.3\% to 3.1\% per month. Third, model-free information coefficient analysis confirms this rotation in the raw data, independent of any model: all 12 momentum horizons are significant in calm, none in panic, and the short horizons flip sign.

\subsection{Outline}\label{sec:outline}

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:literature} reviews the relevant literature on the momentum anomaly, regime-switching models in finance, the market-state momentum approach of \citet{GHM2023} (which conditions momentum on trailing market returns), and machine learning approaches to cross-sectional asset pricing. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} develops the two-stage empirical framework: the Bayesian HMM for regime classification and the three portfolio construction methods. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data sources, sample construction, feature definitions, and the HMM feature selection procedure. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the out-of-sample results, including performance comparisons, regime-conditional analysis, the IC rotation evidence, SHAP feature importance, ablation studies, a risk-aversion analysis, and an investigation of the economic mechanism behind the regime-dependent stock selection. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} summarises the findings, discusses limitations, and suggests directions for future research.
