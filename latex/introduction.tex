\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

This thesis sits in quantitative asset pricing and applied machine learning. The setting is the U.S. equity cross-section from January 1990 to November 2025, with international replication in the United Kingdom and Japan. The central object of study is the cross-sectional momentum anomaly, the tendency for recent stock-market winners to continue outperforming and losers to continue underperforming \citep{JegadeeshTitman1993}.

Cross-sectional momentum is one of the most robust anomalies in empirical finance and one of the most fragile. Momentum portfolios suffered losses exceeding 50\% during the 2009 market rebound \citep{DanielMoskowitz2016}, and similar crashes have recurred around regime transitions. The fragility motivates a substantial literature on momentum-crash management, but existing responses share a structural assumption. The underlying selection rule (winners minus losers, ranked) is treated as unconditional, and only the rule's exposure or weighting is adjusted around regime transitions. \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} and \citet{BarrosoSantaClara2015} scale gross exposure during stress; \citet{Cooper2004} treat regimes as a binary up-or-down variable; \citet{GHM2023} blend fast and slow horizon weights. The possibility that the selection rule itself, not just its scaling, may need to change has not been systematically tested.

This thesis tests that possibility. The approach is a two-stage framework. Stage one classifies the current market regime via a Bayesian Hidden Markov Model estimated on four real-time macrofinancial stress indicators (drawdown, return dispersion, relative market participation, and the BAA-AAA credit spread), producing a filtered probability of market panic that updates monthly. Stage two combines this regime signal with twelve momentum horizons in a cross-sectional stock-selection model, with several specifications evaluated in increasing order of complexity (a deterministic formula, two linear specifications, and an XGBoost ensemble). All specifications are evaluated out of sample on 2011 to 2025 in long-short construction.

The central claim is that the regime signal is not a direct return predictor but a \emph{context variable} that conditions how the model jointly uses the full momentum term structure in a given market state. No prior study has documented how the full term structure of cross-sectional momentum predictability reorganises across regimes, or that exploiting this reorganisation requires nonlinear modelling.

\paragraph{Research questions.} The analysis answers three questions. (i)~Does the interaction between the regime signal and cross-sectional momentum require nonlinear modelling, and if so, why do linear models on identical features fail? (ii)~How does the cross-sectional structure of momentum change between calm and panic regimes? (iii)~How robust is the regime-momentum interaction to alternative HMM feature sets, investor risk preferences, prolonged market stress, and replication in other equity markets?

\paragraph{Headline findings.} XGBoost achieves an out-of-sample Sharpe of $1.11$ with a six-factor alpha of $24.1\%$ ($t = 4.81$), while every benchmark on the same features either collapses or stays near zero. Removing the regime signal cuts XGBoost's Sharpe by more than $60\%$, establishing that the signal is essential. Cluster analysis of the model's picks recovers four recurring selection modes that trace a single market cycle, in which calm-period winner selection gives way to a hedged transition mode and then to two distinct panic-side reversal modes during recovery and active crisis. International replication produces Sharpes of $0.84$ in the United Kingdom and $0.50$ in Japan with locally fitted HMMs.

\paragraph{Contributions.} The thesis makes three contributions to the momentum literature. First, it identifies two reversal modes in the panic regime that have no direct analogue in the classical reversal literature, namely a dispersed-recovery state where the long leg sits below the cross-section average while the cross-section itself is up, and a term-structure-wide reversal at the panic peak. Second, the regime signal's marginal value scales with cross-sectional ambiguity rather than acting as a constant feature, in contrast to single-coefficient regime treatments. Third, the model selects by momentum level rather than rank, which reframes momentum's crash management as a re-ranking decision rather than an exposure-scaling one.

\paragraph{Outline.} The remainder of the thesis is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:literature} reviews the relevant literature on momentum's horizon structure, momentum crashes and exposure-based risk management, regime models in asset pricing, and machine learning in cross-sectional finance. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} develops the two-stage framework, including the Bayesian HMM specification and Gibbs sampling estimation, and the four cross-sectional stock-selection methods. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the U.S. equity sample, the three-pass HMM feature selection pipeline, and the cross-sectional features. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the out-of-sample results, the cross-sectional z-score and cluster analysis that documents the regime-momentum mechanism, robustness and risk-aversion analyses, and international replication. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} summarises the contributions, discusses limitations, and suggests directions for future research.
