\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Cross-sectional momentum, the tendency for recent stock market winners to continue outperforming and losers to continue underperforming, is one of the most robust and widely documented anomalies in empirical finance. Since the seminal work of \citet{JegadeeshTitman1993}, momentum strategies have been shown to generate significant risk-adjusted returns across markets, asset classes, and time periods.

Yet momentum is also one of the most fragile anomalies: it is subject to severe and sudden crashes, most notably during market stress episodes such as the 2009 rebound from the global financial crisis, when momentum portfolios suffered losses exceeding 50\% in a matter of months \citep{DanielMoskowitz2016}. This tension between the strategy's long-run profitability and its vulnerability to regime shifts motivates the central question of this thesis: can a systematic, real-time regime classification improve cross-sectional momentum investing?

The existing literature offers two broad responses to momentum's fragility. The first, exemplified by \citet{BarrosoSantaClara2015}, scales momentum exposure inversely with recent volatility, treating risk management as a post-construction overlay. The second, pursued by \citet{GHM2023}, conditions the momentum signal itself on the market state, blending short-term and long-term lookback horizons according to a classification of market cycles derived from trailing market returns. Both approaches improve upon unconditional momentum, but both rely on backward-looking indicators (recent portfolio volatility or past market returns) to infer the current market regime.

This thesis proposes an alternative: using a Bayesian Hidden Markov Model (HMM) estimated on forward-looking equity market stress indicators to produce a real-time regime signal, and then feeding this signal into machine learning models that learn how to adapt cross-sectional stock selection to the current regime. The specific combination -- a Bayesian HMM on real-time equity stress indicators, fed into cross-sectional ML models alongside 12 momentum horizons, and interpreted via SHAP as a context variable conditioning momentum's term structure -- does not appear in the existing literature. Each component is well established; the contribution lies in their integration and in the economic interpretation that emerges from comparing linear and nonlinear models' use of the regime signal.

The approach differs from prior work in three respects. First, the regime classification is based on real-time stress indicators (market drawdown, realised volatility, cross-sectional return dispersion, and relative market participation) rather than trailing returns, providing earlier detection of regime shifts. Second, the regime signal enters the stock selection models as one feature among many, allowing the models to learn its optimal use rather than imposing a fixed rule for how regime information should affect portfolio construction. Third, by comparing a linear model (logistic regression) with a nonlinear model (XGBoost), the framework provides evidence for the thesis's central claim: the regime signal is not a direct return predictor; it is a context variable that governs which momentum horizons are informative.

\subsection{Research Questions}\label{sec:research_questions}

This thesis addresses three research questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Do regime-conditioned cross-sectional momentum strategies outperform unconditional momentum and the market-state approach of \citet{GHM2023} on a risk-adjusted basis?
    \item What drives the performance improvement: the regime signal, the inclusion of fundamental stock characteristics, or the use of nonlinear models?
    \item How does the regime signal interact with momentum at different lookback horizons, and what does this interaction reveal about the structure of the momentum anomaly?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Preview of Main Findings}\label{sec:preview}

The empirical analysis, evaluated out-of-sample on 167 months of U.S. equity data (2011--2025), yields three main findings.

First, the comparison between logistic regression (M1) and XGBoost (M2) provides evidence for the thesis's central economic insight. The logistic regression assigns a near-zero coefficient to the regime signal and achieves its Sharpe ratio improvement (1.03) entirely through fundamental quality screening. XGBoost, by contrast, uses the regime signal as its second most important feature and generates substantially higher absolute returns (21.6\% vs. 14.8\% annualised) by switching which momentum horizons it relies on depending on the market state. The regime signal is not a direct return predictor; it is a context variable that governs which momentum horizons are informative. This divergence between a linear and a nonlinear model's use of the same input is consistent with the interpretation that cross-sectional momentum is not a single stable anomaly but a family of horizon-specific effects whose composition rotates with market conditions.

Second, fundamental quality screening -- selecting stocks that are profitable, reasonably valued, and not overleveraged -- is the primary driver of risk-adjusted improvement. The regime signal contributes by changing the return profile (higher returns, higher volatility) rather than the Sharpe ratio, a distinction that a risk-aversion analysis confirms: the regime signal's importance drops from nearly 30\% under return maximisation to approximately 6\% under high risk aversion.

Third, the HMM regime classification, based on real-time equity stress indicators rather than trailing market returns, produces a more informative signal than the backward-looking market-state approach of \citet{GHM2023}. Market drawdown (DD) is the single most important HMM input, consistent with the findings of \citet{DanielMoskowitz2016} on the role of past market declines in momentum dynamics.

\subsection{Outline}\label{sec:outline}

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:literature} reviews the relevant literature on the momentum anomaly, regime-switching models in finance, the market-state momentum approach of \citet{GHM2023} (which conditions momentum on trailing market returns), and machine learning approaches to cross-sectional asset pricing. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data sources, sample construction, and feature definitions. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} develops the two-stage empirical framework: the Bayesian HMM for regime classification and the three portfolio construction methods. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the out-of-sample results, including performance comparisons, a decomposition of the sources of improvement with supporting feature importance and ablation analysis, and a risk-aversion analysis that examines how the regime signal's value varies with investor risk preferences. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} summarises the findings, discusses limitations, and suggests directions for future research.
