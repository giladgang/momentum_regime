\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

This chapter evaluates the drawdown-conditioned momentum strategy out-of-sample on the applied configuration: a long-only top-decile portfolio, value-weighted, drawn from the point-in-time top-1000 universe, rebalanced on an expanding yearly walk over 2011--2025, and measured against the value-weighted top-1000 benchmark. Section~\ref{sec:performance_results} reports headline performance and shows that the strategy earns no unconditional risk-adjusted alpha but concentrates its entire edge in panic regimes. Section~\ref{sec:regime_results} decomposes the model's per-month selections and locates that edge in a deep-crisis loser-selection mode. Section~\ref{sec:risk_aversion} stress-tests the result across episodes, a risk-aversion sweep, a pre-2011 historical backtest, and an international replication. Section~\ref{sec:cost} shows that the regime signal's practical value is a transaction-cost one: it relocates trading to the months where it is worth paying for.


\subsection{Portfolio Performance}\label{sec:performance_results}

\subsubsection{Results Overview}

\input{tables/table_performance}

Table~\ref{tab:performance} reports gross out-of-sample performance for the main model, the drawdown-conditioned XGBoost regressor, alongside its no-signal ablation, an alternative feature-selection variant, unconditional 12-month momentum, and the value-weighted top-1000 benchmark. The honest headline is stated first: on a large-cap, long-only book the strategy does \emph{not} deliver risk-adjusted outperformance. Its Sharpe ratio of $0.78$ is below the benchmark's $0.99$, and although it compounds \$1 into \$8.7 against the benchmark's \$7.2 over the window, it does so at higher volatility ($21.6\%$ vs $14.4\%$) and higher market beta ($1.28$). The excess return is a beta-and-tilt effect, not alpha: the six-factor alpha is an insignificant $+1.6\%$ per year ($t = 0.34$; Appendix~\ref{app:factor_alphas}), and even the CAPM alpha ($+2.8\%$, $t = 0.55$) does not clear conventional thresholds.\footnote{The factor alpha is the intercept of a regression of monthly excess returns on the market, SMB, HML, RMW, CMA, and UMD factors; full specification in Appendix~\ref{app:factor_alphas}.} This is a sharp departure from the long-short, full-universe setting, where the same architecture earns a large and significant alpha: stripping the short leg, restricting to large capitalizations, and measuring against the index the book is actually held against removes the headline result.

What survives is a timing result. The information ratio against the benchmark is $0.21$ for the main model, the highest of the conditional variants, and positive where dropping the regime signal ($0.12$), feeding the raw stress indicators to the tree ($0.12$), or selecting the yearly best-scoring feature combination ($-0.03$) are weaker. The regime signal adds value, but that value is an active-return, benchmark-relative quantity, not a standalone premium.

The regime-conditional view (Table~\ref{tab:regime_sharpe}) shows where it comes from. In calm months the strategy is, if anything, worse than holding the index: its calm Sharpe is $0.42$ against the benchmark's $0.87$, and its mean active return in calm months is $-0.28\%$ per month. In panic months ($\pi_t^{\text{filter}} \geq 0.5$) the picture inverts: the strategy earns a panic Sharpe of $1.35$ against the benchmark's $1.25$, with a mean active return of $+1.35\%$ per month. Every edge the strategy has is earned in the $54$ panic months of the $179$-month window; the $125$ calm months are, on average, a drag. The practitioner reading is direct: be the index in calm, tilt in panic.

\input{tables/table_regime_sharpe}

The panic edge is economically large but statistically underpowered, and this qualification must travel with it. Estimated as a factor alpha on the panic subsample alone, the main model earns a six-factor alpha of roughly $+16\%$ annualized, but on $53$ panic months the standard error is wide ($t = 1.15$), so it does not clear significance; the calm-subsample alpha is $-3.0\%$ ($t = -0.63$). Moreover the benchmark itself carries recovery alpha in panic months (a three-factor alpha of $+9.9\%$, $t = 2.08$), so the strategy \emph{amplifies} a panic-month effect that the static factors do not span rather than cleanly separating a new one. The defensible claim is therefore regime-conditional active return and cost-robustness (Section~\ref{sec:cost}), not significant risk-adjusted alpha.

\subsubsection{Regime Signal Ablation}

The regime signal is what does the work, and the HMM stage is what makes it usable (Table~\ref{tab:regime_signal_ablation}). Dropping $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ and retraining on the twelve momentum features alone lowers the information ratio from $0.21$ to $0.12$. Feeding the four raw stress indicators to the tree directly, rather than the HMM-distilled probability, does no better ($0.12$): with the extra dimensions the model must reconstruct the regime structure at each split, whereas the HMM solves regime identification upstream and hands the cross-sectional model a single conditioning variable one split can act on. The GHM turning-point rule, evaluated at a fair seed budget, also trails ($0.10$), and adding firm-level fundamentals degrades the strategy ($-0.13$; Section~\ref{sec:fund_ablation}). The drawdown-only HMM signal is thus not a convenient feature but the operative one, and its parsimony (Section~\ref{sec:feature_selection}) is a feature rather than a limitation.

\subsection{The Regime-Momentum Mechanism}\label{sec:regime_results}

This section reads the structure of the regime-momentum signal off the model's per-month selections: first the aggregate contribution by feature (Section~\ref{sec:horizon_evidence}), then the recurring selection modes recovered by clustering the long-leg z-curves (Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity_picks}), and finally the interpretation (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsubsection{Momentum Contribution by Horizon}\label{sec:horizon_evidence}

The model's predictions are decomposed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values \citep{Lundberg2017}, which attribute the prediction across inputs (Appendix~\ref{app:shap}). A single feature, $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$, carries $60\%$ of total SHAP, with the twelve momentum horizons together accounting for the other $40\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:shap}). Equal weighting would give $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ just $1/13 \approx 8\%$, so the model loads on the regime signal roughly seven times its share of the feature set, consistent with a routing-variable role: the regime probability governs how the momentum term structure is used. Between regimes, the regime signal's share \emph{rises} from $57\%$ in calm to $65\%$ in panic, indicating that the model leans hardest on the regime call precisely when the market is in stress.

\subsubsection{Cross-Sectional Z-Scores}\label{sec:heterogeneity_picks}

To trace the picks themselves, we examine the cross-sectional z-scores of the stocks the model selects for the long leg. For each selected stock $s$ the z-score at horizon $k$ is $(mom_k^s - \bar{mom}_k)/\sigma_k$, where $\bar{mom}_k$ and $\sigma_k$ are the cross-sectional mean and standard deviation of horizon-$k$ trailing momentum across the universe that month. The long-leg z-curve for a month is the average of these per-stock z-scores, a 12-element profile locating the model's picks relative to the cross-sectional mean at each lookback. The sign indicates which side of the mean the basket sits on; the magnitude, how far, in standard-deviation units.

Clustering the $179$ monthly z-curves with $K$-means ($L_2$, $K=4$) on the applied strategy's own picks recovers four recurring selection modes (Table~\ref{tab:cluster_k4_descriptors}).\footnote{$K=4$ is retained for comparability with the mechanism decomposition; the clustering is run on the applied long-leg z-curves rather than transplanted from the thesis-window model.} The four modes span a single market cycle. Clusters~1 and~2 sit above the cross-sectional mean (average z-scores $+1.23$ and $+0.29$) at low regime probability: classical winner-continuation, strongly tilted in the calm-continuation mode (cluster~1) and hedged in the transition mode (cluster~2). Clusters~3 and~4 sit below the mean ($-0.33$ and $-1.01$) at elevated regime probability: the long leg is rebuilt from cross-sectional losers, mildly in the post-panic recovery mode (cluster~3) and emphatically in the deep-crisis mode (cluster~4, mean $\pi = 0.70$).

\input{tables/table_cluster_k4_descriptors}

The performance across modes is the central result of the decomposition. The deep-crisis mode (cluster~4) earns the highest cluster Sharpe, $0.92$, and by far the highest information ratio, $+0.73$: when the model commits to deep-loser selection at the panic peak, the bet pays. The calm-continuation mode (cluster~1) earns a Sharpe of $0.76$ and a positive IR ($+0.33$), the transition mode (cluster~2) is essentially benchmark-like ($0.84$, IR $+0.03$), and the post-panic recovery mode (cluster~3) is the weakest, with a Sharpe of $0.66$ and a \emph{negative} information ratio ($-0.30$). The edge is thus concentrated in the deepest-stress months and in confident calm continuation; the ambiguous transition and the late-recovery phases add little or detract. No single-horizon rank-based momentum strategy would select the cluster~3 and~4 baskets for the long side at all, since they sit below the cross-sectional mean at every horizon.

\subsubsection{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The mechanism is therefore ``momentum where momentum works, reversal where momentum crashes'': above-average winner selection in calm (clusters~1--2) and below-average loser selection in panic (clusters~3--4), with the strongest active return coming from the deep-crisis loser bet. Because the model ranks on predicted forward return rather than on a per-horizon momentum score, the long leg can hold stocks not top-ranked at any individual horizon, which is exactly what implements the regime-conditioned direction flip. This is the same qualitative mechanism documented in the long-short thesis setting; what changes in the large-cap long-only setting is the \emph{magnitude}: with the short leg gone and the universe restricted to liquid large caps, the reversal bet still works but its Sharpe is much smaller, and the strategy no longer beats a passive index on a risk-adjusted basis.

The economic reading connects to the momentum-crash literature. In panic, past losers among large caps are disproportionately high-beta names that fell hardest; conditioning on $\pi_t^{\text{filter}}$ places them in the long leg, so the same rebound that crashes unconditional momentum \citep{DanielMoskowitz2016} lifts the strategy instead. The premise is that panic-period declines are temporary; where it fails, in a prolonged deterioration, the reversal bet bleeds, which the historical backtest in Section~\ref{sec:historical_stress} examines directly.


\subsection{Robustness and Limitations}\label{sec:risk_aversion}

\subsubsection{Episode Evidence}\label{sec:episodes}

Three out-of-sample stress episodes fall inside the window (formation-month cumulative returns). Through the COVID crash and recovery the main model returned $+62.4\%$ against the benchmark's $+22.8\%$; through the April 2025 tariff episode, $+28.2\%$ against $+14.2\%$; through the 2022 tightening bear market, $+0.5\%$ against the benchmark's $-8.6\%$. In each the strategy outperformed the index, and in each the outperformance came from holding beaten-down large caps into the rebound rather than liquidating them.

\subsubsection{Risk-Return Trade-off}\label{sec:risk_aversion_control}

The strategy's volatility ($21.6\%$) is high relative to the benchmark's ($14.4\%$) and a risk-averse investor may prefer to scale it down. A risk-aversion parameter $\gamma$ is incorporated through a per-direction log-utility that penalizes forecast volatility (Appendix~\ref{app:crra_construction}), and the out-of-sample trade-off is traced across $\gamma \in [0, 1]$.

\subsection{Transaction Costs and the When-to-Trade Rule}\label{sec:cost}

The results above are gross. Because the strategy's entire edge is concentrated in panic months, its economic case is a transaction-cost one: the regime signal identifies the minority of months in which trading is worth paying for, and converts a cost-fragile strategy into a cost-robust one by relocating turnover to where the payoff is.
